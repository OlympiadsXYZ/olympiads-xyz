### 1. (I07 - T13 - A)

A crater on the surface of the Moon has a diameter of 80 km. Is it possible to resolve this crater with naked eyes, assuming the eye pupil aperture is 5 mm?

Source: [native 37, printed 23](/collections/ioaa-problems-by-topic/source/711ac8d837c5f8d9a39467d3a734940e193d151edef342c2c232a543d4b48062.pdf#page=37).




<p>Условие: <a href="/collections/ioaa-problems-by-topic/source/711ac8d837c5f8d9a39467d3a734940e193d151edef342c2c232a543d4b48062.pdf#page=37">PDF 37</a></p>

<details id="c4-t1-key">

<summary>Печатно решение / форма</summary>



### 1.

Let us say that diametrically opposite points on the said crater subtend an angle $\phi$ at our eye. If the minimum separation between these two points to make them resolvable is $x$, then,

$$
\phi\ge1.22\frac{\lambda}{D_{eye}}\tag{14.1}
$$

$$
\phi=\frac{x}{\text{distance from Earth to Moon}}\tag{14.2}
$$

$$
\therefore x=\frac{1.22\times5\times10^{-7}\times3.78\times10^8}{5\times10^{-3}}
$$

$$
\approx46\,\mathrm{km}\tag{14.3}
$$


As this is smaller than 80 km, the said crater can be resolved. Alternatively,

$$
\phi\ge1.22\frac{\lambda}{D_{eye}}
$$

$$
\mathrm{L.H.S.}=\phi=\frac{80\mathrm{km}}{\text{distance from Earth to Moon}}
$$

$$
=\frac{80\times10^3\mathrm m}{3.78\times10^8\mathrm m}=2.11\times10^{\text{â}4}\mathrm{radian}\tag{14.4}
$$

$$
\mathrm{R.H.S.}=1.22\frac{\lambda}{D_{eye}}
$$

$$
=\frac{1.22\times5\times10^{-7}}{5\times10^{-3}}=1.22\times10^{-4}\mathrm{radian}\tag{14.5}
$$


Thus, the ineuqlity is true and it is possible to resolve the 80 km-diameter crater with naked eye. Note the particular choice of wavelength which makes the calculations easier.

### 2. (I11 - T05 - A)

What would be the diameter of a radio telescope, working at a wavelength of $\lambda=1\,\mathrm{cm}$, with the same resolution as an optical telescope of diameter $D=10\,\mathrm{cm}$?

Source: [native 37, printed 23](/collections/ioaa-problems-by-topic/source/711ac8d837c5f8d9a39467d3a734940e193d151edef342c2c232a543d4b48062.pdf#page=37).




<p>Условие: <a href="/collections/ioaa-problems-by-topic/source/711ac8d837c5f8d9a39467d3a734940e193d151edef342c2c232a543d4b48062.pdf#page=37">PDF 37</a></p>

<details id="c4-t2-key">

<summary>Печатно решение / форма</summary>



### 2.

Since resolution of both the telescopes is same,

$$
1.22\frac{\lambda_1}{D_1}=1.22\frac{\lambda_2}{D_2}
$$

$$
D_2=\frac{D_1\lambda_2}{\lambda_1}\tag{14.6}
$$

$$
=\frac{0.1\times0.01}{5\times10^{-7}}
$$

$$
=2\mathrm{km}\tag{14.7}
$$


Answers may vary slightly depending on your choice of optical wavelength. For this reason, the final answer is rounded to single significant digit.

### 3. (I10 - T08 - B)

The Galactic Center is believed to contain a super-massive black hole with a mass $M=4\times10^6M_\odot$. The astronomy community is trying to resolve its event horizon, which is a challenging task. For a non-rotating black hole, this is the Schwarzschild radius, $R_\odot=3(M/M_\odot)$ km. Assume that we have an Earth-sized telescope (using Very Long Baseline Interferometry). What wavelengths should we adopt in order to resolve the event horizon of the black hole?

Source: [native 37, printed 23](/collections/ioaa-problems-by-topic/source/711ac8d837c5f8d9a39467d3a734940e193d151edef342c2c232a543d4b48062.pdf#page=37).




<p>Условие: <a href="/collections/ioaa-problems-by-topic/source/711ac8d837c5f8d9a39467d3a734940e193d151edef342c2c232a543d4b48062.pdf#page=37">PDF 37</a></p>

<details id="c4-t3-key">

<summary>Печатно решение / форма</summary>



### 3.

One should use angular size of the galactic black hole to find resolution

$$
\theta_{BH}=\frac{2R_{BH}}{d_{GC}}\tag{14.8}
$$

$$
\theta_{tel}=\frac{1.22\lambda}{2R_\oplus}\tag{14.9}
$$

$$
\theta_{BH}\ge\theta_{tel}\tag{14.10}
$$

$$
\frac{2R}{D}\ge\frac{1.22\lambda}{2R_\oplus}
$$

$$
\lambda\le\frac{4R_{BH}R_\oplus}{1.22D}\tag{14.11}
$$

$$
\le\frac{12M_{BH}R_\oplus}{1.22DM_\odot}
$$

$$
\le\frac{12\times4\times10^9\times6.371\times10^6}{1.22\times8.5\times10^3\times3.086\times10^{16}}
$$

$$
\lambda\le0.96\mathrm{mm}\tag{14.12}
$$

$$
\lambda\le0.9\mathrm{mm}\tag{14.13}
$$


This means that we need to observe at least at near sub-mm frequencies, which is in radio or far-infrared band. We have rounded limit on $\lambda$ to 0.9 mm instead of 1.0 mm as the answer is in terms an inequality. Thus, we have to respect direction of inequality.

### 4. (I12 - T12 - B)

What is the minimum diameter of a telescope, observing in the visible and near ultraviolet bands and located at one of the Lagrangian points $L_4$ or $L_5$s of the Sun-Earth system, in order to be able to detect the Earth’s wobbling relative to the ecliptic plane caused by the gravitational action of the Moon?

Source: [native 37, printed 23](/collections/ioaa-problems-by-topic/source/711ac8d837c5f8d9a39467d3a734940e193d151edef342c2c232a543d4b48062.pdf#page=37).




<p>Условие: <a href="/collections/ioaa-problems-by-topic/source/711ac8d837c5f8d9a39467d3a734940e193d151edef342c2c232a543d4b48062.pdf#page=37">PDF 37</a></p>

<details id="c4-t4-key">

<summary>Печатно решение / форма</summary>



### 4.

Let us assume the moon is at the maximum possible separation from the ecliptic plane. We know that the displacements of the Earth and moon are inversely proportional to their masses. Let us call distance between the Earth and the moon as $d$, semi-amplitude of the Earth’s oscillation as $x$ and distance between the centre of the Earth and barycentre of the Earth-Moon system as $y$.

$$
\frac{x}{y}=\tan5.14^\circ\tag{14.14}
$$

$$
M_\oplus y=M_m(d-y)\tag{14.15}
$$

$$
\therefore y=\frac{M_md}{M_\oplus+M_m}\tag{14.16}
$$

$$
x=\frac{M_md}{M_\oplus+M_m}\tan5.14^\circ\tag{14.17}
$$

$$
=\frac{7.4377\times10^{22}\times3.84\times10^8}{(597.36+7.4377)\times10^{22}}\times0.0900
$$

$$
=4.248\times10^5\mathrm m\tag{14.18}
$$

$$
\therefore2x=849.6\mathrm{km}\tag{14.19}
$$


Note that points $L_4$ or $L_5$ are at eaxtly 1 A. U. distance from the earth. Also note that the resolution of the telescope improves with decreasing wavelengths. As the telescope is capable of observing in near UV, we will use a reasonable wavelength in near UV range to determine smallest possible diameter.

$$
\alpha=\frac{2x}{d_{L4}}=1.22\frac{\lambda}{D}\tag{14.20}
$$

$$
\therefore D=\frac{1.22d_{L4}\lambda}{2x}
$$

$$
=\frac{1.22\times1.496\times10^{11}\times350\times10^{-9}}{2\times4.248\times10^5}
$$

$$
D=7.5\mathrm{cm}\tag{14.21}
$$

### 5. (I08 - T10 - B)

The coordinates of the components of Visual Binary star $\mu$ Sco as on the August 28, 2008 are given in the table below.

| Star | $\alpha$ (RA) | $\delta$ (Dec) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\mu$ Sco (primary) | $20^h17^m38.90^s$ | $-12^\circ30'30''$ |

![Complete printed binary coordinate table](/collections/ioaa-problems-by-topic/assets/80087d58c9dbb30d9e20d0d78c3fd6d4ed3042d32a941267b8f2180d3e3ade9f.png)

The stars are observed using Zeiss refractor telescope at the Bosscha Observatory whose aperture and focal length are 600 mm and 10,780 mm respectively. The telescope is equipped with $765\times510$ pixels CCD camera. The pixel size of the chip is $9\,\mu\mathrm m\times9\,\mu\mathrm m$.

(a) Can both components of the binary be inside the single frame of the camera?

(b) What is the position angle of the secondary star, with respect to the North?

Source: [native 37, printed 23](/collections/ioaa-problems-by-topic/source/711ac8d837c5f8d9a39467d3a734940e193d151edef342c2c232a543d4b48062.pdf#page=37), [native 38, printed 24](/collections/ioaa-problems-by-topic/source/711ac8d837c5f8d9a39467d3a734940e193d151edef342c2c232a543d4b48062.pdf#page=38).




<p>Условие: <a href="/collections/ioaa-problems-by-topic/source/711ac8d837c5f8d9a39467d3a734940e193d151edef342c2c232a543d4b48062.pdf#page=37">PDF 37</a>, <a href="/collections/ioaa-problems-by-topic/source/711ac8d837c5f8d9a39467d3a734940e193d151edef342c2c232a543d4b48062.pdf#page=38">PDF 38</a></p>

<details id="c4-t5-key">

<summary>Печатно решение / форма</summary>



### 5.

(a) We note that longest separation one can fit on a CCD in a single frame would be along the diagonal of the CCD.

$$
S=\sqrt{765^2+510^2}\times9\,\mu\mathrm m\tag{14.22}
$$

$$
=919.4\times9\,\mu\mathrm m\approx8.27\mathrm{mm}\tag{14.23}
$$

$$
f=10780\mathrm{mm}\tag{14.24}
$$

$$
\theta=\frac{S}{f}=\frac{206265\times8.27''}{10780}\tag{14.25}
$$

$$
=158.3''=2.64'\tag{14.26}
$$


As the two stars are very close to each other, one can approximate the situation to flat geometry. Hence, angular separation between them is,

$$
\gamma=\sqrt{\triangle\alpha^2+\triangle\delta^2}\tag{14.27}
$$

$$
\because\triangle\alpha=20^h18^m03^s.3-20^h17^m38^s.9=0^m24^s.4\tag{14.28}
$$

$$
\because\triangle\delta=-12^\circ32'41''+12^\circ30'30''=-2'11''\tag{14.29}
$$

$$
\therefore\gamma=\sqrt{((0^m24^s.4)\times15)^2+(-2'11'')^2}\tag{14.30}
$$

$$
=\sqrt{(366'')^2+(131'')^2}\tag{14.31}
$$

$$
\approx389''\tag{14.32}
$$


This is far larger than $158.3''$. Thus, the stars will not fit on a single frame.

One can also make an even simpler argument of simply noting the separation in R.A. ($24^s.40$) itself is bigger than the CCD diagonal.

It should be noted that the plane trigonometry works only for the stars which are reasonably close to each other. For other cases, spherical trigonometry must be used.

(b) To find position angle, we note that the secondary star is towards the south-west of primary star. As position angle ($\phi$) is measured from the north in anti-clockwise fashion, we expect the value to be between $90^\circ$ and $180^\circ$.

$$
\phi=\tan^{-1}\frac{\triangle\alpha}{\triangle\delta}\tag{14.33}
$$

$$
=\tan^{-1}\frac{366}{131}=\tan^{-1}2.794\tag{14.34}
$$

$$
\approx109^\circ42'\tag{14.35}
$$

<p>Условие: <a href="/collections/ioaa-problems-by-topic/source/711ac8d837c5f8d9a39467d3a734940e193d151edef342c2c232a543d4b48062.pdf#page=38">PDF 38</a></p>

<details id="c4-t6-key">

### 6.

We first find image scale of the picture in arcseconds per mm.

$$
S=\frac{206265}{f}=\frac{206265}{D/F}\tag{14.36}
$$

$$
=\frac{206265F}{D}=\frac{206265}{1500}\tag{14.37}
$$

$$
=137.51''/\mathrm{mm}\tag{14.38}
$$


Thus, we can get angular diameter and then by dividing physical diameter by the angular diameter, distance can be obtained.

$$
\theta_\odot=137.51\times13.817\tag{14.39}
$$

$$
=1900.0''=0^\circ31'40''\tag{14.40}
$$

$$
d_\odot=\frac{2R_\odot}{\theta_\odot}=\frac{2\times6.955\times10^8\times180}{(0^\circ31'40'')\times\pi}\tag{14.41}
$$

$$
=1.510\times10^{11}\mathrm m\tag{14.42}
$$

$$
\theta_{moon}=137.51\times13.235\tag{14.43}
$$

$$
=1820.0''=0^\circ30'20''\tag{14.44}
$$

$$
d_{moon}=\frac{2R_{moon}}{\theta_{moon}}=\frac{2\times1.7374\times10^6\times180}{(0^\circ30'20'')\times\pi}\tag{14.45}
$$

$$
=3.772\times10^8\mathrm m\tag{14.46}
$$


The percentage of the solar disk covered by the Moon is

$$
\left(\frac{13.235}{13.817}\right)^2\times100\%=91.75\%\tag{14.47}
$$

### 7. (I08 - T02 - C)

On April 2, 2008 a telescope (10 cm diameter, $f/10$) at the Bosscha Observatory was used to observe the Sun and found an active region 0987 (based on the NOAA number) at $8^\circ$ South and $40^\circ$ West from the center of the solar disk. The region was recorded with a SBIG ST-8 CCD camera ($1600\times1200$ pixels, $(9\,\mu\mathrm m\times9\,\mu\mathrm m)/\mathrm{pixel}$) and size of the spot was $5\times4$ pixels. According to the Astronomical Almanac, the solar diameter on that day is $32'$. How large is the corrected area of the active region in unit of millionth of solar hemisphere (msh)?

Source: [native 38, printed 24](/collections/ioaa-problems-by-topic/source/711ac8d837c5f8d9a39467d3a734940e193d151edef342c2c232a543d4b48062.pdf#page=38), [native 39, printed 25](/collections/ioaa-problems-by-topic/source/711ac8d837c5f8d9a39467d3a734940e193d151edef342c2c232a543d4b48062.pdf#page=39).




<p>Условие: <a href="/collections/ioaa-problems-by-topic/source/711ac8d837c5f8d9a39467d3a734940e193d151edef342c2c232a543d4b48062.pdf#page=38">PDF 38</a>, <a href="/collections/ioaa-problems-by-topic/source/711ac8d837c5f8d9a39467d3a734940e193d151edef342c2c232a543d4b48062.pdf#page=39">PDF 39</a></p>

<details id="c4-t7-key">

<summary>Печатно решение / форма</summary>



### 7.

Focal ratio of telescope is ratio of its aperture and focal length. Thus, $f=10\times0.1=1\mathrm m$. Next we find angular distance (on solar disk) of the active region form the centre of the disk.

$$
\alpha=\cos^{-1}(\cos40^\circ\cos8^\circ)\quad\text{using cosine rule}\tag{14.48}
$$

$$
=40^\circ40''\tag{14.49}
$$


The tangential plane of active region is inclined at $40^\circ40''$ to normal plane. Let $\theta_{1,2}$ be the Angular Diameters of active region seen from the Earth and let $S$ be its size seen on CCD. Let $d_{1,2}$ be the actual size and $A$ denote area of the active region.

$$
S=(5\times4)\times9\,\mu\mathrm m\tag{14.50}
$$

$$
S=45\,\mu\mathrm m\times36\,\mu\tag{14.51}
$$

$$
S=f\theta\tag{14.52}
$$

$$
\theta=\frac{45\,\mu\mathrm m\times36\,\mu\mathrm m}{1\mathrm m}\times206265''\tag{14.53}
$$

$$
=9.3''\times7.4''\tag{14.54}
$$

$$
\theta_{1,2}=\frac{d_{1,2}\cos\alpha}{d_\odot}\tag{14.55}
$$

$$
\text{Also,}\quad\frac{32'\times60}{206265}=\frac{2R_\odot}{d_\odot}\tag{14.56}
$$

$$
d_{1,2}=\frac{\theta_1\times\theta_2\times2R_\odot\times206265}{32\times60\times\cos\alpha}\tag{14.57}
$$

$$
=\frac{(9.3\times7.4)}{960\times0.7585}R_\odot\tag{14.58}
$$

$$
=(0.0128R_\odot\times0.0102R_\odot\tag{14.59}
$$

$$
A=(0.0128\times0.0102)R_\odot^2\tag{14.60}
$$

$$
\text{Area of Hemisphere}=2\pi R_\odot^2\tag{14.61}
$$

$$
\frac{A}{A_{hemi}}=0.00001446\approx14.46\mathrm{\ msh}\tag{14.62}
$$

### 2.

This problem closely simulates the process of converting CCD reading to magnitudes of stars. At the start, we notice, that there are five regions with high pixel count. These are clearly the stars. Rest of the field is empty sky. Problem explicitly states that we should approximate the stars as squares. We can see that differnt stars have different sizes. Biggest of these (star 3) can be properly included in a $5\times5$ pixel square.

We have to realise, that even dark sky is giving us some non-zero pixel count. So the total pixel count of the square containing the stars also includes this contribution from overall sky brightness. To get flux of the star, we have to subtract total pixel count from the equivalent area of the dark sky. As the pixel count in dark sky appears random, we should measure pixcel counts from squares over dark sky at more than one places and then take average. Now, if we take different square sizes for different stars, we will add our own work as we have to measure total count from dark sky separately for each square size. Thus, for the sake of convenience, it makes sense to use same size square for all the stars. One should be worried about dark sky pixels getting included in the square as those contributions will automatically get subtracted when we subtract average count of dark sky.

As mentioned above, we should take minimum size of the square as $5\times5$ pixels. We can even go for a bigger square, but it will unnecessarily increase our labour. Further, as we want the centre of the star to be at the centre of our square, the side of the square should be taken as a odd number. Thus, the choice is restricted to $5\times5$ pixels (correct size), $3\times3$ pixels (too small) or $7\times7$ pixels (too large).

(a) We take five random $5\times5$ pixel squares of dark sky. Each region should be relatively far from any of the stars in the field. We get counts as 121, 113, 115, 123 and 113. The average of these five counts is 117 (for 25 pixels.
