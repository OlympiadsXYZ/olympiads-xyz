### 9.1 Theory · 2

<h4 id="c9-exg-t02-question">Условие</h4>



The observed redshift of a QSO is $z=0.20$, estimate its distance. The Hubble constant is $72\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}$.




<p>Условие: <a href="/collections/ioaa-problems-by-topic/source/711ac8d837c5f8d9a39467d3a734940e193d151edef342c2c232a543d4b48062.pdf#page=77">PDF 77</a></p>

<details id="c9-exg-t02-key">

<summary>Печатно решение / форма</summary>



Let recession velocity of the QSO be $v$, Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sqrt{\frac{1+v/c}{1-v/c}}&=1+z\qquad\text{(19.5)}\\
\frac{1+v/c}{1-v/c}&=(1+z)^2\\
\therefore\frac{v}{c}&=\frac{(1+z)^2-1}{(1+z)^2+1}\qquad\text{(19.6)}\\
&=\frac{(1.2)^2-1}{(1.2)^2+1}\\
&=\frac{0.44}{2.44}\\
&=0.18\qquad\text{(19.7)}\\
v&=H_0D\qquad\text{(19.8)}\\
\therefore D&=\frac{v}{H_0}\qquad\text{(19.9)}\\
&=\frac{0.18\times3.00\times10^5}{68}\\
&\approx800\,\mathrm{Mpc}\qquad\text{(19.10)}
\end{aligned}
$$

### 9.1 Theory · 3

<p>I08 - T05 - B</p>



<h4 id="c9-exg-t03-question">Условие</h4>



The average temperature of the Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB) is currently $T=2.73\,\mathrm{K}$. It yields the origin of CMB to be at redshift $z_{CMB}=1100$. The current densities of the Dark Energy, Dark Matter, and Normal Matter components of the Universe as a whole are, $\rho_{DE}=7.1\times10^{-30}\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}$, $\rho_{DM}=2.4\times10^{-30}\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}$ and $\rho_{NM}=0.5\times10^{-30}\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}$, respectively. What is the ratio between the density of Dark Matter to the density of Dark Energy at the time CMB was emitted, if we assume that the dark energy is the energy of empty space?




<p>Условие: <a href="/collections/ioaa-problems-by-topic/source/711ac8d837c5f8d9a39467d3a734940e193d151edef342c2c232a543d4b48062.pdf#page=77">PDF 77</a></p>

<details id="c9-exg-t03-key">

<summary>Печатно решение / форма</summary>



Key to solving this question is the fact that since dark energy is the energy of empty space, its energy density remains constant during expansion of the universe. If $a_0$ is scale factor today and $a_z$ as the scale factor at the recombination epoch.

$$
\begin{aligned}
1+z&=\frac{a_0}{a_z}\qquad\text{(19.11)}\\
a_z&=\frac{1}{1+z}a_0=\frac{1}{1+1100}a_0\qquad\text{(19.12)}\\
&=0.00091a_0\qquad\text{(19.13)}
\end{aligned}
$$


Matter densities will change as per size of the universe.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\rho_{DM_z}&=\frac{\rho_{DM}}{a^3}\qquad\text{(19.14)}\\
&=\frac{2.4\times10^{-30}}{(9.1\times10^{-4})^3}=\frac{2.4\times10^{-30}}{7.5\times10^{-10}}\qquad\text{(19.15)}\\
&=3.2\times10^{-21}\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}\qquad\text{(19.16)}\\
\frac{\rho_{DM}}{\rho_{DE}}&=\frac{3.2\times10^{-21}}{7.1\times10^{-30}}\qquad\text{(19.17)}\\
&=4.5\times10^8\qquad\text{(19.18)}
\end{aligned}
$$

### 9.1 Theory · 4

<p>I08 - T15 - B</p>



<h4 id="c9-exg-t04-question">Условие</h4>



Galaxy NGC 2639 is morphologically identified as an Sa galaxy with measured maximum rotational velocity $v_{max}=324\,\mathrm{km/s}$. After corrections for any extinction, its apparent magnitude in B is $m_B=12.22$. It is customary to measure a radius $R_{25}$ (in units of kpc) at which the galaxy’s surface brightness falls to $25\,\mathrm{mag_B/arcsec^2}$.

Spiral galaxies tend to follow a typical relation:

$$
\log R_{25}=-0.249M_B-4.00\tag{9.1}
$$

where $M_B$ is the absolute magnitude in B.

Apply the B-band Tully-Fisher relation for Sa spirals,

$$
M_B=-9.95\log(v_{max})+3.15,\tag{9.2}
$$

where $v_{max}$ is in km/s, to calculate the mass of NGC 2639 out to $R_{25}$. If colour index of the sun is $(B_\odot-V_\odot)=0.64$, write the mass in units of solar mass $M_\odot$ and its luminosity B-band in unit of $L_\odot$.




<p>Условие: <a href="/collections/ioaa-problems-by-topic/source/711ac8d837c5f8d9a39467d3a734940e193d151edef342c2c232a543d4b48062.pdf#page=77">PDF 77</a>, <a href="/collections/ioaa-problems-by-topic/source/711ac8d837c5f8d9a39467d3a734940e193d151edef342c2c232a543d4b48062.pdf#page=78">PDF 78</a></p>

<details id="c9-exg-t04-key">

<summary>Печатно решение / форма</summary>



By Tully - Fischer Relation,

$$
\begin{aligned}
M_B&=-9.95\log v_{max}+3.15\qquad\text{(19.19)}\\
&=-9.95\log324+3.15\qquad\text{(19.20)}\\
M_B&=-21.83\qquad\text{(19.21)}\\
\log R_{25}&=-0.249M_B-4.00\qquad\text{(19.22)}\\
R_{25}&=27.27\,\mathrm{kpc}\qquad\text{(19.23)}\\
\mu_{25}&=\frac{R_{25}V^2}{G}\qquad\text{(19.24)}\\
&=\frac{27.27\times10^3\times3.0856\times10^{16}\times(3.24\times10^5)^2}{6.672\times10^{-11}}\qquad\text{(19.25)}\\
&=1.324\times10^{42}\,\mathrm{kg}\qquad\text{(19.26)}\\
\mu_{25}&=6.66\times10^{11}M_\odot\qquad\text{(19.27)}
\end{aligned}
$$


Sun’s absolute Magnitude in B-Band is

$$
\begin{aligned}
M_{B\odot}&=M_{V\odot}+[B_\odot-V_\odot]\qquad\text{(19.28)}\\
&=4.82+0.64\qquad\text{(19.29)}\\
&=5.46\qquad\text{(19.30)}\\
\text{and }M_B&=-21.83\qquad\text{(19.31)}\\
M_B-M_{B\odot}&=-2.5\log\left(\frac{L}{L_\odot}\right)\qquad\text{(19.32)}\\
&=-21.83-5.46=-27.29\qquad\text{(19.33)}\\
L&=10^{\frac{-27.29}{-2.5}}L_\odot\qquad\text{(19.34)}\\
L&=8.24\times10^{10}L_\odot\qquad\text{(19.35)}
\end{aligned}
$$

### 9.1 Theory · 5

<p>I08 - T12 - B</p>



<h4 id="c9-exg-t05-question">Условие</h4>



Consider a type Ia supernova in a distant galaxy which has a luminosity of $5.8\times10^9L_\odot$ at its maximum light. Suppose you observe this supernova using your telescope and find that its brightness is $1.6\times10^{-7}$ times the brightness of Vega. The redshift of its host galaxy is known to be $z=0.05$. Calculate the distance of this galaxy (in pc) and also the Hubble time.




<p>Условие: <a href="/collections/ioaa-problems-by-topic/source/711ac8d837c5f8d9a39467d3a734940e193d151edef342c2c232a543d4b48062.pdf#page=78">PDF 78</a></p>

<details id="c9-exg-t05-key">

<summary>Печатно решение / форма</summary>



Note that Vega is standard zero magnitude star for Johnson UBV photometry. We know magnitude of the Sun is -26.7 and we know luminosity of the Sun. Combining this information,

$$
\begin{aligned}
m_\odot-m_{vega}&=-2.5\log\left(\frac{F_\odot}{F_{vega}}\right)\qquad\text{(19.36)}\\
-26.7-0&=2.5\log\left(\frac{F_{vega}}{F_\odot}\right)\qquad\text{(19.37)}\\
F_{vega}&=10^{\frac{-26.7}{2.5}}F_\odot=10^{-10.68}F_\odot\qquad\text{(19.38)}\\
&=2.09\times10^{-11}F_\odot\qquad\text{(19.39)}\\
F_{SN}&=1.6\times10^{-7}F_{vega}\qquad\text{(19.40)}\\
&=1.6\times10^{-7}\times2.09\times10^{-11}F_\odot\qquad\text{(19.41)}\\
F_{SN}&=3.34\times10^{-18}F_\odot\qquad\text{(19.42)}\\
\frac{L_{SN}}{4\pi d_{SN}^2}&=3.34\times10^{-18}\frac{L_\odot}{4\pi d_\odot^2}\qquad\text{(19.43)}\\
d_{SN}^2&=\frac{L_{SN}}{3.34\times10^{-18}L_\odot}\times d_\odot^2\qquad\text{(19.44)}\\
d_{SN}&=\sqrt{\frac{5.8\times10^9}{3.34\times10^{-18}}}\times1.496\times10^{11}\qquad\text{(19.45)}\\
&=6.2\times10^{24}\,\mathrm{m}\qquad\text{(19.46)}\\
d_{SN}&\approx202\,\mathrm{Mpc}\qquad\text{(19.47)}
\end{aligned}
$$


If $t_H$ is the Hubble time, then by combining Hubble’s law and approximate relation between redshift and velocity (for low redshift),

$$
\begin{aligned}
t_H&=\frac{1}{H_0}=\frac{d_{SN}}{v_{SN}}=\frac{d_{SN}}{cz}\qquad\text{(19.48)}\\
&=\frac{202\,\mathrm{Mpc}}{(1\,\mathrm{lyr/yr})(0.05)}\qquad\text{(19.49)}\\
&=202\times10^6\times3.262\times20\text{ years}\qquad\text{(19.50)}\\
&=1.32\times10^{10}\text{ years}\qquad\text{(19.51)}\\
t_H&=13.2\,\mathrm{Gyrs}\qquad\text{(19.52)}
\end{aligned}
$$


You can note clever use of speed of light as one light year per year to get the answer directly in years. The Hubble time thus obtained is for a non-accelerating universe and based on observation of only one galaxy. Thus, we are not surprised by the fact that the answer is lower than actual age of the universe.

### 9.1 Theory · 6

<p>I09 - T11 - C</p>



<h4 id="c9-exg-t06-question">Условие</h4>



Assume the mass of neutrinos is $m_v=10^{-5}m_e$. Calculate the number density of neutrinos needed to compensate the dark matter of the universe. Assume the universe is flat and $25\%$ of its mass is dark matter.

Hint: Take the classical total energy equal to zero.




<p>Условие: <a href="/collections/ioaa-problems-by-topic/source/711ac8d837c5f8d9a39467d3a734940e193d151edef342c2c232a543d4b48062.pdf#page=78">PDF 78</a></p>

<details id="c9-exg-t06-key">

<summary>Печатно решение / форма</summary>



In a Flat Universe, total energy is zero. For a test particle at the edge of the universe, we can take $R$ to be radius of visible universe and $M$ as its total mass. Let $\rho_c$ be the critical density of the universe.

$$
\begin{aligned}
KE&=-PE\qquad\text{(19.53)}\\
\frac12v^2&=\frac{GM}{R}\qquad\text{(19.54)}\\
\frac12(H_0R)^2&=\frac{4\pi R^3\rho_cG}{3R}\qquad\text{(19.55)}\\
\therefore\rho_c&=\frac{3H_0^2}{8\pi G}\qquad\text{(19.56)}\\
&=\frac{3\times(67.80\times10^3)^2}{8\pi\times6.672\times10^{-11}\times(3.0856\times10^{22})^2}\\
\rho_c&=8.6378\times10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\qquad\text{(19.57)}\\
m_{dark}&=0.25m_{total}\\
\therefore\rho_{dark}&=0.25\rho_c\qquad\text{(19.58)}\\
n_\nu&=\frac{\rho_{dark}}{m_\nu}=\frac{0.25\rho_c}{10^{-5}m_e}\qquad\text{(19.59)}\\
&=\frac{0.25\times8.6378\times10^{-27}}{10^{-5}\times9.1\times10^{-31}}\\
&=2.37\times10^8\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\\
n_\nu&\approx2\times10^8\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\qquad\text{(19.60)}
\end{aligned}
$$


Note that the final accuracy is rounded to a single digit due to approximation in the mass of neutrino.

### 9.1 Theory · 7

<p>I12 - T10 - C</p>



<h4 id="c9-exg-t07-question">Условие</h4>



Assume that the universe currently is well described by a density parameter $\Omega_0=1$, there is no dark energy, and the current temperature of the universe is $2.73\,\mathrm{K}$. Knowing that the temperature of the universe is inversely proportional to its radius (the scale factor), compute how long, starting from the present time, it will take to the Universe to cool down by $0.1\,\mathrm{K}$.




<p>Условие: <a href="/collections/ioaa-problems-by-topic/source/711ac8d837c5f8d9a39467d3a734940e193d151edef342c2c232a543d4b48062.pdf#page=78">PDF 78</a></p>

<details id="c9-exg-t07-key">

<summary>Печатно решение / форма</summary>



If the assumed model of the universe is without dark energy, one cannot simply take present age of the universe as 13.7 Gyrs. One should go back to the definition of the Hubble constant and start from there. Let $T$, $t$ and $a$ be the temperature, age and scale factor of the Universe. We assume that variation in $H$ is very small and it can be approximated as a constant.

$$
\begin{aligned}
H_0&=\frac1a\frac{da}{dt}\approx\frac1a\frac{\Delta a}{\Delta t}\qquad\text{(19.61)}\\
\frac{\Delta a}{\Delta t}&=H_0a\qquad\text{(19.62)}\\
T&\propto a^{-1}\qquad\text{(19.63)}\\
\frac{\Delta T}{\Delta t}&=-H_0T_i\qquad\text{(19.64)}\\
\Delta t&=\frac{-\Delta T}{H_0T_i}\\
&=\frac{-(-0.1)\times3.0856\times10^{22}}{67.80\times10^3\times2.73}\\
&=1.667\times10^{16}\,\mathrm{s}\\
\Delta t&=528\,\mathrm{Myr}\qquad\text{(19.65)}
\end{aligned}
$$


Alternatively, one can solve this more rigourously using calculus. In a matter dominated universe,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\rho&=\zeta a^{-3}\qquad\text{(19.66)}\\
a&\propto t^{2/3}\qquad\text{(19.67)}\\
\Omega_0&=\frac{8\pi G\rho}{3H^2}=\frac{8\pi G}{3H^2}\frac{\zeta}{a^3}=1\qquad\text{(19.68)}
\end{aligned}
$$


Again using the definition of Hubble constant and find expression for $a$ by integration,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{da}{dt}&=Ha\\
\left(\frac{da}{dt}\right)^2&=H^2a^2=\frac{8\pi G\zeta}{3a}\\
\therefore\frac{da}{dt}&=\sqrt{\frac{8\pi G\zeta}{3a}}\qquad\text{(19.69)}\\
\therefore\int\sqrt a\,da&=\sqrt{\frac83\pi G\zeta}\int dt\qquad\text{(19.70)}\\
\frac23\left(a_f^{3/2}-a_i^{3/2}\right)&=\sqrt{\frac83\pi G\zeta}\,\Delta t\\
\frac23\left(\left(\frac{a_f}{a_i}\right)^{3/2}-1\right)&=\sqrt{\frac{8\pi G\zeta}{3a_i^3}}\,\Delta t\\
&=H_i\Delta t\\
\Delta t&=\frac23\left(\left(\frac{a_f}{a_i}\right)^{3/2}-1\right)\frac1{H_i}\qquad\text{(19.71)}\\
&=\frac23\left(\left(\frac{T_i}{T_f}\right)^{3/2}-1\right)\frac1{H_i}\\
&=\frac23\left(\left(\frac{2.73}{2.63}\right)^{3/2}-1\right)\frac{3.0856\times10^{22}}{67.80\times10^3}\\
&=1.730\times10^{16}\,\mathrm{s}\\
\Delta t&=548\,\mathrm{Myr}\qquad\text{(19.72)}
\end{aligned}
$$

<h4 id="c9-exg-t08-question">Условие</h4>



Based on the spectrum of a galaxy with redshift $z=6.03$ it was determined that the age of the stars in the galaxy is from 560 to 600 million years. At what $z$ did the epoch of star formation occur in this galaxy? Assume that the rate of expansion of the Universe is given by a flat cosmological model with cosmological constant $\Lambda=0$. (In such a model the scale factor $a\propto t^{2/3}$, where $t$ is the time since the Big Bang.)




<p>Условие: <a href="/collections/ioaa-problems-by-topic/source/711ac8d837c5f8d9a39467d3a734940e193d151edef342c2c232a543d4b48062.pdf#page=78">PDF 78</a></p>

<details id="c9-exg-t08-key">

<summary>Печатно решение / форма</summary>



$$
\begin{aligned}
1+z&=\frac{a_0}{a_z}=\left(\frac{t_0}{t_z}\right)^{2/3}\qquad\text{(19.73)}\\
&=\frac{t_0}{(1+z)^{1.5}}=\frac{1.377\times10^{10}}{(1+6.03)^{1.5}}\\
t&=739\times10^6\text{ years}\qquad\text{(19.74)}\\
t_{form,max}&=t-560\times10^6\\
&=179\times10^6\text{ years}\qquad\text{(19.75)}\\
t_{form,min}&=139\times10^6\text{ years}\qquad\text{(19.76)}\\
z_{form,min}&=\left(\frac{t_0}{t_f}\right)^{2/3}-1\qquad\text{(19.77)}\\
&=\left(\frac{13770}{179}\right)^{2/3}-1\\
&=17.1\qquad\text{(19.78)}\\
z_{form,max}&=20.4\qquad\text{(19.79)}
\end{aligned}
$$


Epoch of star formation was from $z=17.3$ to $z=20.8$

### 9.1 Theory · 9

<p>I07 - T18 - C</p>



<h4 id="c9-exg-t09-question">Условие</h4>



The deflection of light by a gravitational field was first predicted by Einstein in 1912, a few years before the publication of the General Relativity in 1916. A massive object that causes a light deflection behaves like a classical lens. This prediction was confirmed by Sir Arthur Stanley Eddington in 1919. Consider a spherically symmetric object with a mass $M$. This object will act like a lens, with an impact parameter $\xi$ measured from the centre of the object. The deflection equation in this case is given by:

![Figure 9.1 – Image of Abel Cluster](/collections/ioaa-problems-by-topic/assets/79f4de98a7edf36321624aa6729702603afe50328a5d63f5e8749403a5b6139f.png)

![Figure 9.2 – Geometric Model of Gravitational Lensing](/collections/ioaa-problems-by-topic/assets/b0a1042cdea6c67ae112aa19c470afc21e117b42d73f986e317c675c600e18bf.png)

$$
\phi=\frac{4GM}{\xi c^2}\tag{9.3}
$$

In a simpified model, the impact parameter may be seen as the shortest separation between the centre of the lens and path of a particular light ray. In figure 9.2, the massive object which behaves like a lens is at L. Light rays emitted from the source S being deflected by the lens are observed by observer O as images $S_1$ and $S_2$. Here, $\phi$, $\beta$, and $\theta$ are very very small angles.

(a) For a special case in which the source is perfectly aligned with the lens such that $\beta=0$, show that a ring-like image will occur with the angular radius,called Einstein radius $\theta_E$, given by:

$$
\theta_E=\sqrt{\left(\frac{4GM}{c^2}\right)\left(\frac{D_S-D_L}{D_LD_S}\right)}\tag{9.4}
$$

(b) The distance (from Earth) of a source star is about $50\,\mathrm{kpc}$. A solar-mass lens, along line-of-sight, is about $10\,\mathrm{kpc}$ from the star. Calculate the angular radius of the Einstein ring formed by this solar-mass lens with the perfect alignment.

(c) What is the resolution of the Hubble space telescope with $2.4\,\mathrm{m}$ diameter mirror? Can the Hubble telescope resolve the Einstein ring in 9b?

(e) Find the ratio $\dfrac{\theta_{1,2}}{\beta}$ ($\dfrac{\theta_1}{\beta}$ or $\dfrac{\theta_2}{\beta}$) in terms of $\eta$. Here $\theta_{1,2}$ represents each of the image positions in 9d and $\eta$ stands for the ratio $\dfrac{\beta}{\theta_E}$.

(f) Find also the values of magnifications $\dfrac{\Delta\theta}{\Delta\beta}$ in terms of $\eta$ for $\theta=\theta_{1,2}$ ($\theta=\theta_1$ or $\theta=\theta_2$), when $\Delta\beta\ll\beta$, and $\Delta\theta\ll\theta$.

In this question, it helps if you start with a general solution (i.e. $\beta\ne0$) then converge to special case $\beta=0$. Refer to figure 9.2. As $\phi$, $\beta$, and $\theta$ are very very small angles, the point of bending is very very close to the lens and almost at distance $D_L$ from the observer.

$$
\begin{aligned}
PS_1&=PS+SS_1\\
\theta D_S&=\beta D_S+(D_S-D_L)\phi\qquad\text{(19.80)}\\
(\theta-\beta)D_S&=(D_S-D_L)\frac{4GM}{\xi c^2}\\
&=(D_S-D_L)\frac{4GM}{\theta D_Lc^2}\\
\therefore\theta(\theta-\beta)&=\frac{4GM}{c^2}\frac{(D_S-D_L)}{D_LD_S}\\
\theta^2-\beta\theta&=\frac{4GM}{c^2}\frac{D_S-D_L}{D_LD_S}\qquad\text{(19.81)}
\end{aligned}
$$


(a) For a perfect alignment in which $\beta=0$, we have $\theta=\pm\theta_E$, where

$$
\theta_E=\sqrt{\frac{4GM}{c^2}\frac{(D_S-D_L)}{D_LD_S}}\tag{19.82}
$$

(b) For given parameters,

$$
\begin{aligned}
D_L&=50-10=40\,\mathrm{kpc}\qquad\text{(19.83)}\\
\theta_E&=\sqrt{\frac{4GM}{c^2}\frac{(D_S-D_L)}{D_LD_S}}\\
\theta_{E1}&=\sqrt{\frac{4\times6.672\times10^{-11}\times1.989\times10^{30}\times(50-40)}{(3\times10^8)^2\times50\times40\times3.0856\times10^{19}}}\\
&=9.8\times10^{-10}\,\mathrm{rad}\qquad\text{(19.84)}\\
\theta_{E1}&=2.0\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{arcsec}\qquad\text{(19.85)}
\end{aligned}
$$


(c) The resolution of the Hubble space telescope for mean optical wavelength is,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\theta_{Hubble}&=\frac{1.22\lambda}{D}=1.22\times\frac{1.22\times5\times10^{-7}}{2.4}\\
&=2.5\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm{rad}\qquad\text{(19.86)}\\
\theta_{Hubble}&>\theta_{E1}\qquad\text{(19.87)}
\end{aligned}
$$


Hence the Hubble telescope can not resolve this Einstein ring.

(d) The quadratic equation 19.81 has two distinct roots, namely,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\theta_1&=\frac\beta2+\sqrt{\left(\frac\beta2\right)^2+\theta_E^2}\qquad\text{(19.88)}\\
\theta_2&=\frac\beta2-\sqrt{\left(\frac\beta2\right)^2+\theta_E^2}\qquad\text{(19.89)}
\end{aligned}
$$


This implies that there are two images for a single isolated source.

(e) Dividing the above equations by $\beta$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\theta_{1,2}&=\frac\beta2\pm\sqrt{\left(\frac\beta2\right)^2+\theta_E^2}\\
\frac{\theta_{1,2}}\beta&=\frac12\pm\sqrt{\left(\frac12\right)^2+\left(\frac{\theta_E}\beta\right)^2}\\
\frac{\theta_{1,2}}\beta&=\frac12\pm\sqrt{\frac14+\frac1{\eta^2}}\\
&=\frac12\left(1\pm\frac{\sqrt{\eta^2+4}}\eta\right)\tag{19.90}
\end{aligned}
$$

(f) From the equation 19.81,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\theta^2-\beta\theta-\theta_E^2&=0\\
(\theta+\Delta\theta)^2-(\beta+\Delta\beta)(\theta+\Delta\theta)-\theta_E^2&=0\qquad\text{(19.91)}\\
(\theta^2-\beta\theta-\theta_E^2)+2\theta\Delta\theta-\beta\Delta\theta-\theta\Delta\beta&=0\\
(2\theta-\beta)\Delta\theta-\theta\Delta\beta&=0\qquad\text{(19.92)}
\end{aligned}
$$


Thus, we get,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\Delta\theta}{\Delta\beta}&=\frac\theta{2\theta-\beta}\qquad\text{(19.93)}\\
\left[\frac{\Delta\theta}{\Delta\beta}\right]_{\theta=\theta_{1,2}}&=\frac{\theta_{1,2}}{2\theta_{1,2}-\beta}\\
&=\frac{\theta_{1,2}/\beta}{2\theta_{1,2}/\beta-1}\\
&=\frac{\left(\frac12\left(1\pm\frac{\sqrt{\eta^2+4}}\eta\right)\right)}{2\left(\frac12\left(1\pm\frac{\sqrt{\eta^2+4}}\eta\right)\right)-1}\\
&=\frac{\eta\pm\sqrt{\eta^2+4}}{2(\eta\pm\sqrt{\eta^2+4}-\eta)}\\
\left[\frac{\Delta\theta}{\Delta\beta}\right]_{\theta=\theta_{1,2}}&=\frac12\left(1\pm\frac\eta{\sqrt{\eta^2+4}}\right)\qquad\text{(19.94)}
\end{aligned}
$$

### 9.1 Theory · 10

<h4 id="c9-exg-t10-question">Условие</h4>



Within the field of a galaxy cluster at a redshift of $z=0.500$, a galaxy which looks like a normal elliptical is observed, with an apparent magnitude in the B filter $m_B=20.40\,\mathrm{mag}$. The luminosity distance corresponding to a redshift of $z=0.500$ is $d_L=2754\,\mathrm{Mpc}$. The spectral energy distribution (SED) of elliptical galaxies in the wavelength range $250\,\mathrm{nm}$ to $500\,\mathrm{nm}$ is adequately approximated by the formula:

$$
L_\lambda(\lambda)\propto\lambda^4\tag{9.5}
$$

(i.e., the spectral density of the object’s luminosity, known also as the monochromatic luminosity, is proportional to $\lambda^4$.)

(a) What is the absolute magnitude of this galaxy in the B filter ?

(b) Can it be a member of this cluster?

Hint: Try to establish a relation that describe the dependence of the spectral density of flux on distance for small wavelength interval. Normal elliptical galaxies have maximum absolute magnitude equal to $-22\,\mathrm{mag}$. For blue band, effective midpoint wavelength is $445\,\mathrm{nm}$ and FWHM is $94\,\mathrm{nm}$.




<p>Условие: <a href="/collections/ioaa-problems-by-topic/source/711ac8d837c5f8d9a39467d3a734940e193d151edef342c2c232a543d4b48062.pdf#page=80">PDF 80</a></p>

<details id="c9-exg-t10-key">

<summary>Печатно решение / форма</summary>



$$
\begin{aligned}
F(\lambda_B)&=\frac{L_\lambda(\lambda_B)}{4\pi d_L^2}\qquad\text{(19.95)}\\
\lambda_{emitted}&=\frac{\lambda_{observed}}{(1+z)}\qquad\text{(19.96)}
\end{aligned}
$$


Let monochromatic flux of the galaxy be denoted by $S(\lambda)$, then for small wavelength interval $\Delta\lambda$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
S(\lambda_{ob})\Delta\lambda_{ob}&=\frac{L_\lambda(\lambda_{emitted})}{4\pi d_L^2}\Delta\lambda_{emitted}\\
&=\frac1{4\pi d_L^2}L_\lambda\left(\frac{\lambda_{emitted}}{1+z}\right)\frac{\Delta\lambda_{ob}}{1+z}\qquad\text{(19.97)}\\
S_{10}(\lambda)&=\frac{L_\lambda(\lambda)}{4\pi d_{10}^2}\\
\therefore\frac{S(\lambda)}{S_{10}(\lambda)}&=\left(\frac{d_{10}}{d_L}\right)^2\frac{L_\lambda\left(\frac\lambda{1+z}\right)}{L_\lambda(\lambda)}\frac1{1+z}\qquad\text{(19.98)}\\
&=\left(\frac{d_{10}}{d_L}\right)^2\left(\frac1{1+z}\right)^4\frac1{1+z}\\
\frac{S(\lambda)}{S_{10}(\lambda)}&=\left(\frac{d_{10}}{d_L}\right)^2\left(\frac1{1+z}\right)^5\qquad\text{(19.99)}
\end{aligned}
$$


Here we have used SED approximation, which is justified by the fact that $\lambda_{min}=250\,\mathrm{nm}$ for the approximation to be valid and the lower end of B band filter is at 445 - 47 = 398 nm. Thus,

$$
\lambda_{SED,min}(1+z)<\lambda_{B,min}\tag{19.100}
$$

This, means $\lambda$ is in the range of SED approximation. Now evaluting,

$$
\begin{aligned}
m_B-M_B&=-2.5\log\left(\frac{S(\lambda)}{S_{10}(\lambda)}\right)\qquad\text{(19.101)}\\
&=-2.5\log\left(\frac{d_{10}}{d_L}\right)^2-2.5\log(1+z)^{-5}\\
\therefore M_B&=m_B-5\log\left(\frac{d_L}{d_{10}}\right)-12.5\log(1+z)\qquad\text{(19.102)}\\
&=20.40-5\log\left(\frac{2754\times10^6}{10}\right)-12.5\log(1.5)\\
&=-2.5\log\frac{f(\lambda_B)4\pi(10pc)^2}{L(\lambda_B)}\qquad\text{(19.103)}\\
&=-24.00\qquad\text{(19.104)}
\end{aligned}
$$


As normal elliptical galaxies are fainter than this by atleast 2 magnitudes, we conclude that this galaxy is not a member of this cluster but is a forground object.

<h4 id="c9-exg-t11-question">Условие</h4>



Measurement of the cosmic microwave background radiation (CMB) shows that its temperature is practically the same at every point in the sky to a very high degree of accuracy. Let us assume that light emitted at the moment of recombination ($T_r\approx3000\,\mathrm{K}$, $t_r\approx300000$ years) is only reaching us now ($T_0\approx3\,\mathrm{K}$, $t_0\approx1.5\times10^{10}$ years). Scale factor ($a$) is defined as such $a_0=a(t=t_0)=1$ and $a_t=a(t<t_0)<1$. Note that the time from the end of inflation ($t=10^{-32}$ seconds) to the time of recombination was the radiation dominated period and the matter dominated period started at the recombination. During the radiation dominated period $a$ is proportional to $t^{1/2}$, while during the matter dominated period $a$ is proportional to $t^{2/3}$.

(a) Estimate the horizon distances when recombination took place. Assume that temperature $T$ is proportional to $1/a$.

(b) Consider two points in CMB imprint, which are currently observed at a separation angle $\alpha=5^\circ$. At the recombination epoch, would it have been possible for observers at these two points to communicate with each other using photons?

(c) Estimate the size of our Universe at the end of inflation period.

Note: “Horizon distance” is the present day angular separation between two points, such that observer at one of the point would be just able to receive photon from the other point.




<p>Условие: <a href="/collections/ioaa-problems-by-topic/source/711ac8d837c5f8d9a39467d3a734940e193d151edef342c2c232a543d4b48062.pdf#page=81">PDF 81</a></p>

(a) Let $\theta_0$ be the horizon distance and let $X_r$ be the physical distance between two points, which are on mutual horizon at recombination epoch. It would be tempting to relate the two by simple

$$
X_r=d\theta_0=c(t_0-t_r)\theta_0\tag{19.105}
$$

where $d$ is the distance to CMB horizon. However, it must be remembered that the space itself is continuously expanding. Hence the light from CMB has in fact travelled much shorter distance. The physical distance $X_r$ is the real horizon distance at that epoch.

$$
\begin{aligned}
X_r&=c(t_0-t_r)\theta_0\times\frac{a_r}{a_0}\qquad\text{(19.106)}\\
ct_r&=c(t_0-t_r)\theta_0\times\frac{T_0}{T_r}\qquad\text{(19.107)}\\
\therefore\theta_0&=\frac{T_rt_r}{T_0(t_0-t_r)}\approx\frac{T_rt_r}{T_0t_0}\qquad\text{(19.108)}\\
&\approx\frac{3000\times3\times10^5}{3\times1.5\times10^{10}}\qquad\text{(19.109)}\\
\theta_0&\approx0.02\,\mathrm{rad}=1.15^\circ\qquad\text{(19.110)}
\end{aligned}
$$


(b) No. It is not possible. As the given separation is more than $\theta_0$.

(c) We note that magnitude of current age of the universe can be also interpreted as the size of present universe in light years. Let $S$ denote size of the universe at a given epoch.

$$
\begin{aligned}
S_0&=1.5\times10^{10}\times9.46\times10^{15}\,\mathrm{m}\qquad\text{(19.111)}\\
S_r&=\frac{a_r}{a_0}S_0=\left(\frac{t_r}{t_0}\right)^{2/3}S_0\qquad\text{(19.112)}\\
S_i&=\frac{a_i}{a_r}S_r=\left(\frac{t_i}{t_r}\right)^{1/2}S_r\qquad\text{(19.113)}\\
&=\left(\frac{t_i}{t_r}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{t_r}{t_0}\right)^{2/3}S_0\qquad\text{(19.114)}\\
&=\left(\frac{10^{-32}}{3\times10^5\times3.1557\times10^7}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{3\times10^5}{1.5\times10^{10}}\right)^{2/3}S_0\qquad\text{(19.115)}\\
&=3.25\times10^{-23}\times7.37\times10^{-4}S_0\qquad\text{(19.116)}\\
&=2.40\times10^{-26}S_0\qquad\text{(19.117)}\\
&=2.40\times10^{-26}\times1.5\times10^{10}\times9.46\times10^{15}\qquad\text{(19.118)}\\
&\approx3.40\,\mathrm{mt}\qquad\text{(19.119)}
\end{aligned}
$$


Thus, the size of the universe was just 3.4 metres at the end of the inflation period.
