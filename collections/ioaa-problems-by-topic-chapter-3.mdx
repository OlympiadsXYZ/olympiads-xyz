<h4 id="c3-ga-t02-question">Условие</h4>



One night during a full Moon, the Moon subtends an angle of $0.46°$ to an observer. What is the observer’s distance to the Moon on that night?




<p>Условие: <a href="/collections/ioaa-problems-by-topic/source/711ac8d837c5f8d9a39467d3a734940e193d151edef342c2c232a543d4b48062.pdf#page=31">PDF 31</a></p>

<details id="c3-ga-t02-key">

<summary>Печатно решение / форма</summary>



During a full Moon we see the whole face of the Moon. Hence

$$
\text{angle in radians}=\frac{\text{diameter of moon}}{\text{distance to moon}} \tag{13.3}
$$


$$
\begin{aligned}
\therefore\text{distance to moon}&=\frac{\text{diameter of moon}}{\text{angle in radians}}\\
&=\frac{2\times1.7374\times10^6\mathrm m}{(0.46\pi)/180}\\
&=\frac{2\times1.7374\times10^6\times180}{0.46\pi}\\
&=4.3\times10^8\mathrm m. \tag{13.4}
\end{aligned}
$$

### 3.1 Theory · 4

<p>I12 - T03 - A</p>



<h4 id="c3-ga-t04-question">Условие</h4>



What is the time interval between two consecutive oppositions of Mars? Assume the orbit is circular.




<p>Условие: <a href="/collections/ioaa-problems-by-topic/source/711ac8d837c5f8d9a39467d3a734940e193d151edef342c2c232a543d4b48062.pdf#page=31">PDF 31</a></p>

<details id="c3-ga-t04-key">

<summary>Печатно решение / форма</summary>



By Kepler’s third Law,

$$
\frac{T_M^2}{T_E^2}=\frac{a_M^3}{a_E^3} \tag{13.7}
$$


$$
\begin{aligned}
\therefore T_M^2&=1.52^3\times(365.2564)^2\\
T_M&=1.52^{1.5}\times365.2564\\
T_M&=684.48\text{ days}. \tag{13.8}
\end{aligned}
$$


To find time betwen successive oppositions, we have to find the synodic period of the Mars.

$$
\frac1{T_{Sy}}=\frac1{T_E}-\frac1{T_M} \tag{13.9}
$$


$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac1{T_{Sy}}&=\frac1{365.2564}-\frac1{684.48}\\
T_{Sy}&=783.18\text{ days}. \tag{13.10}
\end{aligned}
$$


Thus the time between successive oppositions (synodic period) of Mars is 783 days.

### 3.1 Theory · 5

<p>I10 - T11 - A</p>



<h4 id="c3-ga-t05-question">Условие</h4>



Mars arrived at its great opposition at UT $17^h56^m$ Aug.28, 2003. The next great opposition of Mars will be in 2018, estimate the date of that opposition. The semi-major axis of the orbit of Mars is 1.524 AU.




<p>Условие: <a href="/collections/ioaa-problems-by-topic/source/711ac8d837c5f8d9a39467d3a734940e193d151edef342c2c232a543d4b48062.pdf#page=31">PDF 31</a></p>

<details id="c3-ga-t05-key">

<summary>Печатно решение / форма</summary>



First we find orbital period and synodic period of the Mars.

$$
\begin{aligned}
T_M&=\sqrt{\frac{R_M^3}{R_\oplus^3}}T_\oplus \qquad\text{(13.11)}\\
&=\sqrt{(1.524)^3}\\
&=1.8814\text{ years}\\
&=687.18\text{ days} \qquad\text{(13.12)}\\
\frac1{T_{sy}}&=\frac1{T_\oplus}-\frac1{T_M} \qquad\text{(13.13)}\\
&=\frac{T_\oplus T_M}{T_M-T_\oplus} \qquad\text{(13.14)}\\
&=\frac{1.8814}{1.8814-1}\text{ years} \qquad\text{(13.15)}\\
&=779.65\text{ days}. \qquad\text{(13.16)}
\end{aligned}
$$


This means there is an opposition of Mars about every 780 days. To find the dats of great opposition in the year 2018,

$$
15\times365+4=5479\text{ days} \tag{13.17}
$$


$$
\frac{5479}{779.65}=7.0275 \tag{13.18}
$$


It means that there will be 7 oppositions between Aug. 28, 2003 and Aug. 28, 2018. So the date for the great opposition in 2018 should be

$$
5479-7\times779.65=21.38\mathrm{days} \tag{13.19}
$$


i.e. 21.4 days before Aug. 28, 2018. It is on Aug. 7, 2018.

### 3.1 Theory · 6

<p>I12 - T02 - A</p>



<h4 id="c3-ga-t06-question">Условие</h4>



Calculate the length of the sidereal day on Earth. What would be the length of the solar and sidereal days, in the current time measures (our solar hours, minutes and seconds), if the Earth would rotate in the opposite direction, but with the same rotation speed?




<p>Условие: <a href="/collections/ioaa-problems-by-topic/source/711ac8d837c5f8d9a39467d3a734940e193d151edef342c2c232a543d4b48062.pdf#page=31">PDF 31</a></p>

<details id="c3-ga-t06-key">

<summary>Печатно решение / форма</summary>



The length of solar day is exactly 24 hrs. But sidereal day is a little less than solar day because of the yearly motion of the Sun.

Now, in one solar year the Earth completes 365.25 solar days but it completes one additional rotation around itself. Thus number of sidereal days will be 366.25.

$$
\begin{aligned}
T_{sid}&=T_{Sol}\times\frac n{n+1} \qquad\text{(13.20)}\\
&=24^h\times\frac{365.25}{365.25+1}\\
&=23^h56^m4^s. \qquad\text{(13.21)}
\end{aligned}
$$


Now, if Earth rotates in opposite direction then exactly opposite thing will happen i.e. the Earth will complete one rotation less than number of solar days in a solar year.

On the other hand, the Sidereal day will remain the same as it doesn’t depends on the rotation direction. (neglecting the precession)

$$
\begin{aligned}
T'_{Sol}&=T_{sid}\times\frac{n-1}n \qquad\text{(13.22)}\\
&=T_{Sol}\times\frac n{n+1}\times\frac{n-1}n\\
&=24^h\times\frac{365.25-1}{365.25+1}\\
&=23^h52^m8^s. \qquad\text{(13.23)}
\end{aligned}
$$

### 3.1 Theory · 8

<p>I11 - T04 - B</p>



<h4 id="c3-ga-t08-question">Условие</h4>



Assuming that Phobos moves around Mars on a perfectly circular orbit in the equatorial plane of the planet, give the length of time Phobos is above the horizon for a point on the Martian equator.




<p>Условие: <a href="/collections/ioaa-problems-by-topic/source/711ac8d837c5f8d9a39467d3a734940e193d151edef342c2c232a543d4b48062.pdf#page=32">PDF 32</a></p>

<details id="c3-ga-t08-key">

<summary>Печатно решение / форма</summary>



If the observer was at the centre of the Mars (C), Phobos would have been visible above horizon for exactly half the period. However, the observer is on martian surface (O), thus as shown in the figure 13.2, the Phobos will set at point P.

![Figure 13.2 — Horizon for martian observer](/collections/ioaa-problems-by-topic/assets/ed453e9a864ee9de21c85be89f935fc2aceb225dd9af2a6e411bb10368a3b93d.png)

Figure 13.2 – Horizon for martian observer

$$
\begin{aligned}
T_{ph}&=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{a_{ph}^3}{GM_{Mars}}} \qquad\text{(13.28)}\\
&=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{(9.38\times10^6)^3}{6.6726\times10^{-11}\times6.421\times10^{23}}}\\
&=2.76\times10^4\mathrm{sec}\\
T_{ph}&\approx7.660\mathrm{hrs}. \qquad\text{(13.29)}
\end{aligned}
$$


$$
\frac1{T_{syn}}=\frac1{T_{ph}}-\frac1{T_{Mars}} \tag{13.30}
$$


$$
\begin{aligned}
T_{syn}&=\frac{T_{Mars}T_{ph}}{T_{Mars}-T_{ph}}\\
&=\frac{24.623\times7.660}{24.623-7.660}\\
&=11.12\mathrm{hrs}. \tag{13.31}
\end{aligned}
$$


This is synodic period of Phobos for a martian observer. Now,

$$
\theta=\cos^{-1}\left(\frac{R_{Mars}}{R_{Ph}}\right) \tag{13.32}
$$


$$
\begin{aligned}
t&=\frac{2\theta}{360°}\times T_{syn}\\
&=\frac{T_{syn}}{180°}\cos^{-1}\left(\frac{R_{Mars}}{R_{Ph}}\right) \qquad\text{(13.33)}\\
&=\frac{11.12}{180}\cos^{-1}\left(\frac{3393}{9380}\right)\\
&=4.25\mathrm{hrs}. \qquad\text{(13.34)}
\end{aligned}
$$


Hence Phobos will be visible in the martian sky for about 4 hours and 15 minutes

$$

\begin{aligned}
R_{ex}^3&=\frac{1.6^2R_{en}^3}{0.2^2}\\

$$
\begin{aligned}

\end{aligned}
$$


The angular velocity of the Venus around the Sun, with respect to the earth will be,

$$
\begin{aligned}

&=\frac{2\pi\times206265}{86400\times60}\left(\frac1{224.70}-\frac1{365.25}\right)\\

\end{aligned}
$$

### 3.1 Theory · 12

<p>I10 - T15 - D</p>



<h4 id="c3-ga-t12-question">Условие</h4>



On average, the visual diameter of the Moon is slightly less than that of the Sun, so the frequency of annular solar eclipses is slightly higher than total solar eclipses. For an observer on the Earth, the longest total solar eclipse duration is about 7.5 minutes, and the longest annular eclipse duration is about 12.5 minutes. Here, the longest duration is the time interval from the second contact to the third contact. Suppose we count the occurrences of both types of solar eclipses for a very long time, estimate the ratio of the occurrences of annular solar eclipses and total solar eclipses. Assume the orbit of the Earth to be circular and the eccentricity of the Moon’s orbit is 0.0549. Count all hybrid eclipses as annular eclipses.




<p>Условие: <a href="/collections/ioaa-problems-by-topic/source/711ac8d837c5f8d9a39467d3a734940e193d151edef342c2c232a543d4b48062.pdf#page=33">PDF 33</a></p>

<details id="c3-ga-t12-key">

<summary>Печатно решение / форма</summary>



Let us call the semi-major axis of Moon’s orbit as $a$, its eccentricity as $e$, the revolution period $T$, apparent radius $r$, the distance between Earth and Moon as $d$, the apparent radius of the Sun as $r_\odot$. When the Moon is at perigee, the total eclipse will be longest and when the Moon is at apogee, the annular eclipse will be longest. By conserving angular momentum,

$$
d_{ap}^2\omega_{ap}=d_{peri}^2\omega_{peri} \tag{13.83}
$$


$$
\begin{aligned}
\therefore\frac{\omega_{peri}}{\omega_{ap}}&=\left(\frac{a(1+e)}{a(1-e)}\right)^2\\
&=\frac{(1+e)^2}{(1-e)^2}. \tag{13.84}
\end{aligned}
$$


As the Earth’s orbit around the Sun is circular, we can assume $\omega_\oplus$ is constant. Thus, time of eclipse at these two points,

$$
t_{ap}=\frac{2(r_\odot-r_{ap})}{\omega_{ap}} \tag{13.85}
$$


$$
t_{peri}=\frac{2(r_{peri}-r_\odot)}{\omega_{peri}} \tag{13.86}
$$


$$
\begin{aligned}
\therefore\frac{t_{ap}}{t_{peri}}&=\frac{\omega_{peri}}{\omega_{ap}}\frac{2(r_\odot-r_{ap})}{2(r_{peri}-r_\odot)}\\
&=\frac{(1+e)^2}{(1-e)^2}\frac{(r_\odot-r_{ap})}{(r_{peri}-r_\odot)}. \tag{13.87}
\end{aligned}
$$


Now we try to get $r_\odot$ in terms of $r_{ap}$.

$$
\frac{r_{ap}}{r_{peri}}=\frac{a(1-e)}{a(1+e)} \tag{13.88}
$$


$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{(r_\odot-r_{ap})}{(r_{peri}-r_\odot)}&=\frac{t_{ap}}{t_{peri}}\frac{(1-e)^2}{(1+e)^2}=k\\
(r_\odot-r_{ap})&=k\left(\frac{(1+e)}{(1-e)}r_{ap}-r_\odot\right)\\
(1+k)r_\odot&=\left(\frac{(1+e)}{(1-e)}k+1\right)r_{ap}. \tag{13.89}
\end{aligned}
$$


Evaluating,

$$
\begin{aligned}
k&=\frac{12.5}{7.5}\times\frac{(1-0.0549)^2}{(1+0.0549)^2}\\
&=1.667\times(0.8959)^2\\
k&=1.3378 \qquad\text{(13.90)}\\
r_\odot&=\left(\frac{(0.8959^{-1}\times1.3378+1)}{(1.3378+1)}\right)r_{ap}\\
r_\odot&=1.0665r_{ap}. \qquad\text{(13.91)}
\end{aligned}
$$


(13.92)

Now, for elliptical orbit, we find $\theta$ for which $r_\odot=r$.

$$
d_\theta=\frac{a(1-e^2)}{1-e\cos\theta} \tag{13.93}
$$


$$
d_{ap}r_{ap}=d_\theta r_\odot \tag{13.94}
$$


$$
\begin{aligned}
a(1+e)r_{ap}&=\frac{a(1-e)(1+e)}{1-e\cos\theta}1.0665r_{ap}\\
1-e\cos\theta&=1.0665(1-e)\\
\cos\theta&=-(0.0549)^{-1}(0.0665-1.0665e)\\
&=-0.145. \tag{13.95}
\end{aligned}
$$


Thus, annular eclipses will happen for about $98°$ orbit on either side of apogee. Our assumption here is that the Moon’s orbit is in the same plane as the earth. As eccentricity of the moon’s orbit is small, we make a further approximation of assuming average $\omega$ throughout the motion.

Thus, ratio of number of eclipses,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{f_A}{f_T}&\approx\frac{\Delta\theta_A}{\Delta\theta_T} \qquad\text{(13.96)}\\
&\approx\frac{98.32}{(180-98.32)} \qquad\text{(13.97)}\\
&\approx1.2. \qquad\text{(13.98)}
\end{aligned}
$$


*EA: Notes*

- Assumption in the last part (constant $\omega$) is a poor assumption. However, calculating exact area swept will require calculus and hence will be deemed beyond the scope of this book.
- We note that the Moon will move slower near apogee and faster near perigee hence this estimate is a lower end estimate.
- Inclination of the Moon’s orbit, positions of its nodes and precession of its nodes will complicate the calculation further.

<summary>Печатно решение / форма</summary>



From the figures of the Moon’s phases, we see that the Moon is almost full on June 10 as well as June 11. Thus, we can conclude that the full moon occurs on June 11 ($\pm0.5$ day), 2006. We are going to use this full moon as the reference for calculating the Moon’s age on Albert Einstein’s birthday (March 14, 1879).

Approximate time span between June 11, 2006 and March 14, 1879 is 127 years. There are 31 leap years between the two years.

So, there are $365\times127+31=46386$ days starting from January 1, 2006 to January 1, 1879. As a consequence, there are 46,475 days starting from June 11, 2006 to March 14, 1879.

Thus number of lunar months will be

$$
N=\frac{46475}{29.5306}=1573.79 \tag{13.99}
$$


This means 1573 lunar months and $0.79\times29.5306=23.37$ days. As we are going backwards in time, this means 23 and half days before full moon. i.e.

$$
p=\frac{29.5306\times3}2-23.37=23.37-14.77=8.6 \tag{13.100}
$$


That is 8.6 days before the new moon or $29.53-8.6=20.9$ days since last the new moon.

We note that our first assumption of exact date of the full moon is accurate only to $\pm0.5$ days. Errors in other quantities taken in this calculation are much smaller. Hence the error in our answer will also be about $\pm0.5$ days.

Thus, the Moon’s age on Albert Einstein’s birthday (March 14, 1879) is $20.9\pm0.5$ days.

(e) Semi-major axis is

$$
a=\frac{(r_{vmax}+r_{vmin})}2=0.723AU \tag{13.111}
$$


(f) Eccentricity could be calculated from both of aphelion and perihelion distances as

$$
e=\frac{(r_{vmax}-r_{vmin})}{2a}=0.00612 \tag{13.112}
$$
