## Условие

The White Bear and the Penguin from the previous International Astronomy Olympiads returned to their poles (North and South respectively), and decided to observe an annular solar eclipse. The Penguin was lucky to see an amazing picture: at the maximum phase of the eclipse the centres of both discs, solar and lunar, appeared just on the visible horizon. And what did the Bear observe at this time? Draw what the White Bear saw at that moment, and also contour by dotted line the true positions of the Sun and the Moon. Assume that the Earth is spherical. The drawing should include an artistic picture with an image of the Bear on North Pole; necessary sizes or angular sizes should be pointed out in the picture. Recollect for yourself the necessary information about the animals.

### Solution

It seems that since the points of observations are opposite, the situation should be absolutely symmetric: the White Bear should also observe half of the eclipsed solar disk on the horizon. But this is quite incorrect. Three circumstances must be taken into account.

**Horizon depression.** For any observer the physical horizon is slightly lowered. A person standing on a flat surface sees about 2.5′ depression. For Earth's radius $R$ and eye height $h$, the horizon dip is

$$
\arccos\frac{R}{R+h}\approx\frac{\sqrt{(R+h)^2-R^2}}R\approx\sqrt{\frac{2h}R}\quad\text{(radians)}.
$$

Take an Imperial Penguin with eyes about 1 m above the surface: depression about 2′. The White Bear is sitting, with eyes also about 1 m high, so depression about 2′ (standing, more than 2 m and about 3′). If the Penguin sees the solar centre on its horizon, it is already 2′ below the mathematical horizon. At the North Pole it is 2′ above the mathematical horizon, or 4′ above the sitting Bear's physical horizon — a quarter of the solar radius. Horizon depression is the smallest effect here.

**Atmospheric refraction.** This varies slightly with the weather, averaging about 35′ at the horizon. When the solar centre is visible on the South Pole horizon, at the North Pole it can be seen well above the horizon. Refraction at the South Pole contributes 35′, and at the North Pole about 25′ (not 35′, since the Sun is higher). This is still not the principal effect.

**Lunar parallax.** An annular eclipse is visible only in a narrow band on Earth. The distance to the Moon is 384 thousand km. The source then calls 12730 km the Earth's “polar radius” and prints

$$
12730/38400\approx0.0332\ \mathrm{rad}\approx1.9^\circ.
$$

*Source discrepancy: the printed 12730 km is a diameter, and the denominator 38400 lacks a zero relative to the preceding lunar distance. The printed numerical angular result is retained.*

The Bear does **not observe any solar eclipse**, neither annular nor partial. The solar centre is about $4'+35'+25'=64'$ (about 1°) above the horizon. The lunar disk is approximately 1°54′ below the solar one, totally below the horizon. More precisely this is 1°54′ below the true solar position; including refraction, the true Moon is 2°19′ below the apparent Sun.
