## Решение

<Spoiler title="Покажи официалното решение">

**Q1** — **a. The radius of the Crab pulsar**

A trivia/qualitative-ish question – common starter in recent IOAA papers.

Their dimensions are 10 km, 6 km, 120 nm, and 8.5 km respectively.

**Q2** — **d. -4.90**

$$
\begin{aligned} m_1 - m_0 &= -2.5\log\frac{\alpha_1 F}{\alpha_0 F} \\ m_1 &= m_0 - 2.5\log\frac{\alpha_1}{\alpha_0} \\ &= -2.91 - 2.5\log\frac{1}{0.16} \\ &= -4.899 \end{aligned}
$$

**Q3** — **d. 7:39 am**

Coordinate transformation equations for an object from equatorial to alt-azimuth reference frames are given by:

$$
\begin{aligned} \sin A\cos a &= \sin h\cos\delta \\ \cos A\cos a &= \cos h\cos\delta\sin\varphi - \sin\delta\sin\varphi \\ \sin a &= \cos h\cos\delta\cos\varphi + \sin\delta\sin\varphi \end{aligned}
$$

where $a$ is the altitude, $A$ is the azimuth, $h$ is the hour angle, $\delta$ is the declination and $\varphi$ is the altitude of the celestial pole / latitude of the observer.

Setting $a = 0$ in the third equation and rearranging for $h$

$$
\cos h = -\tan\delta\tan\varphi
$$

On December 21, $\delta = -23.5^{\circ}$. Hence

$$
\begin{aligned} h &= \cos^{-1}(-\tan(-23.5)\tan 39.9) \\ &= 68.68^{\circ} \\ &= 4h\ 34m\ 40s \end{aligned}
$$

Sunrise occurs 4 h 34 m 40 s before local solar noon, or 7:25:20 am.

Their civil time is +8 GMT, but their local time is $\frac{24\times 60}{360}\times 116.4 = 465.4\ min$ or +7:45:24 GMT.

They are 14m 26s behind their civil time: their watch will display 7:39 am at 7:25 local time.

**Q4** — **b. 23 h 56 m 4 s**

Trick question: Sidereal days are measured with respect to faraway stars; their length only depends on the speed of rotation of the Earth. The rotation direction hence doesn’t matter. The length of a sidereal day hence stays the same.

**Q5** — **a. 2.79 earth radii**

$$
\begin{aligned} OAS &= 180^{\circ} - 52^{\circ} = 128^{\circ} \\ OSA &= 52^{\circ} - 35.6^{\circ} = 16.4^{\circ} \end{aligned}
$$

From sine rule,

$$
\frac{r_{earth-satellite}}{r_{earth}} = \frac{\sin OAS}{\sin OSA}
$$

$$
r_{earth-satellite} = \frac{\sin 128^{\circ}}{\sin 16.4^{\circ}}r_{earth} = 2.791\,r_{earth}
$$

**Q6** — **b. 40 000 stars**

$$
\begin{aligned} v_{esc} &= \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{R}} \\ M &= \frac{Rv^2}{2G} \\ N\times(2M_{sun}) &= \frac{Rv^2}{2G} \end{aligned}
$$

$$
N = \frac{(20\times 3.26\times(3\times 10^{8})\times 365\times 86400)(6000)^2}{2(6.67\times 10^{-11})(4\times 10^{30})} \approx 41600\ stars
$$

**Q7** — **b. 8900 years ago**

$$
\text{diameter } s = r\theta
$$

$$
\begin{aligned} s &= [250\times 3.26\times(3\times 10^{8})\times 365\times 86400]\times((5/60)/180\times\pi) \\ &= 1.12\times 10^{16}\ m \end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned} r_{supernova} &= 1.12\times 10^{16}\div 2 \\ &= 5.61\times 10^{16}\ m \\ t &= \frac{d}{v} \\ &= \frac{5.61\times 10^{16}}{20000}\ seconds \\ &= 8890\ \text{years} \end{aligned}
$$

**Q8** — **c. A, D**

The wavelengths span the Fe + Ni absorption peaks.

Population III stars, the oldest stars in the universe, are metal poor.

Our sun, on the other hand, is rich in metals, after multiple generations of supernovae where these metals were synthesised.

**Q9** — **c. 280 K**

At equilibrium, $P_{absorbed} = \mathrm{P}_{emitted}$

$$
\begin{aligned} \frac{4\pi r_*^2\sigma T_*^4}{4\pi D_*^2}\times\pi r_g^2 &= 4\pi r_g^2\sigma T_g^4 \\ \frac{r_*^2 T_*^4}{D_*^2} &= 4T_g^4 \\ T_g &= T_*\sqrt{\frac{r_*}{2D_*}} \\ &= 5800\sqrt{\frac{r_*}{2(215)r_*}} \end{aligned}
$$

**Q10** — **d. D**

The hot exposed cores of stars. Dim as nuclear fusion has stopped.

**Q11** — **c. C**

The nearest red supergiant to Earth is Antares, which is 550 ly away.

**Q12** — **a. 200 mm**

Taking a quick and dirty guesstimate:

$$
\frac{g_{neutron}}{g_{earth}} = \left(\frac{r_{neutron}}{r_{earth}}\right)^2
$$

Assume $g \propto \tau$

$$
\begin{aligned} \tau_{neutron} &= \left(\frac{r_{neutron}}{r_{earth}}\right)^2\times\tau_{earth} \\ \tau_{neutron} &= \left(\frac{10\ 000}{6400\ 000}\right)^2\times 100\ 000 \end{aligned}
$$

The answer of 24 cm is not that far off from the actual value of about 10 cm.

**Q13** — **c. Its diameter is 1/10 of the star.**

$$
\begin{aligned} A &\propto \mathrm{r}^2 \\ 0.01 &= \left(\frac{r_{planet}}{r_{star}}\right)^2 \\ \frac{r_{planet}}{r_{star}} &= 0.1 \\ \frac{d_{planet}}{d_{star}} &= 0.1 \end{aligned}
$$

**Q14** — **b. 33%**

1n : 7p ⇔ 2n : 14p

2p fuse with 2n to form He.

Hence after fusion, the ratio of atoms would be 1 He : 12 H.

By mass, this would be

4 amu: 12 amu, or 1:3.

Repeating the above with 1n : 5p, we get a post-fusion atomic ratio of 1 He : 8 H.

By mass, this would be equivalent to

4 amu : 8 amu, or 1:2.

The answer is hence 33 %.

**Q15** — **b. 3000 Mpc**

$$
\begin{aligned} \sqrt{\frac{1+\frac{v}{c}}{1-\frac{v}{c}}} &= 1+z \\ \frac{1+\frac{v}{c}}{1-\frac{v}{c}} &= (1+z)^2 \\ \frac{v}{c} &= \frac{(1+z)^2-1}{(1+z)^2+1} \\ &= \frac{(2.4)^2-1}{(2.4)^2+1} \\ &= 0.704 \end{aligned}
$$

Invoking Hubble’s Law,

$$
\begin{aligned} v &= H_0 D \\ D &= \frac{v}{H_0} \\ &= \frac{0.704\times 3\times 10^5}{70} \\ &= 3017\ Mpc \end{aligned}
$$

**Q16** — **b. 46 km**

The angular resolution for a circular aperture is given by

$$
\theta = 1.22\frac{\lambda}{d_{aperture}}
$$

A lunar feature with angular length equal to the angular resolution has a diameter x. The two are related by

$$
\theta = \frac{x}{D_{Earth-Moon}}
$$

Putting the two together,

$$
\frac{x}{D_{Earth-Moon}} = 1.22\frac{\lambda}{d_{aperture}}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned} x &= 1.22\frac{\lambda D_{Earth-Moon}}{d_{aperture}} \\ &= 1.22\frac{(500\times 10^{-9})(3.78\times 10^{8})}{5\times 10^{-3}} \\ &= 46116\ m \end{aligned}
$$

**Q17** — **c. September**

The winter constellations are just about rising in the East, and the prominent summer triangle is halfway to setting in the West. The season hence is autumnal in the Northern hemisphere – September.

The sky is actually taken on 26 September, the second day of the GeCAA.

**Q18** — **e. Talinn, Estonia (59.4370° N, 24.7536° E)**

Polaris is more than halfway across the above the horizon in the North. The only possible option is Talinn, Estonia.

(Estonia is also the host country for the GeCAA).

**Q19** — **a. A**

A passes through Gemini, Taurus and Aries, which are all fully visible on this map. It passes through Pisces, Aquarius and Capricornus as well, though some of their stars are not visible on the map due to the magnitude filter applied.

**Q20** — **d. D**

The only great circle perpendicular to the celestial meridian is D.
