*ΩWAO 2023 ◇ Theoretical Round ◇ Problems*

## Условие

An artificial satellite moves in an orbit with eccentricity $e \gt 0$, semi-major axis $a$, and inclination $0 \lt i \lt 90^{\circ}$. The argument of perigee $\omega = 0^{\circ}$. Assume the Earth to be an ideal sphere rotating at a constant angular velocity $W$.

**a)** What are the satellite’s geographic latitude $\varphi(\nu)$ and longitude $\lambda(\nu)$ depending on the true anomaly $\nu$?

**b)** Consider a satellite in a geosynchronous orbit ($T = 23^{\mathrm{h}}\,56^{\mathrm{m}}\,04^{\mathrm{s}}$) with $e = 0.30$ and $i = 1.00$ rad. Calculate the ground track of the satellite (projection of the trajectory onto the surface of the rotating Earth) and draw it. For convenience, use the answer sheet with table and graph grid.

*Hint:* $$\int\frac{dx}{(1 + a\cos x)^2} = \frac{2\arctan\left(\sqrt{\frac{1-a}{1+a}} \cdot \tan\frac{x}{2}\right)}{(1 - a^2)^{3/2}} - \frac{a\sin x}{(1 - a^2)(1 + a\cos x)} + \mathrm{const}.$$

## Отговори

<Spoiler title="Покажи отговорите">

- **a)** $\varphi' = \arcsin(\sin i\sin\nu),\quad \lambda' = \arctan(\cos i\tan\nu) - W\sqrt{\frac{a^3}{GM_{\oplus}}}\left[2\arctan\left(\sqrt{\frac{1-e}{1+e}} \cdot \tan\frac{\nu}{2}\right) - \frac{e\sin\nu\sqrt{1-e^2}}{1+e\cos\nu}\right]$
- **b)** The ground track is a closed figure-eight loop through the origin, symmetric under $(\lambda', \varphi') \to (-\lambda', -\varphi')$, with latitude reaching $\pm 1$ rad (plot in the official solution).

</Spoiler>

## Решение

<Spoiler title="Покажи официалното решение">

a) Let the origin point be the ascending node of the satellite’s orbit — in this problem it coincides with perigee ($\omega = 0^{\circ}$). For the respective spherical triangle:

</figure>

$$\frac{\sin\varphi}{\sin i} = \frac{\sin\nu}{\sin 90^{\circ}} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \sin\varphi = \sin i\sin\nu,$$

$$\cos i = \frac{\tan\lambda}{\tan\nu} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \tan\lambda = \cos i\tan\nu.$$

Taking into account the rotation of the Earth, we may put down the geographical coordinates of the satellite:

$$\varphi' = \varphi = \arcsin(\sin i\sin\nu),$$

$$\lambda' = \lambda - W\int_0^t dt. \qquad (3)$$

Due to rotational symmetry, the longitude of the satellite is determined up to a shift by an arbitrary constant value.

The angular momentum of the satellite is conserved,

$$l = \frac{d\nu}{dt}r^2 = \sqrt{G\mathfrak{M}_{\oplus}\,a(1 - e^2)} = \mathrm{const}.$$

At the same time, the geocentric distance of the satellite depends on its true anomaly:

$$r = \frac{a(1 - e^2)}{1 + e\cos\nu}.$$

Therefore,

$$dt = \frac{r^2\,d\nu}{\sqrt{G\mathfrak{M}_{\oplus}\,a(1 - e^2)}} = \sqrt{\frac{a^3(1 - e^2)^3}{GM_{\oplus}}}\frac{d\nu}{(1 + e\cos\nu)^2}.$$

Next, we can replace the integration variable in (3):

$$\begin{aligned} \lambda' &= \lambda - W\sqrt{\frac{a^3(1 - e^2)^3}{GM_{\oplus}}}\int_0^{\nu}\frac{d\nu}{(1 + e\cos\nu)^2} = \\ &= \lambda - W\sqrt{\frac{a^3}{GM_{\oplus}}}(1 - e^2)^{3/2}\left[\frac{2\arctan\left(\sqrt{\frac{1-e}{1+e}} \cdot \tan\frac{\nu}{2}\right)}{(1 - e^2)^{3/2}} - \frac{e\sin\nu}{(1 - e^2)(1 + e\cos\nu)}\right] = \\ &= \arctan(\cos i\tan\nu) - W\sqrt{\frac{a^3}{GM_{\oplus}}}\left[2\arctan\left(\sqrt{\frac{1 - e}{1 + e}} \cdot \tan\frac{\nu}{2}\right) - \frac{e\sin\nu\sqrt{1 - e^2}}{1 + e\cos\nu}\right]. \end{aligned}$$

b) For geosynchronous orbit

$$\frac{2\pi}{T} = W = \sqrt{\frac{G\mathfrak{M}_{\oplus}}{a^3}},$$

so we can simplify the expression above a bit:

$$\lambda' = \arctan(\cos i\tan\nu) + \frac{e\sin\nu\sqrt{1 - e^2}}{1 + e\cos\nu} - 2\arctan\left(\sqrt{\frac{1 - e}{1 + e}} \cdot \tan\frac{\nu}{2}\right).$$

*Thoughts on plotting.* It is pretty obvious that the relative motion of the satellite is periodical and the track is closed. To plot the graph, we have to calculate $\varphi'$ and $\lambda'$ values for $\nu$ in range from $-\pi$ to $\pi$. In fact, the work can be simplified by noting that

$$\varphi'(-\nu) = -\varphi(\nu), \quad \lambda'(-\nu) = -\lambda'(\nu),$$

so it is enough to probe the range of $\nu$ from 0 to $\pi$, and then mirror the results.

Also, note that $\arctan(\#\tan\nu)$ is ambiguous. $\lambda'$ follows the motion of the satellite, therefore arctan should be re-defined to become a continuous function for $\nu \in [0;\ \pi)$:

$$\arctan(\cos i\tan\nu) := \begin{cases} \underline{\arctan}(\cos i\tan\nu), & \nu \leqslant \pi/2, \\ \underline{\arctan}(\cos i\tan\nu) + \pi, & \nu \gt \pi/2. \end{cases}$$

Here $\underline{\arctan}$ is “conventional” inverse trigonometric function ranged from $-\pi/2$ to $\pi/2$. Of course, $\underline{\arctan}(+\infty) := \pi/2$. Such a trick does not affect the parts of the expression that came along with that *remarkable* integral, since $\nu/2$ appears there as an argument.

For calculations, it is convenient to substitute the values of $e$ and $i$ and regroup:

$$\lambda'(\nu) \approx \begin{cases} \arctan(0.54\tan\nu) + \dfrac{0.286 \cdot \sin\nu}{1 + 0.3\cos\nu} - 2\arctan\left(0.734\tan\frac{\nu}{2}\right), & \nu \in [0;\ \pi/2), \\ \arctan(0.54\tan\nu) + 3.142 + \dfrac{0.286 \cdot \sin\nu}{1 + 0.3\cos\nu} - 2\arctan\left(0.734\tan\frac{\nu}{2}\right), & \nu \in (\pi/2;\ \pi), \\ -\lambda'(-\nu), & \nu \in (-\pi;\ 0) \setminus \{-\pi/2\}. \end{cases}$$
