## Условие

Particles of characteristic cometary matter of various sizes come off a comet. Estimate, the characteristic sizes $D$ of the particles which are not ejected outside the Solar System due to the solar radiation pressure.

*Note: You will get more points for the solution if you first derive the algebraic formula of the answer $D=f(a,b,c,d,e\ldots)$ and only then get the numerical answer by inserting the numerical data $a,b,c,d,e\ldots$ into this formula.*

*For Group α only. Note: In the middle of the time of the round you will be provided with one additional formula.*

## Решение

<Spoiler title="Покажи официалното решение">

Let us consider, that at some point, when the comet is at a distance $R$ from the Sun, as shown in Figure 4.1, comet particles with characteristic size $d$ the mass $m$ have detached from the comet, and their velocity with respect to the Sun being equal to the velocity of the comet, $\vec v$. Let us consider the shape of the particles as spherical with the radius $r=d/2$, and that they reflect all the light falling on them.

<figure className="problem-figure problem-figure--sized" style={{'--fig-w': '45.9%', '--fig-max': '294px'}}>
<img src="https://pub-43290baaaff14857b5dd59610ea438c7.r2.dev/problems/iao-2019-theory-theo/p6-sol-fig41.png" alt="Fig.4.1. Gravitational and radiation-pressure forces" width="920" height="520" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
</figure>


The forces acting on the comet particle are: $\vec F_g$ – the gravitational force of the Sun; $\vec F_p$ – the rejection force due to the solar radiation pressure;

$$
F_g=G\frac{mM_S}{R^2}.
$$

Let us find formula for the force of solar radiation.

Let's admit, as in Figure 4.2, that the Sun is a sphere with $\Sigma_0$ surface, having $R_S$ radius. It is called integral luminosity of Sun, $L$, the total radiation energy emitted per unit of time by Sun, on its entire surface, across all wavelengths in all directions ($L=A\cdot4\pi\cdot(\mathrm{au})^2=3.86\cdot10^{26}\ \mathrm W$). Dimension of luminosity is a power.

If $\Sigma$ is the circumferential sphere, whose radius $R$ represents the instantaneous distance between the ice particle and the center of the Sun, then, obviously, the solar radiation energy per unit of time through $\Sigma$ surface is equal to $L$.

If we use mirror reflection model of a disk, the solar radiation energy per unit of time which arrives on the surface $\pi r^2$ of the particle, is:

$$
x=L\cdot\frac{\pi r^2}{4\pi R^2}.
$$

<figure className="problem-figure problem-figure--sized" style={{'--fig-w': '47.8%', '--fig-max': '306px'}}>
<img src="https://pub-43290baaaff14857b5dd59610ea438c7.r2.dev/problems/iao-2019-theory-theo/p6-sol-fig42.png" alt="Fig.4.2. Radiation geometry" width="957" height="955" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
</figure>

<figure className="problem-figure problem-figure--sized" style={{'--fig-w': '45.3%', '--fig-max': '290px'}}>
<img src="https://pub-43290baaaff14857b5dd59610ea438c7.r2.dev/problems/iao-2019-theory-theo/p6-sol-fig43.png" alt="Fig.4.3. Photon momentum on a reflecting disk" width="907" height="1008" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
</figure>


As a result, the illumination of the surface of the circular disk (the solar radiation energy per unit of time, per unit area of the disk) is:

$$
E=\frac{x}{\pi r^2}=\frac{L}{4\pi R^2};\qquad \langle E\rangle_{\mathrm{SI}}=\frac{\mathrm W}{\mathrm m^2}.
$$

Using Figure 4.3 let's calculate the variation of photon momentum due to solar light reflection on the disk surface into a point:

$$
\Delta\vec p=\vec p_r-\vec p_i;\qquad \Delta p=p_r+p_i;
$$
$$
p_r=p_i=p_0=\frac{h\nu}{c};\qquad\Delta p=2\frac{h\nu}{c},
$$

where $h$ – the Planck constant, $\nu$ – light frequency, and $c$ – speed of light in vacuum.

As a result of principle of reciprocal action, on the disk will act a force:

If in $\Delta t$ time on the disk surface is reflecting $\Delta N_k$ photons with $\nu_k$ frequency, then the force that will act on ice particle will be:

$$
F_k=f_k\cdot\Delta N_k=\Delta N_k\cdot\frac{\Delta p_k}{\Delta t}=N_k\pi r^2\cdot\Delta t\cdot\frac{2h\nu_k/c}{\Delta t},
$$

where $N_k$ is the photon number with $\nu_k$ frequency which arrive on the unit surface area of the disk in unit time;

$$
F_k=2\cdot\frac{N_kh\nu_k}{c}\cdot\pi r^2;\qquad\langle N_kh\nu_k\rangle_{\mathrm{SI}}=\frac{\mathrm W}{\mathrm m^2};
$$
$$
N_kh\nu_k=E_k,
$$

representing the illumination of the surface of the disk due to its component with $\nu_k$ frequency of the solar radiation;

$$
F_k=2\frac{E_k}{c}\pi r^2;\qquad p_k=\frac{F_k}{\pi r^2}=2\frac{E_k}{c},
$$

representing the pressure exerted on disk by the light solar with $\nu_k$ frequency. According to all solar radiation components ($\nu_1,\nu_2,\ldots,\nu_n$), we calculate the resulting force acting on the disk:

$$
F_p=\sum_{k=1}^nF_k=2\frac{\sum_{k=1}^nE_k}{c}\pi r^2;\qquad\sum_{k=1}^nE_k=E,
$$

representing the total illumination of the disk due to all solar radiation components:

$$
F=2F_p=2\cdot\frac{E}{c}\pi r^2;\qquad p=\frac{F_p}{\pi r^2}=2\frac{E}{c},
$$

representing the solar light pressure on the ice particle. So for mirror plate:

$$
F_p=pS=2\frac{L}{4\pi R^2c}\times\pi r^2=\frac{Lr^2}{2cR^2}.
$$

where: $p$ – the solar radiation pressure, $L$ – the Sun luminosity, and $c$ – speed of light in vacuum, so that the result of the two collinear and opposite forces, acting on the ice particle is:

This issue is correct for the photons reflect just to back direction (back to the Sun). But this coefficient $k=2$ is different for the spherical shape of the ice particles. To calculate the average coefficient for income of all the directions of the photons reflected from the sphere of radius $r$, you should use integral:

$$
\int_0^{\pi/2}2\pi r\sin\alpha\cdot r\cos\alpha(1+\cos2\alpha)\,d\alpha;
$$

(where 1 is the part of momentum due to coming photons, and $\cos2\alpha$ is the is the part due to reflecting photons);

$$
k=\frac{\int_0^{\pi/2}2\pi r\sin\alpha\cdot r\cos\alpha(1+\cos2\alpha)\,d\alpha}{\pi r^2};
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
k&=\int_0^{\pi/2}2\sin\alpha\cos\alpha(1+\cos2\alpha)\,d\alpha\\
&=\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin2\alpha(1+\cos2\alpha)\,d\alpha\\
&=\int_0^{\pi/2}(\sin2\alpha+\sin2\alpha\cos2\alpha)\,d\alpha\\
&=\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin2\alpha\,d\alpha+\frac12\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin4\alpha\,d\alpha=1+0=1;
\end{aligned}
$$

so for spherical shape the average coefficient is $k=1$, and

$$
F_p=pS=\frac{L}{4\pi R^2c}\times\pi r^2=\frac{Lr^2}{4cR^2}.
$$

The total force,

$$
F=F_g-F_p=G\frac{mM_S}{R^2}-\frac{Lr^2}{4cR^2};
$$
$$
F=G\frac{m}{R^2}\left(M_S-\frac{Lr^2}{4cGm}\right),
$$

which is a central force resulting from the gravitational interaction of the comet particle and an equivalent Sun, having an effective mass:

$$
M_{S,\mathrm{effective}}=M_S-\frac{Lr^2}{4cGm}.
$$

These two interactions (gravitational and electromagnetic) between the ice particle and the Sun, with $M_S$ mass, can be replaced by a single interaction (gravitational) between the ice particle and an effective equivalent Sun, with mass, $M_{S,\mathrm{effective}}$, as shown in Figure 4.4.

<figure className="problem-figure problem-figure--sized" style={{'--fig-w': '41%', '--fig-max': '262px'}}>
<img src="https://pub-43290baaaff14857b5dd59610ea438c7.r2.dev/problems/iao-2019-theory-theo/p6-sol-fig44.png" alt="Fig.4.4. Effective Sun and comet particle" width="820" height="496" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
</figure>


Because $F<F_g$, the trajectory of the ice particle will no longer be the same as the comet trajectory. From that moment, the ice particle is in the gravitational field of an effective Sun with a smaller mass, so that the particle trajectory may be another ellipse, a parabola, or a hyperbolic, depending on the total energy of the particle – effective Sun system.

The total mechanical energy of the equivalent system, immediately after its formation (after detachment of the ice particle on the comet), is:

$$
E=\frac{mv^2}{2}-G\frac{mM_{\mathrm{ef}}}{R}.
$$

Depending on the algebraic sign of $E$, the ice particle leaves the Solar System or not. In order for the ice particle not to leave the Solar System, the condition must be fulfilled:

$$
E<0,
$$

so that, it follows:

$$
\frac{mv^2}{2}-G\frac{m}{R}\left(M_S-\frac{Lr^2}{4cGm}\right)<0.
$$

Let's admit that the detachment of the ice particle from the comet occurred when the comet was at aphelion, where velocity is very small, so it follows:

$$
v\approx0;\quad M_S-\frac{Lr^2}{4cGm}>0;\quad M_S>\frac{Lr^2}{4cGm};\quad m=\rho\cdot\frac{4\pi r^3}{3};
$$
$$
r>\frac{3L}{16\pi c\rho GM_S}.
$$

The value of luminosity $L$ we may get from the solar constant $A=1367\ \mathrm{W/m^2}$ and 1 au distance:

$$
L=A\cdot4\pi\cdot(\mathrm{au})^2;\qquad r>\frac{3A\cdot(\mathrm{au})^2}{4c\rho GM_S}.
$$

We may estimate the density of comet particles as the characteristic density of the ice matter in comets, about ~200–400 kg/m³. Let us take for calculation $\rho=300\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$. Also we should take the values of speed of light in vacuum: $c=299\,792\,458\ \mathrm{m/s}$, constant of gravitation: $G=6.674\cdot10^{-11}\ \mathrm{N\cdot m^2/kg^2}$, and solar constant: $A=1367\ \mathrm{W/m^2}$, from the table of constants; 1 astronomical unit: 1 au = 149.6 mln.km, and mass of the Sun: $M_S=1.989\cdot10^{30}\ \mathrm{kg}$, from the table of Solar System. The calculations give us the critical radius,

$$
r\approx1.9\cdot10^{-6}\ \mathrm m,\qquad d=2r\approx3.8\cdot10^{-6}\ \mathrm m.
$$

As the minimum accuracy of the used data is 1 significant digit (we consider $\rho=300\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$) the final answer should be done with 1 significant digit as well:

$$
d>4\cdot10^{-6}\ \mathrm m=4\ \mu\mathrm m.
$$
