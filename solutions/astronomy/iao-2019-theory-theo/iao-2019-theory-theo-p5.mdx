Supergiant UY Scuti is the largest (its volume is estimated as 5 billion volumes of the Sun) and one of the fastest burning stars currently known. Herewith, the mass loss per unit time due to radiation is only 0.04% of the total mass loss over the same time, and only 0.5% of the light pass through its shell (that is, go to the observers).

## Решение

<Spoiler title="Покажи официалното решение">

<ProblemSection id="solution-solution-1">

### 3.1.

In order to plot the position of the star on the Hertzsprung-Russell diagram we need to know its absolute magnitude and temperature.

The absolute stellar magnitude of UY Scuti, if we consider it only by the light reaching us, is found from the data of its apparent magnitude and the distance that can be obtained by knowing parallax.

$$
M=m+5^{\mathrm m}-5^{\mathrm m}\log D=m+5^{\mathrm m}+5^{\mathrm m}\log p.
$$

$$
M_2=9.1^{\mathrm m}+5^{\mathrm m}+5^{\mathrm m}\log(0.00034)=-3.25^{\mathrm m},
$$

Given the correction for the fact that only 0.5% of the light passes through the envelope surrounding it, we get the true value of the absolute magnitude in the visible range:

$$
M_1=M_2+2.5^{\mathrm m}\log(0.005)=-9^{\mathrm m}.
$$

This value should be used to find to plot the position of the star at the Hertzsprung-Russell diagram on a vertical scale. It also follows from this that the luminosity of UY Scuti in the visible range is greater than the solar one $L_0$ in

$$
L_S/L_0=100^{(4.8+9)/5}\approx330\,000\ \mathrm{times}.
$$

On the other hand,

$$
L_S/L_0=(R_S/R_0)^2(T_S/T_0)^4=(V_S/V_0)^{2/3}(T_S/T_0)^4,
$$

where from

$$
T_S=T_0\cdot(V_0/V_S)^{1/6}(L_S/L_0)^{1/4}=5778\ \mathrm K\times(5000000000)^{-1/6}(330000)^{1/4}\approx3350\ \mathrm K,
$$

Thus, the position of the star in the diagram should be plotted at the point with approximate coordinates $[3350\ \mathrm K,-9^{\mathrm m}]$.

</ProblemSection>

<ProblemSection id="solution-solution-2">

### 3.2.

The Sun radiates

$$
W_0=A\cdot4\pi a^2=1367\ \mathrm{W/m^2}\times4\pi(1.496\cdot10^{11}\ \mathrm m)^2=3.84\cdot10^{26}\ \mathrm W.
$$

UY Scuti star emits ≈330,000 times more, that is,

$$
W_S=330000\cdot3.84\cdot10^{26}\ \mathrm W\approx1.26\cdot10^{32}\ \mathrm W.
$$

For this reason, per unit time, the UY Scuti loses mass

$$
\Delta m_R/\Delta t=(\Delta E/c^2)/\Delta t=W_S/c^2=1.26\cdot10^{32}/(3\cdot10^8\ \text{м/с})^2\ \mathrm W\approx1.4\cdot10^{15}\ \mathrm{kg/s}.
$$

The total mass loss per unit time is

$$
\Delta m_0/\Delta t=(\Delta m_R/\Delta t)/0.0004\approx3.5\cdot10^{18}\ \mathrm{kg/s}.
$$

Over the year, UY Scuti is losing

$$
\Delta m_Y=3.5\cdot10^{18}\ \mathrm{kg/s}\times3600\times24\times365.25\ \mathrm s\approx1.1\cdot10^{26}\ \mathrm{kg}.
$$

For evaluation, we should assume that the rate of mass loss of the star will continue and it will exist until the complete loss of mass. In this case, the remaining life time of the UY Scuti will be

$$
\Delta\tau=8M_\odot/\Delta m_Y=8\cdot2\cdot10^{30}\ \mathrm{kg}/1.1\cdot10^{26}\ \mathrm{kg}\approx1.5\cdot10^5\ \mathrm{years}.
$$

Only 150 thousand years. Very transient on a cosmic scale.

</ProblemSection>
