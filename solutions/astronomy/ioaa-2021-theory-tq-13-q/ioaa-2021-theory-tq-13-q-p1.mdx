*Theory — Q13-1 — English (Official) — Lucy: The First Mission to the Trojan Asteroids (15 points). · 15 т.*

## Условие

CCD cameras on space probes are very sensitive and exposed to space weather conditions. Intense radiation passing through the CCD produces electron-hole pairs in the silicon of the CCD chip. The rate at which these pairs are produced is an important parameter when operating cameras on board spacecraft and can be calculated for radiation of any given energy.

A high energy particle or photon of radiation passing through the CCD will deposit some energy in the chip with each electron-hole pair it creates. The 'stopping power' of silicon for a given type of particle can be measured as the energy per areal density ($areal\ density\ =\ mass\ per\ unit\ area$) that the silicon 'takes away' from the travelling particle.

NASA's Lucy mission will be the first to study the Trojan asteroids and will revolutionize our understanding of the formation of the Solar System. One of the instruments on board is L'LORRI (Lucy LOng Range Reconnaissance Imager), which contains a sensitive CCD in order to produce detailed images of the Trojan asteroids. Unfortunately, the radiation around Jupiter is very intense and it can generate a lot of 'noise' in the pixels of the CCD.

Let us assume that an average charged particle trapped in Jupiter's magnetic field has an energy of $15\ MeV$ and that the flux of such particles in this region is equivalent to about 600 electrons $s^{-1}\ cm^{-2}$. Also assume that for each electron-hole pair which a particle passing through a pixel creates, it deposits exactly the excitation energy of the pair in that pixel. After the pixel crosses a threshold number of electron-hole pairs it is 'excited' and no more pairs can be produced in that pixel. Any remaining energy in the particle is passed to the next pixel (and so on).

Using the data given below for the CCD chip in the L'LORRI camera, answer the following questions:

CCD Data:

- Exposure time of an image = 30 ms
- Pixels on the CCD = 1024 x 1024
- CCD Area = 13 mm x 13 mm
- CCD chip thickness = 0.06 cm
- Density of silicon, $\rho\ =\ 2.34\ \mathrm{g}\ \mathrm{cm}^{-3}$
- Excitation energy of single pair = 2.36 eV
- Excitation threshold of a single pixel = 250 pairs
- Stopping power' of silicon for a 15 MeV electron = 3.012 MeV $\mathrm{g}^{-1}\ \mathrm{cm}^{2}$

**13.1** How many pixels will be excited by one such particle of radiation passing through the CCD when the spacecraft is near Jupiter's orbit? **[10 т.]**

**13.2** Given the radiation flux near Jupiter, what percentage of the total number of pixels in an image will be excited? **[5 т.]**

## Отговори

<Spoiler title="Покажи отговорите">

- **13.1** 716 pixels
- **13.2** 2.08 %

</Spoiler>

## Решение

<Spoiler title="Покажи официалното решение">

### 13.1

A single electron deposits energy in the CCD, as follows:

$$
E_{\mathrm{deposited}}=\text{stopping power}\times\rho_{\mathrm{si}}\times\text{thickness}
$$

$$
E_{\mathrm{deposited}}=3.012\frac{MeV\,cm^2}{g}\times\frac{2.34g}{cm^3}\times0.06cm=422.9\ keV
$$

[4 points]

Since an electron with 15 MeV energy deposits 422.9 keV, we must calculate the number of pairs electron/hole

$$
422.9keV\times\frac1{2.36eV}=1.79\times10^5\frac eh
$$

[4 points]

How many pixels will be able to excite $1.79\times10^5$ pairs of e/h?

$$
1.79\times10^5\times\frac1{250}=716\ pixels
$$

[2 points]

### 13.2

The number of electrons entering the detector area is:

$$
flux\times area\times t
$$

[1 point]

$$
\frac{600e}{cm^2s}\times[1.3\times1.3]cm^2\times0.03s=30\ e^-
$$

[1 point]

~30 electrons enter the detector area, and we know that one electron excites 716 pixels, the total number of excited pixels is:

$$
716\frac{pixels}{electron}\times30.42\ electrons=2.18\times10^4pixels
$$

[1 point]

If the CCD has a total of $1024\times1024$ pixels $=1.048\times10^6pixels$ then the total percentage of pixels that will be driven in a single image is ~2.08% [2 points]


</Spoiler>

<details>
<summary>Official source discrepancy</summary>

The supplied key rounds the incoming electron count to 30 and then uses 30.42 in the subsequent multiplication; both printed values are retained.
