## Условие

An astro-photographer has taken the photo of the moon close to a new moon day shown below right before the sunset on December 21 (Winter Solstice) in a wide open area.

<figure className="problem-figure problem-figure--sized" style={{'--fig-w': '74.7%', '--fig-max': '479px'}}>
<img src="https://pub-43290baaaff14857b5dd59610ea438c7.r2.dev/problems/usaaao-2020-ii-second-exam-apr4/p8-fig1.png" alt="Photograph of the western horizon at sunset: the Sun (labelled “Sun”) sits on the dark horizon at the centre of a semicircle, with green equiangular lines radiating from it; a thin crescent labelled “The Moon” is up and to the right of the Sun." width="1496" height="704" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />

**(a)** In which hemisphere (Northern or Southern) is the photographer located?

**(b)** Find the latitude of the photographer. Ignore the orbital inclination of the Moon and the ellipticity of the Earth’s orbit. Hint: The green equiangular lines are added to the image to help you out in measuring any relevant angle.

**(c)** Calculate the sidereal time when the photo was taken.

## Отговори

<Spoiler title="Покажи отговорите">

- **(a)** Southern
- **(b)** φ = 44.6° S
- **(c)** 1h 42m

</Spoiler>

## Решение

<Spoiler title="Покажи официалното решение">

**Solution:**

(a) By looking at the position of the moon with respect to the sun, we know that the photo has been taken from the Southern hemisphere.

(b) First off, we measure the angle between the horizon and ecliptic. It is $\sim 40^{\circ}$.

<figure className="problem-figure problem-figure--sized" style={{'--fig-w': '46.5%', '--fig-max': '298px'}}>
<img src="https://pub-43290baaaff14857b5dd59610ea438c7.r2.dev/problems/usaaao-2020-ii-second-exam-apr4/p8-sol-fig1.png" alt="The sunset photograph overlaid with a protractor centred on the Sun (scales 0–180 in black and blue) and a red line labelled “ECLIPTIC” through the Sun and the Moon, showing an angle of about 40° between the horizon and the ecliptic." width="932" height="486" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
</figure>

Drawing the celestial spheres, let $S$ be the celestial south pole, $Z$ be the zenith, and $K'$ be the South ecliptic pole. $\widehat{SZ} = 90 - \phi$; $\widehat{S(Sun)} = 90 - 23.5 = 66.5^{\circ}$; $\widehat{ZK'} = 40^{\circ}$; $\widehat{SK'} = 23.5^{\circ}$; $\widehat{Z(Sun)} = 90^{\circ}$; $\widehat{K'(Sun)} = 90^{\circ}$ and $\angle K'S(Sun) = 180^{\circ}$. So, Sun is the pole of a great circle crossing both $K'$ and $Z$ points, $\Rightarrow \angle S(Sun)Z = 40^{\circ}$. Using the spherical law of cosines, $\sin\phi = \cos 23.5^{\circ}\cos 40^{\circ} \Rightarrow \phi = 44.6^{\circ}$. Since the astrophotographer is in the Southern hemisphere, $\phi = 44.6^{\circ}S$.

(c) Sidereal time=$H_{\odot}$+$RA_{\odot}$. Using the spherical law of cosines, $\cos H_{\odot} = -\tan 23.5^{\circ}\tan 44.6^{\circ} \Rightarrow H_{\odot} = 7^h42^m$. $RA_{\odot}$(Winter Solstice)$= 18^h \Rightarrow ST = 7^h42^m + 18^h = 1^h42^m$.


</Spoiler>
