### (T12.1) Stellar mass blackholes

A black hole (BH) typically forms by the gravitational collapse of a massive star at the end of its life cycle to a point called a singularity. Due to the extreme gravity of such an object, nothing that enters the so-called event horizon (a spherical surface with $r=R_\mathrm{SC}$, where $r$ is the distance from the singularity) is able to escape from it. Here, $R_\mathrm{SC}$ is referred to as the Schwarzschild radius.

**(T12.1a)** **Modelling the origin of Hawking radiation:** Consider a pair of particles, each with mass $m$, produced on either side of the BH horizon. One particle is slightly outside the horizon at $r\approx R_\mathrm{SC}$, while the other particle is inside the horizon at $r=\kappa R_\mathrm{SC}$. Assume that the total energy of a particle is the sum of its rest mass energy $mc^2$ and the gravitational potential energy due to the BH. Determine the value of $\kappa$ for which the particle pair has zero total energy. **[4 т.]**

**(T12.1b)** **Temperature of a black hole:** If the particle produced outside the horizon in the above process has enough kinetic energy, it may escape the BH in a process called Hawking radiation. The one inside the horizon, which has negative energy, gets absorbed and decreases the mass of the BH.

Assume that all Hawking radiation is made of photons with a black body spectrum which peaks at the wavelength $\lambda_\mathrm{bb}\approx16R_\mathrm{SC}$. It is known that for a solar mass BH, $R_{\mathrm{SC},\odot}=2.952\,\mathrm{km}$.

Obtain an expression for the temperature, $T_\mathrm{bh}$, of the BH corresponding to this black body radiation, in terms of its mass $M_\mathrm{bh}$ and physical constants. Calculate the Schwarzschild radius, $R_{\mathrm{SC},10\odot}$, and temperature, $T_{\mathrm{bh},10\odot}$, for a BH with mass $10M_\odot$. **[4 т.]**

**(T12.1c)** **Mass loss of a black hole:** Assume that the Hawking radiation is emitted out from the event horizon. Using the mass-energy equivalence, obtain an expression for the rate of mass loss, $dM_\mathrm{bh}(t)/dt$, in terms of the mass $M_\mathrm{bh}(t)$ of the BH and physical constants.

Hence, obtain an expression for $M_\mathrm{bh}(t)$ for a BH with initial mass $M_0$. Sketch $M_\mathrm{bh}(t)$ as a function of $t$ from $M_\mathrm{bh}=M_0$ to $M_\mathrm{bh}=0$. **[8 т.]**

**(T12.1d)** **Lifetime of a black hole:** Obtain an expression for the lifetime $\tau_\mathrm{bh}$ at which a black hole with initial mass $M_0$ completely evaporates due to Hawking radiation, in terms of $M_0$ and physical constants. Calculate the lifetime $\tau_{\mathrm{bh},10\odot}$ (in seconds) for a black hole with $M_0=10M_\odot$. **[3 т.]**

**(T12.1e)** **Black hole in a CMB radiation bath:** Consider an isolated black hole in space, far away from other bodies, with a current temperature $T_\mathrm{bh}^\mathrm{now}$, surrounded by the cosmic microwave background (CMB) with a current temperature $T_\mathrm{cmb}^\mathrm{now}=2.7\,\mathrm{K}$. The black hole can grow in mass by absorbing CMB radiation, and lose its mass by Hawking radiation.

Taking into account the accelerating expansion of the Universe, identify which of the following figures show the long-term time evolution of $T_\mathrm{bh}$ in the following three cases:

(X) $T_\mathrm{bh}^\mathrm{now}>T_\mathrm{cmb}^\mathrm{now}$, (Y) $T_\mathrm{bh}^\mathrm{now}=T_\mathrm{cmb}^\mathrm{now}$, (Z) $T_\mathrm{bh}^\mathrm{now}<T_\mathrm{cmb}^\mathrm{now}$.

### (T12.2) Primordial black holes

Primordial black holes (PBHs) of much smaller masses can form in the very early Universe. All the following questions are related to PBHs. Here, any processes that increase the mass of the black hole may be neglected.

**(T12.2a)** **PBH evaporating at the current epoch:** As you may have noticed from the answers to the previous questions, black holes of solar mass would take a long time to evaporate. However, since PBHs can have a much smaller mass, we may be able to see them evaporating in current times.

Find the initial mass $M_{0,\mathrm{PBH}}$ (in kg), Schwarzschild radius $R_{\mathrm{SC},\mathrm{PBH}}$ (in m), and temperature $T_\mathrm{PBH}$ (in K) of a black hole that may be evaporating away completely at the present epoch, i.e., those with lifetime $\tau_\mathrm{PBH}=14$ billion years. **[4 т.]**

**(T12.2b)** **Formation of a PBH:** In the radiation-dominated early Universe, the scale factor varies as $a(t)\sim t^{1/2}$. In this era, PBHs form due to the collapse of all energy contained in a region of physical size $ct$, where $t$ is the age of the Universe at that time.

A PBH with mass of $10^{12}\,\mathrm{kg}$ forms when the age of the Universe is about $10^{-23}\,\mathrm{s}$. Calculate the age of the Universe, $t_{20}$, when a PBH of mass $10^{20}\,\mathrm{kg}$ forms. **[6 т.]**

**(T12.2c)** **Observed spectrum of Hawking radiation from PBH:** Consider a PBH of initial mass $10^{10}\,\mathrm{kg}$ which completely evaporates at the end of its lifetime $\tau_\mathrm{PBH}$. For this part, assume for simplicity that most of the Hawking radiation is emitted at this time, with a temperature corresponding to its initial mass. Also, take the scale factor of the Universe to be evolving as $a(t)\sim t^{2/3}$.

Calculate the peak wavelength of this Hawking radiation as observed at Earth, $\lambda_\mathrm{earth}$, at the present epoch (at $t=14$ billion years). **[5 т.]**

### Part 1: Stellar mass black holes

#### (T12.1a)

- Total energy of any particle is $E=E_{\mathrm{grav}}+mc^2$, so we have
$$
E_1=-\frac{GM_{\mathrm{bh}}m}{R_{\mathrm{SC}}}+mc^2,\qquad E_2=-\frac{GM_{\mathrm{bh}}m}{\kappa R_{\mathrm{SC}}}+mc^2.\qquad[1.0]
$$
- We require $E_1+E_2=0$. Hence,
$$
2mc^2-\frac{GM_{\mathrm{bh}}m}{R_{\mathrm{SC}}}\left(1+\frac1\kappa\right)=0.\qquad[1.0]
$$
- Then
$$
\frac1\kappa=\frac{2c^2R_{\mathrm{SC}}}{GM_{\mathrm{bh}}}-1=3,
$$
where we have used $R_{\mathrm{SC}}=2GM_{\mathrm{bh}}/c^2$. [1.0]
- This gives $\boxed{\kappa=\frac13}$. [1.0]

#### (T12.1b)

- Using Wien’s law, $\lambda_{\mathrm{bb}}=b/T_{\mathrm{bh}}$, where $b=2.898\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m\,K}$. [1.0]
- Hence,
$$
T_{\mathrm{bh}}=\frac{b}{16R_{\mathrm{SC}}}\implies\boxed{T_{\mathrm{bh}}=\frac{bc^2}{32GM_{\mathrm{bh}}}}.\qquad[1.0]
$$
- Since $R_{\mathrm{SC}}$ for a solar mass black hole is 2.952 km, for a black hole with mass $10\,M_\odot$, we get $\boxed{R_{\mathrm{SC},10\odot}\simeq29.52\ \mathrm{km}}$. [1.0]

Given the significant figures, 29 or 30 km will get full marks.

- The temperature of this BH will be
$$
T_{\mathrm{bh},10\odot}=\frac{2.898\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m\,K}}{16\times R_{\mathrm{SC},10\odot}},
$$
$$
\boxed{T_{\mathrm{bh},10\odot}=6.1\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm K}.\qquad[1.0]
$$

#### (T12.1c)

- Power emitted by an evaporating black hole $=\sigma T_{\mathrm{bh}}^4\,4\pi R_{\mathrm{SC}}^2$. [1.0]
- This gives
$$
\frac{dM_{\mathrm{bh}}}{dt}=-\frac1{c^2}\sigma T_{\mathrm{bh}}^4\,4\pi R_{\mathrm{SC}}^2.\qquad[1.0]
$$
- With $T_{\mathrm{bh}}=b/(16R_{\mathrm{SC}})$,
$$
\boxed{\frac{dM_{\mathrm{bh}}}{dt}=-\frac{\pi b^4\sigma c^2}{16^4G^2}\frac1{M_{\mathrm{bh}}^2}}.\qquad[1.0]
$$
- This gives
$$
\frac{dM_{\mathrm{bh}}}{M_{\mathrm{bh}}^2}=-\frac{\pi b^4\sigma c^2}{16^4G^2}\,dt,\qquad\text{and hence }\frac{M_{\mathrm{bh}}^3}{3}=-\frac{\pi b^4\sigma c^2}{16^4G^2}t+C.\qquad[1.0]
$$
- Since $M_{\mathrm{bh}}=M_0$ at $t=0$, we get $M_{\mathrm{bh}}^3=M_0^3-3\frac{\pi b^4\sigma c^2}{16^4G^2}t$. [0.5]
$$
\boxed{M_{\mathrm{bh}}(t)=\left(M_0^3-3\frac{\pi b^4\sigma c^2}{16^4G^2}t\right)^{1/3}}.\qquad[1.0]
$$
The value of $\frac{\pi b^4\sigma c^2}{16^4G^2}=3.868\times10^{15}\ \mathrm{kg^3\,s^{-1}}$, not necessary here, but gets full credit.

#### (T12.1d)

- We know that $M(t)=\left(M_0^3-3\frac{\pi b^4\sigma c^2}{16^4G^2}t\right)^{1/3}$.
- Clearly, $M(t)=0$ when $t=\frac{M_0^3}{3}\frac{16^4G^2}{\pi b^4\sigma c^2}$. Thus,
$$
\boxed{\tau_{\mathrm{bh}}=\frac{M_0^3}{3}\frac{16^4G^2}{\pi b^4\sigma c^2}}.\qquad[1.0]
$$
- With $A\equiv\frac{\pi b^4\sigma c^2}{16^4G^2}\simeq3.868\times10^{15}\ \mathrm{kg^3\,s^{-1}}$ [0.5], this gives
$$
\tau_{\mathrm{bh}}\approx\frac{M_0^3}{3A}=8.617\times10^{-17}\left(\frac{M_0}{\mathrm{kg}}\right)^3\mathrm s.\qquad[0.5]
$$
- For $M_0=10\,M_\odot$,
$$
\boxed{\tau_{\mathrm{bh},10\odot}\approx6.8\times10^{77}\ \mathrm s}.\qquad[1.0]
$$

#### (T12.1e)

As the CMB will redshift away eventually, there will be no CMB radiation for the BH to absorb at large $t$. It will only decrease in mass and increase in temperature. So only the figures which show the temperature of the black hole increasing eventually ($t\to\infty$) are relevant. The derivatives at the origin (now) should distinguish the three cases.

- (X) If $T_{\mathrm{bh}}^{\mathrm{now}}>T_{\mathrm{cmb}}^{\mathrm{now}}$, then the BH is losing more energy now than it is gaining by CMB absorption, and its temperature should increase due to decreasing mass. $(dT/dt)_{\mathrm{now}}>0$.
- (Y) If $T_{\mathrm{bh}}^{\mathrm{now}}=T_{\mathrm{cmb}}^{\mathrm{now}}$, the temperature will be in equilibrium for some time, i.e. $(dT/dt)_{\mathrm{now}}=0$, before the CMB temperature redshifts to smaller values and $(dT/dt)_\infty>0$.
- (Z) For $T_{\mathrm{bh}}^{\mathrm{now}}<T_{\mathrm{cmb}}^{\mathrm{now}}$, initially the BH will gain mass (and hence cool down) by absorbing from CMB compared to what it loses by evaporation. So $(dT/dt)_{\mathrm{now}}<0$. But eventually, $(dT/dt)_\infty>0$.

### Part 2: Primordial black holes

#### (T12.2a)

- We want $\tau_{\mathrm{PBH}}=14\times10^9\times3.156\times10^7\ \mathrm s=4.418\times10^{17}\ \mathrm s$. [1.0]
- But we have found in (T12.1d) that $\tau_{\mathrm{bh}}=8.617\times10^{-17}(M_0/\mathrm{kg})^3\ \mathrm s$.

This gives
$$
M_{0,\mathrm{PBH}}^3=\left(\frac{\tau_{\mathrm{PBH}}}{8.617\times10^{-17}}\right)\mathrm{kg^3}\implies\boxed{M_{0,\mathrm{PBH}}\simeq1.7\times10^{11}\ \mathrm{kg}}.\qquad[1.0]
$$
- Since $R_{\mathrm{SC}}\propto M_{\mathrm{bh}}$, and we know $R_{\mathrm{SC},\odot}=2952$ m,
$$
R_{\mathrm{SC},\mathrm{PBH}}\simeq R_{\mathrm{SC},\odot}\left(\frac{M_{0,\mathrm{PBH}}}{M_\odot}\right)\implies\boxed{R_{\mathrm{SC},\mathrm{PBH}}=2.6\times10^{-16}\ \mathrm m}.\qquad[1.0]
$$
- Since $T_{\mathrm{bh}}\propto1/M_{\mathrm{bh}}$ and we know $T_{\mathrm{bh},10\odot}$ from (T12.1b),
$$
T_{\mathrm{PBH}}\simeq T_{\mathrm{bh},10\odot}\left(\frac{10M_\odot}{M_{0,\mathrm{PBH}}}\right)\implies\boxed{T_{\mathrm{PBH}}=7.1\times10^{11}\ \mathrm K}.\qquad[1.0]
$$

#### (T12.2b)

- The size of horizon $r_H=ct$. So the total mass contained within the horizon volume is
$$
M=\frac1{c^2}\rho\left(\frac{4\pi}3\right)\left(\frac{ct}2\right)^3.\qquad[2.0]
$$
- With $a(t)\sim t^{1/2}$ and $a(t)\sim1/T$, one gets $t\sim T^{-2}$. [2.0]
- With $\rho\sim T^4\sim t^{-2}$, the mass of PBH formed at time $t$ would be $M_{0,\mathrm{PBH}}\propto t$. [1.0]
- The age of the Universe at which PBH of $10^{12}$ kg forms is $t_{12}=10^{-23}$ s.
- Hence,
$$
t_{20}=t_{12}\times\left(\frac{10^{20}\ \mathrm{kg}}{10^{12}\ \mathrm{kg}}\right)\implies\boxed{t_{20}=t_{12}\times10^8=10^{-15}\ \mathrm s}.\qquad[1.0]
$$

#### (T12.2c)

- Lifetime of the PBH with $M_{0,\mathrm{PBH}}=10^{10}$ kg will be
$$
\tau_{\mathrm{PBH}}=8.617\times10^{-17}\cdot(10^{10})^3\ \mathrm s=8.617\times10^{13}\ \mathrm s.\qquad[0.5]
$$
- As given in (T12.1b), $R_{\mathrm{SC},\odot}=2952$ m. Using $R_{\mathrm{SC}}\propto M$, we get the peak wavelength of radiation emitted by this $10^{10}$ kg PBH, at the time of emission, as
$$
\lambda_0\simeq16R_{\mathrm{SC}}=16\cdot\frac{10^{10}\ \mathrm{kg}}{M_\odot}\cdot R_{\mathrm{SC},\odot}=2.4\times10^{-16}\ \mathrm m.\qquad[1.5]
$$
- Redshifted wavelength received at earth at present time $t_0$ is obtained from
$$
\lambda_{\mathrm{earth}}=\lambda_0\frac{a(t_0)}{a(\tau_{\mathrm{PBH}})}.\qquad[1.0]
$$
- With $a(t)\sim t^{2/3}$, we get
$$
\lambda_{\mathrm{earth}}=\lambda_0\left(\frac{t_{\mathrm{now}}}{\tau_{\mathrm{PBH}}}\right)^{2/3}=2.4\times10^{-16}\ \mathrm m\left(\frac{4.418\times10^{17}\ \mathrm s}{8.617\times10^{13}\ \mathrm s}\right)^{2/3}.\qquad[1.0]
$$
$$
\boxed{\lambda_{\mathrm{earth}}=7.1\times10^{-14}\ \mathrm m}.\qquad[1.0]
$$

#### (T12.2d)

- Using the expression for $T_{\mathrm{bh}}$ found in (T12.1b), the energy of a typical photon emitted at time $t$ for a PBH is
$$
E_{\mathrm{emitted}}(t)=k_BT_{\mathrm{bh}}(t)=\frac{bc^2k_B}{32GM_{\mathrm{bh}}(t)}.\qquad[1.0]
$$
- From (T12.1c), we know
$$
M_0^3=3\frac{\pi b^4\sigma c^2}{16^4G^2}t+M_{\mathrm{bh}}(t)^3=3At+\left(\frac{bc^2k_B}{32GE_{\mathrm{emitted}}(t)}\right)^3.\qquad[1.0]
$$
- The value of $E_{\mathrm{det}}$ can be different from $E_{\mathrm{emitted}}$ due to redshift. Hence, the smallest possible value of $E_{\mathrm{emitted}}(t)$ is $E_{\mathrm{det}}=3.0\times10^{20}$ eV. This gives the maximum value of the second term as
$$
\left(\frac{bc^2k_B}{32GE_{\mathrm{emitted}}(t)}\right)^3_{\max}=4.3\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{kg^3}.\qquad[1.0]
$$
- Given the value of $A=3.868\times10^{15}\ \mathrm{kg^3\,s^{-1}}$, the second term can always be neglected for $t\gtrsim10^{-19}$ s. This is true in this problem. So $M_0\approx(3At)^{1/3}$. [1.0]
- **The largest possible** $M_0$ would be obtained when $t$ is as large as possible, i.e., $t=t_{\mathrm{now}}$. This will correspond to a BH radiating in its last stages at present in the nearby region. [1.0]
- Then $M_0^{\max}=(3At_{\mathrm{now}})^{1/3}=(5.13\times10^{33}\ \mathrm{kg^3})^{1/3}$. [0.5]
- Thus, $\boxed{M_0^{\max}=1.7\times10^{11}\ \mathrm{kg}}$. [0.5]
- Note that this was already found in (T12.2a), so the calculation above is not needed.
- **The smallest** $M_0$ will correspond to a BH that has evaporated as early as possible. Such a BH would have evaporated far away from Earth, and its radiation would be redshifted.
- The smallest time of evaporation would correspond to the largest redshift factor, and hence the largest $E_{\mathrm{emitted}}(t)$, which is $E_{\mathrm{emitted}}(t)=k_BT_{\mathrm{Planck}}$. [1.0]
- The ratio of the scale factors at the time of the above PBH evaporation and now is
$$
\frac{a(t)}{a(t_{\mathrm{now}})}=\frac{E_{\mathrm{det}}}{k_BT_{\mathrm{Planck}}}=\frac{3.0\times10^{20}\ \mathrm{eV}}{10^{19}\ \mathrm{GeV}}=3.0\times10^{-8}.
$$
With $a(t_{\mathrm{now}})=1.0$, we have $a(t)=3.0\times10^{-8}$. [1.0]
- Using the plot of $a(t)$ vs $t$ from the figure, $t\simeq10^4$ s. Note that for this $t$, the second term in the original $M_0^3$ expression is still negligible. [1.0]
- Therefore, $\boxed{M_0^{\min}\approx(3At)^{1/3}\approx4.9\times10^6\ \mathrm{kg}}$. [1.0]
