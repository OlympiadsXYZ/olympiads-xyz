**Elements of an elliptic orbit**:

| | |
|---|---|
| $a = \mathbf{OA}\ (= \mathbf{OP})$ | semi-major axis |
| $b = \mathbf{OB}\ (= \mathbf{OC})$ | semi-minor axis |
| $e = \sqrt{1 - \frac{b^2}{a^2}}$ | eccentricity |
| F | focus |
| P | periapsis (point nearest to F) |
| A | apoapsis (point furthest from F) |

**Keplers Third Law**: For an elliptical orbit, the square of the period, $T$, of orbit of an object about the focus is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis, $a$ (the average of the minimum and maximum distances from the Sun). The constant of proportionality is $4\pi^2/GM$, where $M$ is the mass of the central object and $G$ is the universal gravitational constant.

**Magnitudes**: The apparent magnitudes of two objects, $m_1$ and $m_0$, are related to their apparent brightnesses, $b_1$ and $b_0$, via the formula:

$$
\frac{b_1}{b_0} = 10^{-0.4(m_1 - m_0)}
$$

## Условие

On 21st August 2017 the continental United States experienced a total solar eclipse. Dubbed the ‘Great American Eclipse’, it was estimated to be one of the most watched eclipses in history.

The path of totality (where the Moon completely obscures the Sun) is shown in Figure 3, and the point of greatest eclipse (“GE”; where the path was widest since the axis of the cone of the Moon’s shadow passed closest to the centre of the Earth) was near the village of Cerulean, Kentucky. The following data can be used for this question:

- The angular radii of the Sun and the Moon (if observed from the centre of the Earth) at the moment of GE are $15'48.7''$ and $16'03.4''$, respectively, where the notation $xx'yy.y''$ corresponds to $xx$ arcminutes and $yy.y$ arcseconds (60 arcminutes = 1 degree, and 60 arcseconds = 1 arcminute)
- The latitude and longitude of the location of GE are $36^{\circ}58.0'$N and $87^{\circ}40.3'$W, respectively
- Take the mean radii of the Sun, Earth and Moon to be respectively $R_{\odot} = 695\,700$ km, $R_{\oplus} = 6371$ km and $R_{\mathrm{Moon}} = 1737$ km, and a day to be 24 hours
- Take the semi-major axes of the Sun-Earth and Earth-Moon systems to be $149\,600\,000$ km and $384\,400$ km, respectively
- As viewed from a location far above the North Pole, the Moon orbits in an anticlockwise direction around the Earth, and the Earth spins in an anticlockwise direction

For an ellipse with semi-major axis $a$ it can be shown that the velocity $v$, at a distance $r$ from mass $M$, can be written as:

$$
v^2 = GM\left(\frac{2}{r} - \frac{1}{a}\right)
$$

**a.** Calculate the width of the path of totality at GE (in km). You may use the approximation that the Moon is directly overhead so that the shadow is circular, and small enough that the curvature of the Earth can be neglected. **[6 т.]**

**b.** Show that at this point the shadow is moving across the Earth’s surface at approximately $0.68\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. You may use the simplifying approximation that for short time intervals it can be considered as travelling with a constant latitude, and that the apparent movement of the Sun due to the Earth’s orbit can be neglected. **[5 т.]**

**c.** Hence calculate the duration of the eclipse. Give your answer to the nearest 0.1 s. **[1 т.]**

The point of greatest eclipse and greatest duration do not generally coincide, as a more elliptical shadow with a major axis aligned with the path of maximum totality (and thinner path width, equal to the minor axis) can compensate for the shadow moving faster at higher latitudes. For this eclipse the point of greatest duration (“GD”) was at co-ordinates of $37^{\circ}35'$N latitude and $89^{\circ}07'$W longitude, reached about 4 minutes before GE, and where totality lasted 0.1 s longer than the value calculated in part c.

**d. (i)** d. The town of Carbondale, Illinois, is the closest big town to the point of GD, with co-ordinates $37^{\circ}44'$N latitude and $89^{\circ}13'$W longitude. Assuming the path of maximum totality can be treated as linear as it passes through the region around GD and GE:

(i) Calculate the co-ordinates of the closest point (“CP”) to Carbondale on the path of maximum totality. **[5 т.]**

**d. (ii)** Calculate the distance (in km) between Carbondale and CP. **[2 т.]**

**d. (iii)** Hence calculate (to the nearest 0.1 s) how much shorter totality was for residents in Carbondale compared with CP. Take the duration at CP to be the same as GD, the width of the path to be 0.5 km less than for GE (so the Moon’s shadow is elliptical), and the speed of the Moon’s shadow to have only been affected by the change in latitude. **[6 т.]**

## Решение

<Spoiler title="Покажи официалното решение">

**Q3 - Great American Eclipse** **[Total = 25]**

**a.**

Suitable diagram [1]

<figure className="problem-figure problem-figure--sized" style={{'--fig-w': '91%', '--fig-max': '583px'}}>
<img src="https://pub-43290baaaff14857b5dd59610ea438c7.r2.dev/problems/baao-2018-x/p3-sol-fig1.png" alt="Geometry of the umbra: a large circle (Sun) with radius R_Sun on the left, a small circle (Moon) with radius R_M and a medium circle (Earth) with radius R_E on the right. Two tangent lines from the Sun’s limbs past the Moon converge to a point beyond the Earth, making angle α with the axis; the red segment s_umbra marks the shadow width at the Earth. Distances d_Sun (Sun to Earth centre), d_Moon (Moon to Earth centre) and d_umbra (Moon to cone apex) are marked with arrows." width="1822" height="724" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
</figure>

From the apparent angular radii we can calculate the distance to the Moon and the Sun:

Moon: $16'03.4'' = 4.67 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{rad} \quad \therefore d_{\mathrm{Moon}} = \frac{R_{\mathrm{Moon}}}{\theta} = \frac{1737}{4.67 \times 10^{-3}} = 371\,900\ \mathrm{km}$ [1]

Sun: $15'48.7'' = 4.60 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{rad} \quad \therefore d_{\mathrm{Sun}} = \frac{R_{\mathrm{Sun}}}{\theta} = \frac{695700}{4.60 \times 10^{-3}} = 151\,258\,000\ \mathrm{km}$ [1]

Angle of shadow: $\alpha = \sin^{-1}\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{Sun}} - R_{\mathrm{Moon}}}{d_{\mathrm{Sun}} - d_{\mathrm{Moon}}}\right) = 4.60 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{rad} \quad (= 0.2635^{\circ})$ [1]

Shadow cone length: $d_{\mathrm{umbra}} = \frac{R_{\mathrm{Moon}}}{\sin\alpha} = 377\,670\ \mathrm{km}$ [1]

Full width of shadow: $s_{\mathrm{umbra}} = 2\left(d_{\mathrm{umbra}} - (d_{\mathrm{Moon}} - R_{\mathrm{Earth}})\right)\tan\alpha = 111.7\ \mathrm{km}$ [1] **[6]**

[Allow s$_{\mathrm{umbra}}$ = 55.9 km if it is clear that the student recognises that's the radius of the circular shadow, otherwise they lose one mark. Allow use of small angle approximations throughout or other slight variations in the geometry resulting in a width within a few km of the correct answer]

**b.**

At GE, the latitude is $\theta = 36^{\circ}58' = 36.967^{\circ}$

$v_{\mathrm{rot}} = \frac{2\pi R_E \cos\theta}{T} = \frac{2\pi \times 6371 \times \cos 36.967}{24 \times 60 \times 60}$ [1]

$= 0.370\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ [1]

$v^2 = GM\left(\frac{2}{r} - \frac{1}{a}\right) \quad \therefore v_{\mathrm{Moon}} = \sqrt{GM_E\left(\frac{2}{d_{\mathrm{Moon}}} - \frac{1}{a}\right)}$

$= \sqrt{6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 5.97 \times 10^{24}\left(\frac{2}{371900 \times 10^{3}} - \frac{1}{384400 \times 10^{3}}\right)}$ [1]

$= 1.051\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ [1]

Hence: $v_{\mathrm{rel}} = v_{\mathrm{Moon}} - v_{\mathrm{rot}} = 1.051 - 0.370 = 0.681\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ [1] **[5]**

[Since this is a 'show that' question, ensure the student has shown evidence that they have calculated the correct relative velocity (rather than simply quoted it) for the final mark]

**c.**

$t_{\mathrm{eclipse}} = \frac{s_{\mathrm{umbra}}}{v_{\mathrm{rel}}} = \frac{111.74}{0.681} = 164.0\ \mathrm{s} \quad (= 2\ \mathrm{mins}\ 44.0\ \mathrm{secs})$ [1] **[1]**

[Answer must be to the nearest 0.1 s for the mark]

Despite many of the simplifying assumptions made, this is within a few seconds of the measured value of 2 mins 41.6 secs or the official predicted value of 2 mins 40.3 secs

**d. (i)**

Converting the co-ordinates of GE and GD:

GE: $x_1 = 87.67^{\circ}, y_1 = 36.97^{\circ}$ $\quad$ GD: $x_2 = 89.12^{\circ}, y_2 = 37.58^{\circ}$

Equation of a straight line is $y = mx + c$, so:

$m = \frac{x_2 - x_1}{y_2 - y_1} = 0.427$ [1]

$c = y_1 - m x_1 = -0.448$ [1]

[Allow reversed signs of m and c so long as internally consistent]

Equation of the line passing through Cardondale (with co-ordinates x$_{\mathrm{C}}$, y$_{\mathrm{C}}$) and perpendicular to the initial line at CP (with co-ordinates x$_{\mathrm{CP}}$, y$_{\mathrm{CP}}$) must satisfy

$m x_{CP} + c = -\frac{1}{m}(x_{CP} - x_C) + y_C$ [1]

$\therefore x_{CP} = \frac{x_C + m y_C - mc}{m^2 + 1} = 89.26^{\circ} \quad (= 89^{\circ}15.3')$ [1]

$y_{CP} = m x_{CP} + c = 37.64^{\circ} \quad (= 37^{\circ}38.6')$ [1] **[5]**

**d. (ii)**

We can calculate the angular distance, utilising Pythagoras' theorem since they are very close together:

$\theta_{\mathrm{dist}} = \sqrt{(x_{CP} - x_C)^2 + (y_{CP} - y_C)^2} = 0.0987^{\circ} = 0.0017\ \mathrm{rad}$ [1]

Using the small angle approximation (and ignoring the surface curvature on this scale):

$d = \theta_{\mathrm{dist}} \times R_E = 0.0017 \times 6371 = 11.0\ \mathrm{km}$ [1] **[2]**

**d. (iii)**

At CP: $t_{\mathrm{eclipse,CP}} = 164.1\ \mathrm{s} \quad s_{\mathrm{umbra,CP}} = s_{\mathrm{umbra}} - 0.5 = 111.24\ \mathrm{km}$

$v_{\mathrm{Moon}} = 1.051\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ (same as before)

$v_{\mathrm{rot,CP}} = \frac{2\pi R_E \cos(y_{CP})}{T} = 0.367\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}} \quad \therefore v_{\mathrm{rel,CP}} = 0.684\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ [1]

Eclipse shadow is now elliptical with:

Minor axis $= s_{\mathrm{umbra,CP}} = 111.24\ \mathrm{km}$

Major axis $= t_{\mathrm{eclipse,CP}} \times v_{\mathrm{rel,CP}} = 112.36\ \mathrm{km}$ [1]

For an ellipse, $x = a\cos\theta$ and $y = b\sin\theta$

<figure className="problem-figure problem-figure--sized" style={{'--fig-w': '36.8%', '--fig-max': '236px'}}>
<img src="https://pub-43290baaaff14857b5dd59610ea438c7.r2.dev/problems/baao-2018-x/p3-sol-fig2.png" alt="Ellipse with dashed horizontal and vertical axes through the centre; semi-axes labelled a (horizontal) and b (vertical); a radius line drawn at angle θ to the horizontal axis meets the ellipse at a point whose projections are labelled x (on the horizontal axis) and y (on the vertical axis)." width="738" height="594" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
</figure>

Hence if $y = 11.0\ \mathrm{km}$ and $b = \frac{111.24}{2} \quad \Rightarrow \theta = 11.38^{\circ} \quad \therefore x = 55.07\ \mathrm{km}$ [2]

[First mark is for an appropriate diagram and use of the equation of an ellipse]

So the length of the shadow over Carbondale $= 2x = 110.1\ \mathrm{km}$ [1]

$\therefore t_{\mathrm{eclipse,C}} = \frac{110.1}{0.684} = 160.9\ \mathrm{s}$

$\therefore \Delta t = 164.1 - 160.9 = 3.2\ \mathrm{s}$ [1] **[6]**

[Answer must be to the nearest 0.1 s for the mark]

Carbondale is lucky enough that it will also be on the path of totality of the next eclipse to cross the mainland of the USA on 8th April 2024!
