**Keplers Third Law**: For an elliptical orbit, the square of the period, $T$, of orbit of an object about the focus is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis, $a$ (the average of the minimum and maximum distances from the Sun). ## Условие

All stars lose mass during their lifetimes due to two main routes: particles escaping their surface (referred to as the stellar wind), and the mass defect of the nuclear reactions occurring in their cores.

**a. (i)** a. Given that, at the distance of the Earth, the proton flux from the Sun’s stellar wind (which is assumed to be radiated equally in all directions) is $3.0 \times 10^{12}\ \mathrm{m^{-2}\,s^{-1}}$, and that the luminosity of the Sun is solely due to the fusion of hydrogen to helium:

(i) Show that the rate at which the Sun is losing mass, $\dot{M} \equiv \frac{\mathrm{d}M}{\mathrm{d}t}$, due to its stellar wind is $\sim 10^{-14}\,M_{\odot}\ \mathrm{yr^{-1}}$. [Take the mass of a proton to be $1.67 \times 10^{-27}$ kg.] **[2 т.]**

**a. (ii)** Show that $\dot{M}$ due to nuclear fusion is greater than that from the solar wind. **[2 т.]**

**a. (iii)** Estimate how much the Earth-Sun distance and the Earth’s orbital period will have changed after the Sun has lost mass (via both routes) for one year. Assume the orbit remains circular throughout and ignore gravitational effects from all other bodies. **[5 т.]**

In practice, the mass loss rate can vary quite considerably during a star’s lifetime, particularly once it has left the main sequence when the stellar wind can become much more substantial. Wolf-Rayet stars are massive stars near the end of their lives, presumed to be the in the stage just before a supernova, and are losing substantial amounts of mass due to very fast stellar winds. This deposits considerable energy into the surrounding interstellar medium (ISM) and can sweep up material into a thin bubble around the star, visible as a type of planetary nebula.

**b. (i)** b. If every photon leaving a star is able to transfer all of its momentum (given by $p_{\mathrm{photon}} = \frac{E_{\mathrm{photon}}}{c}$) to drive a stellar wind (known as the single scattering limit):

(i) Show that the maximum mass loss rate for a star can be written in terms of the luminosity of the star, $L$, the (terminal) velocity of the stellar wind far from the star, $v_{\infty}$, and the speed of light, $c$, as

$$
\dot{M}_{\mathrm{max}} = \frac{L}{v_{\infty} c}.
$$
**[3 т.]**

**b. (ii)** Hence, derive an expression for the maximum kinetic energy deposited per second by the stellar wind as a function of the luminosity of the star. Comment on your answer. **[2 т.]**

**c. (i)** c. The Wolf-Rayet star WR7 is in the constellation of Canis Major and its strong winds are responsible for the nebula known as Thor’s Helmet (see Figure 1). The star has a mass of $16\,M_{\odot}$, a radius of $1.41\,R_{\odot}$, and a surface temperature of $112\,000$ K, with a measured $v_{\infty}$ of $1545\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$.

(i) Predict the mass loss rate of WR7 based upon the properties of the star, assuming the single scattering limit. Give your answer in units of $M_{\odot}\ \mathrm{yr^{-1}}$. **[3 т.]**

**c. (ii)** The nebula is 5 arcmins in diameter ($1^{\circ} = 60$ arcmin) and 4.8 kpc away, and at its edge is a bright thin shell of swept up material expanding at a rate of $30\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. The age of such a nebula, $t$, is related to the current values of radius, $R$, and expansion speed, $v$, by $t = 0.55R/v$. Using this model, determine the age of the nebula. **[3 т.]**

**c. (iii)** If the expansion is purely driven by the direct impact of the stellar winds, then the radius at time $t$, $R(t)$, can be related to $\dot{M}$ with the formula

$$
R(t) = \left(\frac{3\dot{M} v_{\infty} t^2}{2\pi n_0 m_{\mathrm{H}}}\right)^{1/4}
$$

where $n_0$ is the ambient hydrogen number density in the nebula. If $n_0 = 16\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ and $m_{\mathrm{H}} = 1.67 \times 10^{-27}$ kg, calculate the observed mass loss rate based upon the properties of the nebula. Compare it with the predicted one from earlier and comment on your answer. **[3 т.]**

**c. (iv)** Using your new value for $\dot{M}$, calculate the total mass expelled from the star and hence the total kinetic energy the stellar wind has so far deposited into the ISM during this stage of the star’s life. **[2 т.]**

## Решение

<Spoiler title="Покажи официалното решение">

**Q1 - Stellar Mass Loss** **[Total = 25]**

**a. (i)**

$\dot{M}_{\mathrm{wind}} =$ mass flux $\times$ surface area of sphere 1 au in radius

$= (3.0 \times 10^{12} \times 1.67 \times 10^{-27}) \times (4\pi \times (1.50 \times 10^{11})^2)$ [1]

$= 1.42 \times 10^{9}\ \mathrm{kg\,s^{-1}}$

$= 2.24 \times 10^{-14}\ M_{\odot}\ \mathrm{yr^{-1}}$ (so $\sim 10^{-14}\ M_{\odot}\ \mathrm{yr^{-1}}$) [1] **[2]**

**a. (ii)**

$L = \dot{M}_{\mathrm{fusion}} c^2 \quad \therefore \quad \dot{M}_{\mathrm{fusion}} = L_{\odot}/c^2 = \frac{3.85 \times 10^{26}}{(3.00 \times 10^{8})^2}$ [1]

$= 4.28 \times 10^{9}\ \mathrm{kg\,s^{-1}}$ [1] **[2]**

$(= 6.78 \times 10^{-14}\ M_{\odot}\ \mathrm{yr^{-1}})$ [$so > \dot{M}_{\mathrm{wind}}$]

**a. (iii)**

$\dot{M}_{\mathrm{tot}} = \dot{M}_{\mathrm{wind}} + \dot{M}_{\mathrm{fusion}} = 9.02 \times 10^{-14}\ M_{\odot}\ \mathrm{yr^{-1}}$ [1]

In solar units, Kepler's 3rd Law is $M = a^3 / T^2$, and since the change is mass is very small compared with $M_{\odot}$ we can make the approximation $\frac{\Delta a}{a} = \frac{\Delta T}{T} = \frac{\Delta M}{M}$ [2]

So: $\Delta a = \frac{\Delta M}{M_{\odot}} a = 9.02 \times 10^{-14} \times 1.50 \times 10^{11} = 1.35 \times 10^{-2}\ \mathrm{m}$ [1]

$\Delta T = \frac{\Delta M}{M_{\odot}} T = 9.02 \times 10^{-14} \times (365.25 \times 24 \times 3600)$

$= 2.85 \times 10^{-6}\ \mathrm{s}$ [1] **[5]**

[Allow angular momentum conservation arguments too, as well as ΔT being 3 times larger]

**b. (i)**

$p_{\mathrm{max}} = m v_{\infty} \quad \therefore \quad \frac{p_{\mathrm{max}}}{t} = \dot{M}_{\mathrm{max}} v_{\infty}$ [1]

In the single scattering limit: $p_{\mathrm{max}} = p_{\mathrm{photon}} = \frac{E}{c} \quad \therefore \frac{E}{ct} = \dot{M}_{\mathrm{max}} v_{\infty}$ [1]

(since L = E/t) $\quad \therefore \frac{L}{c} = \dot{M}_{\mathrm{max}} v_{\infty}$ [1] **[3]**

$\left(\therefore \dot{M}_{\mathrm{max}} = \frac{L}{v_{\infty} c}\right)$

**b. (ii)**

$\dot{E}_{\mathrm{K,max}} = \frac{1}{2} \dot{M}_{max} v_{\infty}^2 = \frac{1}{2} \frac{L}{v_{\infty} c} v_{\infty}^2 = \frac{1}{2} \frac{v_{\infty}}{c} L$ [1] **[1]**

Since $v_{\infty} \ll c$, this is $\ll L$ (so photons carry away much more energy) [1] **[1]**

[Also allow that as $v_{\infty} \to c$, $E_{\mathrm{K,max}} \to$ ½L]

**c. (i)**

$L = 4\pi R^2 \sigma T^4$

$= 4\pi \times (1.41 \times 6.96 \times 10^{8})^2 \times 5.67 \times 10^{-8} \times (112000)^4$ [1]

$= 1.08 \times 10^{32}\ \mathrm{W} \quad (= 2.80 \times 10^{5}\ L_{\odot})$ [1]

$\dot{M}_{\mathrm{max}} = \frac{L}{v_{\infty} c} = \frac{1.08 \times 10^{32}}{1545 \times 10^{3} \times 3.00 \times 10^{8}} = 2.33 \times 10^{17}\ \mathrm{kg\,s^{-1}}$

$= 3.69 \times 10^{-6}\ M_{\odot}\ \mathrm{yr^{-1}}$ [1] **[3]**

[Award 2 max if they forget to convert the velocity into m s$^{-1}$]

**c. (ii)**

Radius = (2.5 arcmin in radians) × (distance in metres) (we're given the diameter)

$= \left(\frac{2.5}{60} \times \frac{\pi}{180}\right) \times (4.8 \times 10^{3} \times 3.09 \times 10^{16})$ [1]

$= 1.08 \times 10^{17}\ \mathrm{m} \quad (= 3.49\ \mathrm{pc})$ [1]

$t = \frac{0.55R}{v} = \frac{0.55 \times 1.08 \times 10^{17}}{30 \times 10^{3}} = 1.98 \times 10^{12}\ \mathrm{s} \quad (\approx 63000\ \mathrm{years})$ [1] **[3]**

[Award 2 max if they forget to convert the velocity into m s$^{-1}$]

**c. (iii)**

$R(t) = \left(\frac{3\dot{M} v_{\infty} t^2}{2\pi n_0 m_{\mathrm{H}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}} \therefore \dot{M} = \frac{2\pi n_0 m_{\mathrm{H}} R(t)^4}{3 v_{\infty} t^2}$

$= \frac{2\pi \times 16 \times 10^{6} \times 1.67 \times 10^{-27} \times (1.08 \times 10^{17})^4}{3 \times 1545 \times 10^{3} \times (1.98 \times 10^{12})^2}$ [1]

$= 1.25 \times 10^{18}\ \mathrm{kg\,s^{-1}} \quad (= 1.99 \times 10^{-5}\ M_{\odot}\ \mathrm{yr^{-1}})$ [1] **[2]**

This is (about 5.4 times) larger than the previously predicted value, suggesting the single scattering limit is not a good approximation in this situation [1] **[1]**

[In practice, multiple scattering occurs due to the Doppler Effect in the winds]

**c. (iv)**

Total mass, $M_{\mathrm{tot}} = \dot{M} t = 1.25 \times 10^{18} \times 1.98 \times 10^{12}$

$= 2.48 \times 10^{30}\ \mathrm{kg} \quad (= 1.25\ M_{\odot})$ [1] **[1]**

Total $E_{\mathrm{K,tot}} = \frac{1}{2} M_{\mathrm{tot}} v_{\infty}^2 = \frac{1}{2} \times 2.48 \times 10^{30} \times (1545 \times 10^{3})^2$

$= 2.96 \times 10^{42}\ \mathrm{J}$ [1] **[1]**

[This means Wolf Rayet stars are depositing a very large amount of kinetic energy into the ISM in only a few tens of thousands of years (in this case about 2.5% of the total energy the Sun will radiate in its lifetime!)]
