## Условие

GW170817 was the first gravitational wave event arising from a binary neutron star merger to have been detected by the LIGO & Virgo experiments, and careful localization of the source meant that the electromagnetic counterpart was quickly found in galaxy NGC 4993 (see Figure 2). Such a combination of two completely separate branches of astronomical observation begins a new era of ‘multi-messenger astronomy’. Since the gravitational waves allow an independent measurement of the distance to the host galaxy and the light allows an independent measurement of the recessional speed, this observation allows us to determine a new, independent value of the Hubble constant $H_0$.

**a.** The galaxy NGC 4993 is measured to have a redshift of $z = 0.00980 \pm 0.00079$. Assuming it follows Hubble’s Law, $v = H_0 d$, where $H_0 = 73.24 \pm 1.74$ km s$^{-1}$ Mpc$^{-1}$, as determined by Hubble Space Telescope (HST) measurements of Cepheid variables, calculate the distance to the galaxy (in Mpc) and its (absolute) uncertainty. Give your distance to an appropriate number of significant figures. **[5 т.]**

Another way of measuring distances to galaxies is to use the Fundamental Plane (FP) relation, which relies on the assumption there is a fairly tight relation between radius, surface brightness, and velocity dispersion for bulge-dominated galaxies, and is widely used for galaxies like NGC 4993. It can be described by the relation

$$
\log\left(\frac{D}{(1+z)^2}\right) = -\log R_e + \alpha \log \sigma - \beta \log \langle I_r \rangle_e + \gamma
$$

where $D$ is the distance in Mpc, $R_e$ is the effective radius measured in arcseconds, $\sigma$ is the velocity dispersion in $\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, $\langle I_r \rangle_e$ is the mean intensity inside the effective radius measured in $L_{\odot}$ pc$^{-2}$, and $\gamma$ is the distance-dependent zero point of the relation. Calibrating the zero point to the Leo I galaxy group, the constants in the FP relation become $\alpha = 1.24$, $\beta = 0.82$, and $\gamma = 2.194$.

**b.** For NGC 4993 we measure $R_e = 15.5$ arcseconds, $\sigma = 171\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, and $\langle I_r \rangle_e = 407\,L_{\odot}$ pc$^{-2}$. Given that the scatter in the FP relation introduces an uncertainty in $D$ of $\pm 17\%$, calculate the distance to the galaxy (in Mpc) and its (absolute) uncertainty using the FP relation. **[3 т.]**

By measuring the amplitude (called strain, $h$) and the frequency of the gravitational waves, $f_{\mathrm{GW}}$, one can determine the distance to the source without having to rely on ‘standard candles’ like Cepheid variables or Type Ia supernovae. For two masses, $m_1$ and $m_2$, orbiting the centre of mass with separation $a$ with orbital angular velocity $\omega$, then the dimensionless strain parameter $h$ is

$$
h \simeq \frac{G}{c^4} \frac{1}{r} \mu a^2 \omega^2
$$

where $r$ is the luminosity distance, $c$ is the speed of light, $\mu = m_1 m_2 / M_{\mathrm{tot}}$ is the reduced mass and $M_{\mathrm{tot}} = m_1 + m_2$ is the total mass.

**c.** Given that the gravitational frequency, $f_{\mathrm{GW}}$, is twice the orbital frequency (i.e. $f_{\mathrm{GW}} = \omega/\pi$) and the ‘chirp mass’, $\mathcal{M} = (\mu^3 M_{\mathrm{tot}}^2)^{1/5}$, express $h$ in terms of only $\mathcal{M}$, $r$, $f_{\mathrm{GW}}$, and various fundamental constants. **[5 т.]**

The rate of change of frequency of the gravitational waves (called the ‘chirp’) from a merging binary can be written as

$$
\dot{f}_{\mathrm{GW}} = \frac{96}{5} \pi^{8/3} \left(\frac{G\mathcal{M}}{c^3}\right)^{5/3} f_{\mathrm{GW}}^{11/3}
$$

**d.** Combine your result from c. with the above equation to cancel out $\mathcal{M}$ and so express the distance to the gravitational wave source, $r$, as a function of fundamental constants and the measurables $h$, $\dot{f}_{\mathrm{GW}}$, and $f_{\mathrm{GW}}$ only. **[3 т.]**

**e.** Typically, you measure $\tau \equiv f_{\mathrm{GW}}/\dot{f}_{\mathrm{GW}}$, rather than $\dot{f}_{\mathrm{GW}}$ directly. Given that just as the merger began the detectors measured $\tau = 0.0023$ s, $f_{\mathrm{GW}} = 300$ Hz and $h = 6.0 \times 10^{-21}$, estimate the distance to GW170817 (in Mpc) and its absolute uncertainty (assuming a percentage uncertainty of $\pm 10\%$). How does this compare with your answers in parts a. and b.? **[4 т.]**

**f.** Using the redshift information from NGC 4993 and the gravitational wave distance you have just calculated, determine the Hubble constant $H_0$ in units of km s$^{-1}$ Mpc$^{-1}$, along with its absolute uncertainty. Is this value consistent with the one derived by the HST using Cepheid variables (given in part a.)? **[5 т.]**

## Отговори

<Spoiler title="Покажи отговорите">

- **a.** d = 40.1 ± 4.2 Mpc
- **b.** D = 43.8 ± 7.4 Mpc
- **c.** $h = \frac{(G\mathcal{M})^{5/3}}{c^4} \frac{1}{r} \pi^{2/3} f_{\mathrm{GW}}^{2/3}$
- **d.** $r = \frac{5}{96} \frac{c}{h\pi^2} \frac{\dot{f}_{\mathrm{GW}}}{f_{\mathrm{GW}}^3}$
- **e.** r = 41 ± 4 Mpc (41.3 Mpc), consistent with d and D within uncertainties
- **f.** H0 = 71 ± 13 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹ (71.3), consistent with the HST value

</Spoiler>

## Решение

<Spoiler title="Покажи официалното решение">

**Q2 - Multi-Messenger Astronomy** **[Total = 25]**

**a.**

$v = H_0 d \quad \therefore \quad d = \frac{v}{H_0} = \frac{cz}{H_0} = \frac{3.00 \times 10^{5} \times 0.00980}{73.24}$ (c must be in km s$^{-1}$) [1]

$= 40.1\ \mathrm{Mpc}$ [1]

Answer given to 3 s.f. as redshift has lowest precision (penalise more s.f.) [1] **[3]**

Uncertainty: $\frac{\Delta d}{d} = \frac{\Delta z}{z} + \frac{\Delta H_0}{H_0} = \frac{0.00079}{0.00980} + \frac{1.74}{73.24} (= 10.4\%)$ [1]

$\therefore \Delta d = 4.2\ \mathrm{Mpc}$ [1] **[2]**

Overall: $d = 40.1 \pm 4.2\ \mathrm{Mpc}$

[Allow uncertainties added in quadrature to give 8.4%, leading to Δd = 3.4 Mpc. Also, this is the only part of the question with a s.f. penalty]

**b.**

$D = \left(10^{-\log R_e + 1.24 \log \sigma - 0.82 \log \langle I_r \rangle_e + 2.194}\right)(1+z)^2$

$= \left(10^{-\log 15.5 + 1.24 \log 171 - 0.82 \log 407 + 2.194}\right)(1 + 0.00980)^2$ [1]

$= 43.8\ \mathrm{Mpc}$ [1] **[2]**

$\Delta D = 17\%$ of $43.8\ \mathrm{Mpc} = 7.4\ \mathrm{Mpc}$ [1] **[1]**

Overall: $D = 43.8 \pm 7.4\ \mathrm{Mpc}$

[Allow the uncertainty in z to be propagated too, giving ΔD = 14.5 Mpc or 9.0 Mpc (in quadrature)]

**c.**

Using Kepler's 3rd Law: $G M_{\mathrm{tot}} = a^3 \omega^2 \quad \therefore \quad a = \left(\frac{G M_{\mathrm{tot}}}{\omega^2}\right)^{1/3}$ [1]

Using the definition of chirp mass: $\mathcal{M} = (\mu^3 M_{\mathrm{tot}}^2)^{\frac{1}{5}} \quad \therefore \quad \mu = \left(\frac{\mathcal{M}^5}{M_{\mathrm{tot}}^2}\right)^{1/3}$ [1]

Putting these into the given equation: $h = \frac{G}{c^4} \frac{1}{r} \mu a^2 \omega^2$

$= \frac{G}{c^4} \frac{1}{r} \left(\frac{\mathcal{M}^5}{M_{\mathrm{tot}}^2}\right)^{1/3} \left(\frac{G M_{\mathrm{tot}}}{\omega^2}\right)^{2/3} \omega^2$ [1]

$= \frac{G^{5/3}}{c^4} \frac{1}{r} \mathcal{M}^{5/3} \omega^{2/3}$ [1]

Finally, using that $\omega = \pi f_{\mathrm{GW}}$: $\quad h = \frac{(G\mathcal{M})^{5/3}}{c^4} \frac{1}{r} \pi^{2/3} f_{\mathrm{GW}}^{2/3}$ [1] **[5]**

**d.**

Given that $\dot{f}_{\mathrm{GW}} = \frac{96}{5} \pi^{8/3} \left(\frac{G\mathcal{M}}{c^3}\right)^{5/3} f_{\mathrm{GW}}^{11/3} \quad \therefore \quad (G\mathcal{M})^{5/3} = \frac{5}{96} \frac{c^5 \dot{f}_{\mathrm{GW}}}{\pi^{8/3} f_{\mathrm{GW}}^{11/3}}$ [1]

Using our result from part c. $\quad (G\mathcal{M})^{5/3} = \frac{h r c^4}{\pi^{2/3} f_{\mathrm{GW}}^{2/3}}$ [1]

Combining these together: $\frac{5}{96} \frac{c^5 \dot{f}_{\mathrm{GW}}}{\pi^{8/3} f_{\mathrm{GW}}^{11/3}} = \frac{h r c^4}{\pi^{2/3} f_{\mathrm{GW}}^{2/3}} \quad \therefore \quad r = \frac{5}{96} \frac{c}{h\pi^2} \frac{\dot{f}_{\mathrm{GW}}}{f_{\mathrm{GW}}^3}$ [1] **[3]**

**e.**

$r = \frac{5}{96} \frac{c}{h\pi^2} \frac{1}{\tau f_{\mathrm{GW}}^2} = \frac{5}{96} \times \frac{3.00 \times 10^{8}}{6.63 \times 10^{-34} \times \pi^2} \times \frac{1}{0.0023 \times 300}$ [1]

$= 1.27 \times 10^{24}\ \mathrm{m} = 41.3\ \mathrm{Mpc}$ [1] **[2]**

$\Delta r = 10\%$ of $41.3\ \mathrm{Mpc} = 4.1\ \mathrm{Mpc}$ [1] **[1]**

Overall: $r = 41 \pm 4\ \mathrm{Mpc}$

The values of d, D and r are consistent with each other within their uncertainties [1] **[1]**

**f.**

$v = H_0 r \quad \therefore \quad H_0 = \frac{v}{r} = \frac{cz}{r} = \frac{3.00 \times 10^{5} \times 0.00980}{41.3}$ (c must be in km s$^{-1}$) [1]

$= 71.3\ \mathrm{km\ s^{-1}\ Mpc^{-1}}$ [1] **[2]**

Uncertainty: $\frac{\Delta H_0}{H_0} = \frac{\Delta z}{z} + \frac{\Delta r}{r} = \frac{0.00079}{0.00980} + 0.1 \quad (= 18.1\%)$ [1]

$\therefore \Delta H_0 = 12.9\ \mathrm{km\ s^{-1}\ Mpc^{-1}}$ [1] **[2]**

Overall: $H_0 = 71 \pm 13\ \mathrm{km\ s^{-1}\ Mpc^{-1}}$

The value of H$_0$ derived from multi-messenger astronomy **is consistent** with the value derived by the HST using Cepheid variables [1] **[1]**

[Allow uncertainties added in quadrature, giving ΔH$_0$ = 9.2 km s$^{-1}$ Mpc$^{-1}$]


</Spoiler>
