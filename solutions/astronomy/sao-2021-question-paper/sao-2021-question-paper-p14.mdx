**Eclipses**

On 26 December 2019, we observed an *annular eclipse* in Singapore. In this question, we concern ourselves with the dynamics of a solar eclipse occurring on the **vernal equinox**. The Earth-Sun distance on this day is $1.4897 \times 10^{11}m$ and the Earth-Moon distance is $3.5792 \times 10^8m$.

$$

**A1**

$$
\theta_S = 2\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{R_S}{d_S}\right) = 0.536^{\circ}
$$
(accepted range $0.535^{\circ} - 0.536^{\circ}$)

**A1**

**1b**

**M2 (correct geometry)** **M1 (similar triangles)**

$$
\frac{x}{R_{sun}} = \frac{x - d_{sun} + d_{moon}}{R_{moon}} \to x = \frac{(d_{sun} - d_{moon})}{(1 - \frac{R_{moon}}{R_{sun}})} = 1.4898 \times 10^{11}m
$$

**M2 (correct manipulation)**

$$
r_{umbra} = (x - d_{sun} + R_{earth}) \times \frac{R_{sun}}{x}
$$

$$
= 95.397\mathrm{km}
$$

$$
= 95.4\mathrm{km}
$$

### Part 2: Eclipse Dynamics

**1c**

We may invoke a form of kepler’s second law:

$$
\frac{dA}{dt} = \frac{1}{2}r^2\frac{d\theta}{dt} = \frac{\pi ab}{T}
$$

**M3**

$$
\frac{1}{2}r^2\frac{d\theta}{dt} = \frac{\pi a^2\sqrt{1 - e^2}}{T}
$$

**M1**

$$
\left(\frac{d\theta}{dt}\right)_{moon} = 3.065 \times 10^{-6}\mathrm{rad/s}
$$

**A1**

$$
\left(\frac{d\theta}{dt}\right)_{Sun} = 2.008 \times 10^{-7}\mathrm{rad/s}
$$

**A1**

**1d**

Essentially, what is important here is to calculate the velocity of the moon’s shadow on the surface of the Earth. The previous parts of the question were meant to guide the readers to the following formulation:

$$
v_{E,t} = d_{sun} \times \left(\frac{d\theta}{dt}\right)_{sun} = 2.991 \times 10^4m/s
$$

**M1**

$$
v_{M,t} = d_M \times \left(\frac{d\theta}{dt}\right)_{moon} = 1097m/s
$$

**M1**

$$
v_{shadow} = (v_{E,t} - v_{M,t})\frac{d_E}{d_E - d_M} - v_{E,t} = -1027m/s
$$

**M3**

Another solution that students may provide could be the following, which is a valid approximation – however, it is important to be careful when considering the direction of the shadow’s velocity.

$$
v_{shadow} = \left(-\left(\frac{d\theta}{dt}\right)_{sun} + \left(\frac{d\theta}{dt}\right)_{moon}\right) \times d_{moon} = 1025m/s
$$

**M3**

It is also important to take into the account the Earth’s rotational velocity at the surface $v_{ES} = \omega_E R_E = 463m/s$

Note that this velocity is at angle of $23^{\circ}26'$ to the velocity of the shadow (the ecliptic makes that angle with the equator), and their relative velocities summed through use of the cosine rule **M1**

$$
v = \sqrt{v_S^2 + v_{ES}^2 - 2v_Sv_{ES}\cos 23^{\circ}26'} = 630\mathrm{m/s}
$$

$$
T = \frac{2r}{v} = 303s
$$

(accepted range: $301 - 305$s)

**M1** **M1** **A1**

### Part 4: The Terminator

**1f**

Given the set-up in the question, we are hinted to plot a latitude vs longitude graph, where the line in the graph symbolises the **points on Earth that experience Sunset/Sunrise**. Hence, we should turn our attention to finding an expression that gives us every pair of latitude and longitude points that experience sunset/sunrise.

To do that, we need to employ more celestial geometry. We will want to find out how many hours after noon will sunset/sunrise occur, given a specific latitude. **M1**

(Figure) **M1**

From the figure, we can find a relation between the Hour Angle (HA), latitude $\phi$ and the declination of the sun $\delta$. Remember that $\delta$ is negative, and $\phi$ is negative for the southern hemisphere. **M1**

We can conjure up a spherical cosine law to get the relation.

$$
\cos 90^{\circ} = \cos\bar{\delta}\cos\bar{\phi} + \sin\bar{\delta}\sin\bar{\phi}\cos HA
$$

**M1**

We can simplify it to:

$$
0 = \sin\delta\sin\phi + \cos\delta\cos\phi\cos HA
$$

Hence, we get:

$$
HA = \cos^{-1}(-\tan\phi\tan\delta)
$$

**M1**

Looking at this, we may get stuck, but we need to remind ourselves the importance of the hour angle expression we have gotten. The $HA$ in question represents the number of hours that will have pass before sunset occurs. **M1** Putting a spin to that by bringing in the idea of time zones, it tells us which place that is an $\frac{HA}{15}$ number of hours ahead of Greenwich will be experiencing sunset. **M1**

So, for example at the Equator, $HA = 90^{\circ}$ which translates to 6 hours, and so a place that is 6 hours ahead of Greenwich at the Equator will be experiencing Sunset! This is basically how time zones work.

Using time zones, we can then realise that the $HA$ we calculated is just the longitude coordinate we are looking for. Coincidentally, we have found the expression, we just need to put $\phi$ in terms of $HA$.

$$
\phi = \tan^{-1}\left[-\frac{\cos HA}{\tan\delta}\right]
$$

**M1**

Finally, we need to translate the graph to the right, since the expression above is for local noon at Greenwich, but we require it when it is local noon in Singapore. Luckily, this tweak is a simple one. **M1**

$$
\phi = \tan^{-1}\left[-\frac{\cos(HA - \theta)}{\tan\delta}\right]
$$

Where $\theta = 103.82^{\circ}$, the longitude of Singapore. **A1**
