The Sirius star, located in the constellation of Canis Major, is the brightest star in the night sky of the Earth. What the observer's eye sees as a single star is actually a binary star system.  
The high brightness of Sirius is a consequence of two facts: its intrinsic luminosity and its proximity to the Earth.  
The Mizar multiple star system, in the constellation of Ursa Major, consists of 4 stars seen along the same line of sight from the Earth. Some of these stars form a gravitationally bound system.  
Let's assume that an observer (observer A) is located on one of the planets of the Sirius system.

Determine:

<ProblemSection id="a">

### a)

The magnitude of the Sun as seen by observer A ($m_{\text{Sun,Planet}}$).

</ProblemSection>

<ProblemSection id="b">

### b)

The magnitude of Sirius star system as seen by the observer A ($m_{\text{SY,Planet}}$).

</ProblemSection>

<ProblemSection id="c">

### c)

The combined intrinsic luminosity of the Mizar system, $L_{\text{Mizar}}$.

</ProblemSection>

<ProblemSection id="d">

### d)

The average distance between gravitationally bound stars of the Mizar system and Earth.

</ProblemSection>

<ProblemSection id="e">

### e)

The geocentric angular distance between Mizar system and Sirius, $\Delta\theta$.

</ProblemSection>

<ProblemSection id="f">

### f)

The physical distance between the gravitationally bound stars of the Mizar system and the observer A ($d_{\text{Mizar,Planet}}$).

</ProblemSection>

<ProblemSection id="g">

### g)

The magnitude of the entire Mizar system as seen by the observer A ($m_{\text{Mizar,Planet}}$).

Also estimate amount of errors in all your answers.

</ProblemSection>

### Data

- $d_{\text{Sirius,Earth}}=2.6\,\mathrm{pc}$ – the Sirius–Earth distance;

## Решение

<Warning title="Непълно решение">
The available preliminary Problem 3B key uses seven Big Dipper stars, whereas the final photographed problem asks about four Mizar-system stars and uncertainties. No matching final key for c, d, f, g or the error estimates was found. The compatible Sun calculation and explicitly conditional earlier Sirius calculation are reproduced; the earlier angular calculation is identified as a source discrepancy.
</Warning>

<Spoiler title="Покажи официалното решение">

This is a partial recovery from the preliminary Problem 3B solution, not a complete official solution to the final Mizar version. The changed stellar table is not interchangeable with the final one.

<ProblemSection id="solution-a">

### a) Sun — compatible calculation

$$
\log\frac{E_{\text{Sun,Earth}}}{E_{\text{Sun,Planet}}}=-0.4(m_{\text{Sun,Earth}}-m_{\text{Sun,Planet}});
$$
$$
E_{\text{Sun,Earth}}=\frac{L_{\text{Sun}}}{4\pi d_{\text{Sun,Earth}}^2};\qquad E_{\text{Sun,Planet}}=\frac{L_{\text{Sun}}}{4\pi d_{\text{Sun,Planet}}^2}.
$$

The key uses $d_{\text{Sun,Earth}}=1\,\mathrm{AU}$, $d_{\text{Sun,Planet}}=2.6\,\mathrm{pc}=2.6\cdot206265\,\mathrm{AU}$ and $m_{\text{Sun,Earth}}=-26.78^{\mathrm m}$, and obtains

$$
m_{\text{Sun,Planet}}=1.87^{\mathrm m}.
$$

</ProblemSection>

<ProblemSection id="solution-b-variant">

### b) Earlier Sirius calculation — different supplied data

The preliminary key assumes $L_{\text{Sirius}}=25L_{\text{Sun}}$, which is not supplied in the final problem. With $d_{\text{Sirius,Planet}}=10\,\mathrm{AU}$, it writes

$$
\log\frac{E_{\text{Sirius,Planet}}}{E_{\text{Sun,Planet}}}=-0.4(m_{\text{Sirius,Planet}}-m_{\text{Sun,Planet}}),
$$

and obtains $m_{\text{Sirius,Planet}}=-25.255^{\mathrm m}$ using $m_{\text{Sun,Planet}}=1.87^{\mathrm m}$.

This is the earlier key's result under its extra luminosity assumption. The final question instead supplies the apparent Sirius magnitude measured from Earth; the value above is not presented as a verified answer for that changed data.

</ProblemSection>

<ProblemSection id="solution-e-variant">

### e) Earlier angular calculation — printed discrepancy

The earlier key uses the same printed coordinate pair, but calls the first star Big Dipper rather than Mizar. It applies

$$
\cos a=\cos b\cos c+\sin b\sin c\cos A,\qquad A=90^\circ,
$$

then sets $a=\Delta\theta$, $b=\Delta\alpha$, $c=\Delta\delta$ and writes

$$
\cos\Delta\theta=\cos\Delta\alpha\cos\Delta\delta;
$$
$$
\Delta\alpha=6^{\mathrm h}38^{\mathrm{min}}55.5^{\mathrm s}\approx100^\circ;
$$
$$
\Delta\delta=71^\circ38'31''\approx72.4^\circ;
$$
$$
\cos\Delta\theta=\cos100^\circ\cos72.4^\circ=(-0.173648177)(0.30236989)=-0.05250598;
$$
$$
\Delta\theta\approx90.3^\circ\approx90^\circ.
$$

**Source note:** these are the printed earlier calculation and approximations, which are inconsistent. They are retained as a comparison with the archived key, not as a corrected or validated solution of the final question. Its seven-star luminosity, distance and magnitude calculations do not answer the final four-star Mizar problem and have not been imported here.

</ProblemSection>


</Spoiler>
