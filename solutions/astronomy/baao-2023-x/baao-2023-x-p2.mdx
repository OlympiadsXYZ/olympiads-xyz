**Elements of an elliptic orbit**:

| | |
|---|---|
| $a = \mathbf{OA}\ (= \mathbf{OP})$ | semi-major axis |
| $b = \mathbf{OB}\ (= \mathbf{OC})$ | semi-minor axis |
| $e = \sqrt{1 - \frac{b^2}{a^2}}$ | eccentricity |
| $\mathbf{F}$ | focus |
| $\mathbf{PF} = a(1-e)$ | periapsis distance (shortest distance from $\mathbf{F}$) |
| $\mathbf{AF} = a(1+e)$ | apoapsis distance (longest distance from $\mathbf{F}$) |

**Kepler’s Third Law**: For an elliptical orbit, the square of the period, $T$, of an object about the focus is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis, $a$ (as defined above), such that

$$
T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{GM}a^3\,,
$$

where $M$ is the total mass of the system (typically dominated by the central object) and $G$ is the universal gravitational constant.

**Vis-Viva Equation**: For an elliptical orbit, the speed $v$ of an object at a distance $r$ from the focus is related to the semi-major axis, $a$, total mass of the system, $M$, and universal gravitational constant, $G$, (as defined above), such that

$$
v^2 = GM\left(\frac{2}{r} - \frac{1}{a}\right)\,.
$$

**Magnitudes**: The apparent magnitudes of two objects, $m_1$ and $m_0$, are related to their apparent brightnesses, $b_1$ and $b_0$, via the formula

$$
\frac{b_1}{b_0} = 10^{-0.4(m_1 - m_0)}\,.
$$

The absolute magnitude of an object, $\mathcal{M}$, is the same as its apparent magnitude when viewed from 10 pc, hence the relationship between apparent and absolute magnitude and distance is

$$
m - \mathcal{M} = 5\log\left(\frac{d}{10}\right),
$$

where $d$ is measured in parsecs.

## Условие

Wolf-Rayet (WR) stars are some of the hottest stars known, with very strong stellar winds causing considerable mass to the be lost to the interstellar medium (ISM). In binary systems between a WR star and a very large O or B spectral class star, where their strong stellar winds collide can create the conditions for the formation of dust which goes on to enrich the ISM.

The WR140 system consists of a WR and an O star which produce dust very regularly when the two stars are close together, around periastron. They are in a highly elliptical orbit ($e = 0.8993$) with a period of $2895$ days. Once far from the stars, these dust shells move through space at a remarkably constant speed as indicated by the regularity of the shells in the recent image taken with the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST), shown above in Figure 3. **a. (i)** Take the distance to the system to be $1.64$ kpc.

By taking measurements from Figure 3, show that the average radial expansion velocity between shells 2 to 17 is $\sim 2600$ km s$^{-1}$. [1 arcsecond $= 1''$ is a measurement of angle, where $3600'' = 1^{\circ}$.] **[3 т.]**

**a. (ii)** Shell 1 was observed by JWST on 27th July 2022 to be $1.63''$ away from the central stars. It was formed during the last periastron passage of the O star, which (as viewed from Earth) took place in December 2016. Taking light travel time into account, in what year was the periastron passage responsible for shell 17? **[3 т.]**

The average radial expansion velocity of shell 1 is less than that seen for shells 2 to 17, suggesting a mechanism accelerates the dust up to that speed from some lower speed resultant from the bow shock between the two colliding stellar winds. A prime candidate is radiation pressure, where photons deposit all of their momentum onto an optically thick dust cloud, before it moves too far from the original stars and becomes optically thin (so the transfer of momentum becomes less and less efficient) as shown in the right panel of Figure 5.

<figcaption>Figure 5: *Left:* The collision of stellar winds between the WR and O star creates a conical shock front in which the conditions are right for dust production. The radiation force, $F_{\mathrm{rad}}$, experienced by a single dust grain at a distance $r$ is given as

$$
F_{\mathrm{rad}} = \sigma_g\frac{L_{\mathrm{bin}}}{4\pi r^2 c}\,,
$$

where $\sigma_g$ is the cross-sectional area of the dust grain, $c$ is the speed of light and $L_{\mathrm{bin}}$ is the combined luminosity of the binary system. Han et al. (2022) suggest the following acceleration model to match the observations:

$$
a(t) = \begin{cases} 0 & 0 \le r < r_{inner} \\ a_{\max} & r_{inner} \le r < r_{outer} \\ \frac{a_{\max}r_{outer}^2}{r^2} & r \ge r_{outer} \end{cases}
$$

Here, $r_{inner}$ is the inner radius of the optically thick dust shell and $r_{outer}$ is the distance at which it becomes optically thin. $a_{\max}$ is the constant acceleration experienced by the dust whilst optically thick, and is equal to the acceleration of a dust grain by radiation pressure at $r = r_{outer}$.

**b. (i)** In the Han et al. (2022) model they use $r_{inner} = 50$ au, $r_{outer} = 220$ au, and assume spherical dust grains with a radius of $40$ nm and density $1.6$ g cm$^{-3}$, as well as a dust-to-gas ratio of $0.019$. Take the apparent magnitude of the system (after correcting for substantial extinction by the dust shells and the general ISM) as $m = 0.426$, and you are given the absolute magnitude of the Sun is $\mathcal{M}_{\odot} = 4.74$.

Show that $a_{\max}$ is $\sim 940$ km s$^{-1}$ yr$^{-1}$. **[5 т.]**

**b. (ii)** Hence, sketch $a(r)$ from $0 \le r \le 2500$ au. **[2 т.]**

**b. (iii)** Given shell 1 is measured to be moving at $2540$ km s$^{-1}$, calculate the initial speed of the gas streaming away from the stars in the conical shock front, $v_0$, before it experiences the acceleration due to radiation pressure. **[5 т.]**

The stellar winds from each star are initially thermally accelerated up to a terminal velocity, $v_{\infty}$, following the relationship $v(r) = v_{\infty}(1 - R_{\star}/r)$, where $R_{\star}$ is the radius of the star. The shape of the conical shock front is well approximated as a hyperbolic cross-section so long as the speed of each star’s wind at the shock front is $\approx v_{\infty}$ where they meet. This is true for much of the orbit and the cone, however due to the extreme eccentricity of the WR140 system at periapsis it may not hold close to the stagnation point (the closest the cone gets to the WR star, as seen in Figure 5).

**c. (i)** The measured semi-major axis of the WR140 system from radio interferometry is $8.82$ milliarcseconds. Investigation of spectral lines shows that for the O star $v_{\infty,O} = 3100$ km s$^{-1}$ and for the WR star $v_{\infty,WR} = 2800$ km s$^{-1}$, and measurements of X-ray absorption lead to suggested mass loss rates of $\dot{M}_O = 3.7\times10^{-7}\,M_{\odot}\ \mathrm{yr}^{-1}$ and $\dot{M}_{WR} = 1.7\times10^{-5}\,M_{\odot}\ \mathrm{yr}^{-1}$ respectively for each star. In the shock front, the pressure from each stellar wind is balanced. Considering the system at periapsis:

Show that the distance from the O star to the stagnation point is

$$
r_O = \frac{\sqrt{\eta}}{1+\sqrt{\eta}}r_{peri}\,,
$$

where $\eta$ is the ratio of the momenta of the two stellar winds, $\eta \equiv \frac{p_O}{p_{WR}}$ and $r_{peri}$ is the separation between the stars at periapsis. **[2 т.]**

**c. (ii)** Hence, calculate $r_O$ in au. **[4 т.]**

**c. (iii)** Taking the WR star to be approximately 2 times more luminous than the O star, and the effective surface temperatures of the stars to be $T_{\mathrm{eff,O}} = 35$ kK and $T_{\mathrm{eff,WR}} = 60$ kK, calculate the radius of each star in solar units. **[3 т.]**

**c. (iv)** Hence, determine for which star(s) the assumption of $v \approx v_{\infty}$ at the stagnation point is reasonable, and suggest (if any) the change in the position of the stagnation point taking in the real values of $v$ at $r_O$. **[3 т.]**

**d.** The conditions for dust production turn on when the stars get within $\approx 7.9$ au of each other, corresponding to the solid red region in the right panel of Figure 4. A line from the WR star to the start of dust production region makes an angle of $45^{\circ}$ with the semi-major axis. By using Kepler’s second law, or otherwise, estimate the rough duration of dust production during each orbit. Give your answer in years to 2 decimal places, making any simplifying assumptions clear. [Hint: Kepler’s second law states that the area swept out by one object orbiting another is equal in the same amount of time, such that $\mathrm{d}A/\mathrm{d}t = \mathrm{constant} = \pi ab/T$ where $T$ is the period] **[10 т.]**

$$

[1]

[This formula could also be arrived at by generalising the SUVAT equation $v^2 = u^2 + 2as$ into $v^2 = u^2 + 2\int a(s)\,\mathrm{d}s$]

In a. ii we were told the angular separation of shell 1 from the star, so

$$
r_{shell1} = 1.64\times\left(\frac{1.63}{3600}\times\frac{2\pi}{360}\right) = 1.30\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{kpc} = 4.00\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{m}
$$
[1]

[This corresponds to 2670 au. If they use $r_{\mathrm{shell1}}$ = 2500 au in the subsequent integration lose only this mark (so get max 4 marks)]

Evaluating the integral with everything in SI units

$$
\int_{r_{inner}}^{r_{shell1}}a(r)\,\mathrm{d}r = \int_{r_{inner}}^{r_{outer}}a_{max}\,\mathrm{d}r + \int_{r_{outer}}^{r_{shell1}}\frac{a_{max}r_{outer}^2}{r^2}\,\mathrm{d}r
$$

$$
= [a_{max}\times(r_{outer}-r_{inner})] + \left[-a_{max}r_{outer}^2\left(\frac{1}{r_{shell1}^2}-\frac{1}{r_{outer}^2}\right)\right]
$$
[1]

$$
= [2.99\times10^{-2}\times(220-50)\times1.50\times10^{11}]
$$

$$
+[-2.99\times10^{-2}\times(220\times1.50\times10^{11})^2\times\left(\frac{1}{(4.00\times10^{14})^2}-\frac{1}{(220\times1.50\times10^{11})^2}\right)
$$

$$
= 7.61\times10^{11} + 9.04\times10^{11}
$$

$$
= 1.67\times10^{12}\ \mathrm{m^2\,s^{-2}}
$$
[1]

[0.5 marks for either correctly evaluated integral (flat section or curved section)]

We can now evaluate $v_0$

$$
\therefore v_0 = \sqrt{v_{shell1}^2 - 2\int_{r_{inner}}^{r_{shell1}}a(r)\,\mathrm{d}r} = \sqrt{(2540\times10^3)^2 - 2\times1.67\times10^{12}}
$$

$$
= 1.77\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} = \boxed{1770\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}}
$$
[1] **[5]**

[Accept answer in m s$^{-1}$ or km s$^{-1}$ for final mark. Allow ecf from their $a_{\max}$]

**c. (i)**

</figure>

The ram pressure is $p_{ram} = \frac{F}{A} = \frac{\dot{p}}{A}$ where $\dot{p}$ is the rate of change of momentum, so at the stagnation point (assuming spherically symmetric stellar winds)

$$
\frac{\dot{p}_O}{4\pi r_O^2} = \frac{\dot{p}_{WR}}{4\pi(r_{peri}-r_O)^2}
$$
[1]

$$
\therefore \frac{\dot{p}_O}{\dot{p}_{WR}} = \frac{r_O^2}{(r_{peri}-r_O)^2}
$$

But since $\eta = \frac{p_O}{p_{WR}} = \frac{\dot{p}_O}{\dot{p}_{WR}}$ then $\eta = \frac{r_O^2}{(r_{peri}-r_O)^2} \therefore \sqrt{\eta} = \frac{r_O}{r_{peri}-r_O}$ [1] **[2]**

This can then be rearranged to give the required formula

$$
\sqrt{\eta}(r_{peri}-r_O) = r_O \therefore \sqrt{\eta}r_{peri} = (1+\sqrt{\eta})r_O \therefore r_O = \frac{\sqrt{\eta}}{1+\sqrt{\eta}}r_{peri}
$$

[The first mark is for setting up the problem algebraically **or** for a helpful diagram. The second mark is for correctly introducing $\eta$ **and** having convincing algebra to the final answer (since it was given, no standalone mark for the final expression)]

**c. (ii)**

First, we can calculate $\eta$

$$
\eta = \frac{p_O}{p_{WR}} = \frac{\dot{p}_O}{\dot{p}_{WR}} = \frac{\dot{M}_Ov_{\infty,O}}{\dot{M}_{WR}v_{\infty,WR}} = \frac{3.7\times10^{-7}\times3100}{1.7\times10^{-5}\times2800} = 0.024
$$
[1]

We can also calculate the semi-major axis, and then use the given eccentricity to get the periapsis distance

$$
a = 1.64\times\left(\frac{8.82\times10^{-3}}{3600}\times\frac{2\pi}{360}\right) = 7.01\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{kpc} = 14.44\ \mathrm{au}
$$
[1]

$$
r_{peri} = a(1-e) = 14.44\times(1-0.8993) = 1.45\ \mathrm{au}
$$
[1]

Putting it all into the formula of the previous part

$$
r_O = \frac{\sqrt{\eta}}{1+\sqrt{\eta}}r_{peri} = \frac{\sqrt{0.024}}{1+\sqrt{0.024}}\times1.45 = \boxed{0.195\ \mathrm{au}}
$$
[1] **[4]**

[Must be in au for the final mark, although a and $r_{\mathrm{peri}}$ might be given in metres ($2.17\times10^{12}$ m and $2.18\times10^{11}$ m respectively)]

**c. (iii)**

We can split up the luminosity calculated earlier in b. i.

$$
L_{WR} = \frac{2}{3}L_{bin} = \frac{2}{3}\times1.43\times10^{6} = 9.53\times10^{5}\,L_{\odot} = 3.67\times10^{32}\ \mathrm{W}
$$
[0.5]

$$
L_O = \frac{1}{3}L_{bin} = \frac{1}{3}\times1.43\times10^{6} = 4.77\times10^{5}\,L_{\odot} = 1.83\times10^{32}\ \mathrm{W}
$$
[0.5]

Using the Stephan-Boltzmann law

$$
R_{WR} = \sqrt{\frac{L_{WR}}{4\pi\sigma T_{WR}^4}} = \sqrt{\frac{3.67\times10^{32}}{4\pi\times5.67\times10^{8}\times(60\times10^3)^4}} = 6.30\times10^{9}\ \mathrm{m} = \boxed{9.06\,R_{\odot}}
$$
[1] **[1.5]**

$$
R_O = \sqrt{\frac{L_O}{4\pi\sigma T_O^4}} = \sqrt{\frac{1.83\times10^{32}}{4\pi\times5.67\times10^{8}\times(35\times10^3)^4}} = 1.31\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{m} = \boxed{18.82\,R_{\odot}}
$$
[1] **[1.5]**

[Score max 2 out of 3 if their final answer is not in $R_{\odot}$.

Give full credit if they assume $T_{\odot} \approx 5780\ \mathrm{K}$ and so use $R = \sqrt{\frac{L/L_{\odot}}{(T/T_{\odot})^4}}R_{\odot}$ for calculations]

[This matches up well with the scale of the stars in Figure 4 in the paper, showing these are both large, very hot stars.]

Using the given formula:

v_{r_O} = v_{\infty,O}\left(1-\frac{R_O}{r_O}\right) = \left(1-\frac{1.31\times10^{10}}{0.195\times1.50\times10^{11}}\right)v_{\infty,O} = 0.55v_{\infty,O}
$$

v_{r_{WR}} = v_{\infty,WR}\left(1-\frac{R_{WR}}{r_{peri}-r_O}\right) = \left(1-\frac{6.30\times10^{9}}{(1.45-0.195)\times1.50\times10^{11}}\right)v_{\infty,WR} = 0.97v_{\infty,WR}

**d.**

From Kepler’s 2nd law, $\frac{\text{area of shaded section}}{\text{total area of the ellipse}} = \frac{\text{time spent in shaded section}}{\text{period of the orbit}}$ [1]

We can use approximate geometry to calculate this area e.g. breaking the non-shaded section up into a triangle (ABS) and trapezium (ABDC) sat within a half-ellipse: [1]

<figure className="problem-figure problem-figure--sized" style={{'--fig-w': '83.3%', '--fig-max': '533px'}}>
<img src="https://pub-43290baaaff14857b5dd59610ea438c7.r2.dev/problems/baao-2023-x/p2-sol-fig5.png" alt="The same ellipse with the focus S on the dashed major axis. A red trapezium ABDC has vertical side AB and vertical side CD at the centre of the ellipse (C on top, D at the bottom); its width is marked h with a red double-headed arrow." width="1667" height="885" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
</figure>

Given the symmetry, angle $\angle ASB = 2\times45^{\circ} = 90^{\circ}$

$$
\therefore \text{area of triangle } ABS = \frac{1}{2}\times7.9^2 = 31.2\ \mathrm{au}^2
$$
[1]

We can also use Pythagoras’ theorem to get the length AB

$$
AB = \sqrt{7.9^2+7.9^2} = 11.2\ \mathrm{au}
$$
[1]

The height of the trapezium

$$
h = a - r_{peri} - 7.9\cos45^{\circ} = 14.44 - 1.45 - 5.59 = 7.41\ \mathrm{au}
$$
[1]

The length of CD is the minor axis (= 2b), which can be calculated from rearranging the formula given at the beginning of the paper

$$
e = \sqrt{1-\frac{b^2}{a^2}} \therefore b = a\sqrt{1-e^2} = 14.44\times\sqrt{1-0.8993^2} = 6.3\ \mathrm{au}
$$
[1]

Hence, the area of the trapezium

$$
\text{area } ABDC = \frac{1}{2}(AB+CD)h = \frac{1}{2}(11.2+(2\times6.3))\times7.41 = 88.2\ \mathrm{au}^2
$$
[1]

Finally, the area of the half-ellipse (to the left of CD)

$$
\text{area half-ellipse} = \frac{1}{2}\pi ab = \frac{1}{2}\pi\times14.44\times6.3 = 143.4\ \mathrm{au}^2
$$
[1]

The area of the shaded section is therefore

$$
\text{shaded area} = \text{area of half-ellipse} - (\text{area ABS} + \text{area ABDC})
$$

$$
= 143.4 - (31.2+88.2) = 24.0\ \mathrm{au}^2
$$
[1]

Finally, applying Kepler’s 2nd law

$$
t = \frac{\text{shaded area}}{\text{total area}}\times T = \frac{24.0}{2\times143.4}\times2895 = 0.084\times2895 = 242\ \text{days} = \boxed{0.66\ \text{yrs}}
$$
[1] **[10]**

[Must be in years and to 2 d.p. for the final mark. A wide variety of approaches are possible, so award marks based upon sensible choices of shapes to evaluate the area of. The first mark is for realising how to apply Kepler’s 2nd law in this context, the second mark is for a clear approach to break the ellipse up into shapes (ideally with a diagram – and not necessarily the triangle and trapezium used here), and the final mark is for the final answer – all other marks are available for sensible areas and lengths relevant to their method. Accept any method that gives a value of 0.60-0.70 years so long as the justification is clear]

[The more exact way to work this out is using our knowledge of anomalies, although some approximations were made to simplify the geometric case in terms of the given length and angle. Assuming the angle to be correct, leads to a true anomaly $\nu = 180^{\circ} - 45^{\circ} = 135^{\circ}$. Given this and the quoted eccentricity and semi-major axis, the real length AS is 7.6 au, and utilising Kepler’s equation to get the time for $-135^{\circ} \le \nu \le 135^{\circ}$ gives 0.633 years. Alternatively, assuming the length AS = 7.9 au to be correct, leads to a real true anomaly of 136.3°, and when looking at the time to travel from $-136.3^{\circ} \le \nu \le 136.3^{\circ}$ gives 0.671 years. Students using this approach (rather than areas of shapes) get full credit. However the problem is approached, it is clear that dust is only produced on a relatively short time frame, which is why each shell has such contrast.]
