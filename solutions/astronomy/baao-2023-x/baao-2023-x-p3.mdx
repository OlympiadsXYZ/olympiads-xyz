**Elements of an elliptic orbit**:

| | |
|---|---|
| $a = \mathbf{OA}\ (= \mathbf{OP})$ | semi-major axis |
| $b = \mathbf{OB}\ (= \mathbf{OC})$ | semi-minor axis |
| $e = \sqrt{1 - \frac{b^2}{a^2}}$ | eccentricity |
| $\mathbf{F}$ | focus |
| $\mathbf{PF} = a(1-e)$ | periapsis distance (shortest distance from $\mathbf{F}$) |
| $\mathbf{AF} = a(1+e)$ | apoapsis distance (longest distance from $\mathbf{F}$) |

**Kepler’s Third Law**: For an elliptical orbit, the square of the period, $T$, of an object about the focus is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis, $a$ (as defined above), such that

$$
T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{GM}a^3\,,
$$

where $M$ is the total mass of the system (typically dominated by the central object) and $G$ is the universal gravitational constant.

**Vis-Viva Equation**: For an elliptical orbit, the speed $v$ of an object at a distance $r$ from the focus is related to the semi-major axis, $a$, total mass of the system, $M$, and universal gravitational constant, $G$, (as defined above), such that

$$
v^2 = GM\left(\frac{2}{r} - \frac{1}{a}\right)\,.
$$

**Magnitudes**: The apparent magnitudes of two objects, $m_1$ and $m_0$, are related to their apparent brightnesses, $b_1$ and $b_0$, via the formula

$$
\frac{b_1}{b_0} = 10^{-0.4(m_1 - m_0)}\,.
$$

The absolute magnitude of an object, $\mathcal{M}$, is the same as its apparent magnitude when viewed from 10 pc, hence the relationship between apparent and absolute magnitude and distance is

$$
m - \mathcal{M} = 5\log\left(\frac{d}{10}\right),
$$

where $d$ is measured in parsecs.

## Условие

Young, Earth-like planets interact with the protoplanetary discs in which they form, and as a result migrate to different orbital radii. The aim of this question is to quantify this migration in a simple model. We shall think of protoplanetary discs as consisting of nested circular orbits of gas and dust around a central star. For a thin disc (with small vertical extent), we assign to the disc a surface density (or mass per unit area) $\Sigma$, and semi-thickness $H$, which in general vary over the disc’s extent. The disc’s ‘aspect ratio’ at radius $r$ from the central star is denoted $h = H/r$. This question is concerned with the migration of ‘small’ planets, such that $M_p/M_{\star} = q \ll h^3$.

**a.** Consider a planet of mass $M_p$ in a circular orbit of radius $r_p$ about a star of mass $M_{\star}$, with $M_p/M_{\star} = q \ll 1$. Show that it has angular velocity and angular momentum about the central star given by:

$$
\Omega_p = \sqrt{\frac{GM_{\star}}{r_p^3}}, \quad L = M_p\sqrt{GM_{\star}r_p}
$$
**[2 т.]**

**b.** Consider now a rotationally symmetric disc with surface density profile $\Sigma = \Sigma_0\,(r/r_0)^{-3/2}$, and outer radius $r_{\mathrm{out}} = 9r_0$. Find the mass of the disc $M_{\mathrm{disc}}$ in terms of $\Sigma_0$ and $r_0$ assuming its inner radius $r_{\mathrm{in}} \ll r_0$. **[4 т.]**

Since the planet is assumed small ($q \ll h^3$), its interaction with the gas in the disc constitutes the excitation of a spiral density wave, and redistribution of matter in the co-orbital region (that is, matter orbiting at radii $r \approx r_p$), as shown in Figure 6. The resulting non-uniform density distribution induced in the disc exerts a net gravitational force, and hence a torque on the planet, which has been estimated using analytical methods. This torque, $\Gamma$, acts to change the planet’s angular momentum, and hence its orbital radius, causing it to ‘migrate’, via:

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}L}{\mathrm{d}t} = \Gamma
$$

It is convenient to write the torque in terms of the reference value

$$
\Gamma_0 = \left(\frac{q}{h}\right)^2\Sigma_p r_p^4\Omega_p^2\,.
$$

**c.** From 2-dimensional steady fluid-dynamical disc models, it is predicted that the total torque $\Gamma$ has two main contributions: from the spiral wave, the ‘Lindblad torque’, $\Gamma_L$, and from the co-orbital region, the ‘Corotation torque’, $\Gamma_C$. For a disc of uniform entropy ($\mathrm{d}s = 0$), and with surface density profile $\Sigma \propto r^{-\alpha}$, and pressure profile $P \propto r^{-\delta}$, Tanaka et al. (2002) and Paardekooper & Papaloizou (2009) find these torques are given by:

$$
\Gamma_L = (-3.20 + 0.86\alpha - 2.33\delta)\Gamma_0
$$

$$
\Gamma_C = 5.97\,(1.5 - \alpha)\,\Gamma_0
$$

We assume the gas in the disc obeys the ideal gas law, so that:

$$
\frac{P}{\Sigma T} = \text{constant}, \quad \mathrm{d}s = \text{constant}\times\left(\frac{1}{\gamma-1}\frac{\mathrm{d}T}{T} - \frac{\mathrm{d}\Sigma}{\Sigma}\right)\,,
$$

where $T$ is the absolute temperature and $\gamma$ is the adiabatic index (the ratio of the heat capacity at constant pressure to the heat capacity at constant volume). Show that for a disc of uniform entropy,

$$
\Gamma = \Gamma_L + \Gamma_C = (5.76 - (5.11 + 2.33\gamma)\alpha)\,\Gamma_0\,.
$$

*[Hint: if* $\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{y} = \lambda\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{x}$*, then* $y \propto x^{\lambda}$*.]* **[5 т.]**

**d.** Assume the disc model from part b. with $h = \mathrm{const}$. Find the time taken for the orbital radius of the planet to halve from initial radius $r_0$. Take $\gamma = 1.4$, and give your answer in terms of the migration timescale,

$$
t_{\mathrm{m}} = \frac{1}{h}\left(\frac{h^3}{q}\right)\frac{M_{\star}}{M_{\mathrm{disc}}}\tau_0\,,
$$

where $\tau_0$ is the orbital period at radius $r_0$. **[9 т.]**

**e.** If the disc has mass $M_{\mathrm{disc}} = 0.01M_{\star}$ and aspect ratio $h = 0.05$, and if $q = 5\times10^{-6}$ and $\tau_0 = 10$ years, find the elapsed time in years for the migration described in part d. to occur. Is this a feasible mechanism for planetary migration given the lifetime of the disc is roughly $10$ Myr? **[2 т.]**

**f.** Whilst the planet excites a spiral density wave over the whole disc, in fact the dominant contribution to the torque from the spiral is from only a small neighbourhood around the planet, so that $\alpha$ may be replaced by

$$
-\frac{r_p}{\Sigma_p}\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}\Sigma}{\mathrm{d}r}\right|_p\,.
$$

Near the inner radius of the disc, the surface density departs from the above power-law behaviour and smoothly decreases to $0$. Does the migration stop before the planet reaches the disc’s inner radius? **[3 т.]**

**Helpful equations:**

The moment of inertia, $I$, of a point mass $m$ moving in a circle of radius $r$ is $I = mr^2$.

The angular momentum, $L$, of a spinning object with an angular velocity of $\Omega$ is $L = I\Omega = r\times p$, where $p$ is the linear momentum of a point particle a distance $r$ from the axis of rotation.

**Q3 – Planetary Migration in Protoplanetary Discs** **[25 marks]**

**a.**

Balancing the centripetal force with the gravitational force [accept balancing accelerations]

$$
M_pr_p\Omega_p^2 = \frac{GM_*M_p}{r_p^2}
$$
[1] **[1]**

Cancelling $M_{\mathrm{p}}$ and rearranging gives $\Omega_p = \sqrt{\frac{GM_*}{r_p^3}}$ as required

[This was a ‘show that’, so the mark is for the algebraic route to the answer, not the final answer]

Using the equations for the hint at the end of the question

$$
L = I\Omega_p = M_pr_P^2\Omega_p
$$
[1] **[1]**

Substituting the expression for $\Omega_{\mathrm{p}}$ gives $L = M_p\sqrt{GM_*r_p}$ as required

[This was a ‘show that’, so the mark is for the algebraic route to the answer, not the final answer]

**b.**

We will need to integrate the surface density (mass per unit area) over the total area of the disc

$$
M_{disc} = \int_{r_{in}}^{r_{out}}\Sigma\,\mathrm{d}A
$$
[1]

Given $r_{\mathrm{out}} = 9r_0$ and $r_{\mathrm{in}} \ll r_0$ our lower integral limit is ≈ 0, and $\mathrm{d}A = 2\pi r\ \mathrm{d}r$ so

$$
M_{disc} = \int_0^{9r_0}\Sigma_0\left(\frac{r}{r_0}\right)^{-3/2}2\pi r\ \mathrm{d}r
$$
[1]

$$
= \left[\Sigma_0r_0^{3/2}4\pi r^{1/2}\right]_0^{9r_0}
$$
[1]

$$
= 12\pi\Sigma_0r_0^2
$$
[1] **[4]**

[If the first mark is not already given, it can be scored for either a correct approximation of 0 for the lower integral limit, or for converting from dA to dr. Students that do $M_{disc} = \int_0^{9r_0}\Sigma\,\mathrm{d}r = -\frac{2}{3}\Sigma_0r_0$ get a maximum of 1 mark (for the lower integral limit), since the negative total mass is non-physical]

**c.**

The final expression does not include $\delta$, so we need to find it as a function of $\alpha$ and $\gamma$ so we can combine the given expressions. Given $\Sigma \propto r^{-\alpha}$ and $P \propto r^{-\delta}$,

$\frac{P}{\Sigma T} = \text{constant} \therefore T \propto \frac{P}{\Sigma} \propto \frac{r^{-\delta}}{r^{-\alpha}} \Rightarrow T \propto r^{-\delta+\alpha}$ ① [1]

We are told it is a disc of uniform entropy (so ds = 0)

$$
ds = \text{constant}\times\left(\frac{1}{\gamma-1}\frac{dT}{T} - \frac{d\Sigma}{\Sigma}\right) = 0 \therefore \frac{dT}{T} = (\gamma-1)\frac{d\Sigma}{\Sigma}
$$
[1]

Using the hint given

$$
\Rightarrow T \propto \Sigma^{\gamma-1}
$$
[1]

But given $\Sigma \propto r^{-\alpha}$ from earlier

$\Rightarrow T \propto r^{-\alpha\gamma+\alpha}$ ② [1]

Comparing ① and ②

$$
\Rightarrow \delta = \alpha\gamma
$$
[1] **[5]**

Substituting this into the given expressions for $\Gamma_{\mathrm{L}}$ and $\Gamma_{\mathrm{C}}$ gives the required expression

$$
\Gamma = \Gamma_L + \Gamma_C = (-3.20 + 0.86\alpha - 2.33\alpha\gamma + 5.97\times1.5 - 5.97\alpha)\Gamma_0
$$

$$
= (5.755 - (5.11 + 2.33\gamma)\alpha)\Gamma_0
$$
[‘Show that’ so mark for route not answer]

**d.**

Using our earlier expression for L and assuming only the orbital radius changes with time

$$
\Gamma = \frac{\mathrm{d}L}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(M_p\sqrt{GM_*r_p}\right) = \frac{1}{2}M_p\sqrt{GM_*}r_p^{-1/2}\frac{\mathrm{d}r_p}{\mathrm{d}t}
$$
[1]

In the model of part b., $\Sigma \propto r^{-3/2}$ so by comparison with part c. $\alpha = 3/2$, and we are told $\gamma = 1.4$

Using the expression from part c. for $\Gamma$ and inputting $\Gamma_0$ with the earlier expression for $\Omega_{\mathrm{p}}$

$$
\Gamma = (5.76 - (5.11 + 2.33\gamma)\alpha)\Gamma_0
$$

$$
= (5.76 - (5.11 + 2.33\times1.4)\times1.5)\times\left(\frac{q}{h}\right)^2\Sigma_pr_p^4\frac{GM_*}{r_p^3}
$$
[1]

$$
= -6.80\left(\frac{q}{h}\right)^2\Sigma_pGM_*r_p
$$
[1]

Equating these two expressions for $\Gamma$ and rearranging for $\mathrm{d}r_{\mathrm{p}}/\mathrm{d}t$

$$
\frac{1}{2}M_p\sqrt{GM_*}r_p^{-1/2}\frac{\mathrm{d}r_p}{\mathrm{d}t} = -6.80\left(\frac{q}{h}\right)^2\Sigma_pGM_*r_p
$$

$$
\therefore \frac{\mathrm{d}r_p}{\mathrm{d}t} = -13.60\left(\frac{q}{h}\right)^2\Sigma_p\frac{\sqrt{GM_*}}{M_P}r_p^{3/2}
$$
[1]

However, we recognise that $\Sigma_p = \Sigma_0\left(\frac{r_p}{r_0}\right)^{-3/2}$

$$
\therefore \frac{\mathrm{d}r_p}{\mathrm{d}t} = -13.60\left(\frac{q}{h}\right)^2\Sigma_0\frac{\sqrt{GM_*}}{M_P}r_0^{3/2}
$$
[1]

All of the parameters on the right hand side are constant $\therefore \frac{\mathrm{d}r_p}{\mathrm{d}t} = \mathrm{constant}$, so if in time t the radius of the planet has halved, $\frac{\mathrm{d}r_p}{\mathrm{d}t} = -\frac{\frac{1}{2}r_0}{t}$ [negative since $r_{\mathrm{p}}$ is decreasing] [1]

Substituting it in and rearranging for t

$$
-\frac{\frac{1}{2}r_0}{t} = -13.60\left(\frac{q}{h}\right)^2\Sigma_0\frac{\sqrt{GM_*}}{M_P}r_0^{3/2}
$$

$$
\therefore t = \frac{1}{27.19}\left(\frac{h}{q}\right)^2\frac{1}{\Sigma_0}\frac{M_p}{\sqrt{GM_*}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{r_0}}
$$
[1]

From Kepler’s 3rd law, we can derive an expression for the initial orbital period, $\tau_0$

$$
\tau_0 = \frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{GM_*}}r_0^{3/2}
$$

$$
\therefore t = \frac{1}{27.19}\left(\frac{h}{q}\right)^2\frac{M_p}{2}\frac{\tau_0}{\pi\Sigma_0r_0^2}
$$
[1]

As found in part b., $M_{disc} = 12\pi\Sigma_0r_0^2$ and remembering that $q = M_p/M_*$

$$
\therefore t = \frac{1}{27.19}\left(\frac{h}{q}\right)^2\frac{M_p}{2}\frac{12\tau_0}{M_{disc}} = \frac{6}{27.19}\frac{1}{h}\frac{h^3}{q}\frac{M_*}{M_{disc}}\tau_0 = \boxed{0.221t_m}
$$
[1] **[9]**

[Must be in terms of the migration timescale, $t_{\mathrm{m}}$, for the final mark. The numerical factor may be left as 1/4.53 or equivalent rather than evaluated]

**e.**

Substituting the numbers into the equation we just derived

$$
t = 0.221\times\frac{1}{0.05}\times\frac{0.05^3}{5\times10^{-6}}\times\frac{1}{0.01}\times10\ \text{yrs} = \boxed{110\ 000\ \text{yrs}}
$$
[1] **[1]**

Since $t < 10$ Myr then $\boxed{\text{yes}}$, it is a feasible mechanism [1] **[1]**

[Award 0.5 marks for calculating $t_{\mathrm{m}}$ (= 0.5 Myr). Second mark can only be gained with a sensible comparison between their t (allowing ecf) and 10 Myr, not for a bald statement. Allow use of the more precise formula for $\Gamma$ from c. leading to 110 246 years rather than 110 327 years]

**f.**

The migration will stop when $\Gamma$ = 0, which we can use to define a critical value of $\alpha$

$$
\Gamma = (5.76 - (5.11 + 2.33\gamma)\alpha_{crit})\Gamma_0 = 0
$$

$$
\therefore \alpha_{crit} = \frac{5.76}{5.11+2.33\gamma}
$$
[1]

Since $\gamma \geq 1$ this means $0 < \alpha_{crit} < 0.774$

Far from the inner edge (as used previously in the question), $\alpha = 3/2 = 1.5$

We are told that $\Sigma$ decreases to zero at the inner edge, so the variation of $\Sigma$ as a function of increasing r must be that it starts from zero at $r_{\mathrm{inner}}$, increases smoothly to some maximum, and then decreases again following $\Sigma \propto r^{-3/2}$.

Since $\alpha = -\frac{r_p}{\Sigma_p}\left.\frac{d\Sigma}{dr}\right|_p$ then following the behaviour described above there must be a region near $r_{\mathrm{inner}}$ where $\left.\frac{d\Sigma}{dr}\right|_p$ is positive (since in that region $\Sigma$ is increasing with increasing r) and so (since $r_{\mathrm{p}}$ and $\Sigma_{\mathrm{p}}$ are also positive) $\alpha$ is negative [1]

[This mark is for any reasoning that to match the described behaviour then there must be a change in the sign of $\alpha$ as you approach $r_{\mathrm{inner}}$]

This means far from the inner edge $\alpha = 1.5 > \text{all possible } \alpha_{crit}$

and close to the inner edge $\alpha < 0 < \text{all possible } \alpha_{crit}$

so there must be a place in the disc between $r_{\mathrm{outer}}$ and $r_{\mathrm{inner}}$ where $\alpha = \alpha_{crit}$

hence $\boxed{\text{the migration does halt}}$ before the inner edge of the disc [1] **[3]**

[No mark for a bald statement without some attempt at mathematical justification]

[This is the reason planets are able to form **and** survive to be detected in mature star systems, otherwise planets would only be a feature of young systems (before spiralling into their star) and life on Earth would not have had enough time to evolve]
