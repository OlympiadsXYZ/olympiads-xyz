*ΩWAO 2022 ◇ Theoretical Round ◇ Problems*

## Условие

Light-collecting area of the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) is about $25\ \mathrm{m^2}$. Estimate how long it takes to catch 10 photons emitted by a solar type star in a galaxy at redshift $z = 0.2$.

The JWST wavelength coverage is 0.6 – 28.5 µm (orange to mid-infrared). Assume that the JWST is equipped with a single detector capable of registering photons over the entire wavelength coverage of the telescope.

## Отговори

<Spoiler title="Покажи отговорите">

- 2000000 s

</Spoiler>

## Решение

<Spoiler title="Покажи официалното решение">

First, let us estimate the distance to the star. For simplicity, we will assume here and below that $z \ll 1$ still:

$$d = \frac{cz}{H_0} = \frac{3\cdot 10^5\ \mathrm{km/s}\cdot 0.2}{70\ \mathrm{(km/s)/Mpc}} = 0.86\ \mathrm{Gpc}.$$

We consider the star to be a black body. Average energy of a black-body photon is $\bar{\varepsilon} = 2.70k_BT$. The estimated photon flux density (number of photons per square meter per second) from the star in the Solar system is

$$F = \frac{L_\odot}{4\pi d^2}\cdot\frac{1}{\bar{\varepsilon}} = \frac{4\pi R_\odot^2\sigma T_\odot^4}{4\pi d^2\cdot 2.70k_BT_\odot} = \frac{R_\odot^2\sigma T_\odot^3}{2.70d^2k_B} = 2.0\cdot 10^{-7}\ \mathrm{m^{-2}\cdot s^{-1}}.$$

Not all of these photons fall within the telescope’s wavelength coverage. The observed $\lambda$ range of 0.6 – 28.5 µm corresponds to initial $\lambda' = \frac{\lambda}{1+z}$ range of 0.5 – 23.75 µm.

Recalling Planck’s law and expression $\varepsilon = h\nu = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$ for energy of a photon, we introduce substitution $x \equiv \frac{hc}{\lambda k_BT_\odot}$:

$$x_1 = 0.105, \quad x_2 = 4.97.$$

Such a range covers both the mean value over the energy distribution ($x_{\mathrm{mean}} = 2.70$, mentioned before) and the rather sharp peak of the distribution itself ($x_{\max} = 2.82$). So, we can expect that in fact almost all photons fall within the JWST wavelength coverage (in fact, 90 % of the photons from the star meet that condition).

By the way, the frequency for maximal spectral radiance of a black body with temperature $T_\odot$ is $\nu_{\max} = 341$ THz with corresponding wavelength $\lambda'_{\max} = 0.88$ µm. The redshift only shifts this peak further into the infrared.

*Note.* Constants $k_B$ and $\sigma$ are not given in the table. They can be estimated using well-known energetic properties of the Sun. For example, the solar luminosity

$$L_\odot = 4\pi R_\odot^2\sigma T_\odot^4 \approx 3.8\cdot 10^{26}\ \mathrm{W},$$

or the solar constant

$$A_\odot \approx 1.4\ \mathrm{kW/m^2} = \frac{R_\odot^2\sigma T_\odot^4}{a_\oplus^2}.$$

So, the Stefan–Boltzmann constant

$$\sigma = \frac{L_\odot}{4\pi R_\odot^2T_\odot^4} = \frac{A_\odot a_\oplus^2}{R_\odot^2T_\odot^4} \approx 5.7\cdot 10^{-8}\ \mathrm{W/(m^2\cdot K^4)}.$$

One could also recall Wien’s displacement law:

$$\frac{hc}{\lambda_{\max}} \simeq 5.0k_BT_\odot,$$

where $\lambda_{\max} = 0.5$ µm for the solar spectrum. Of course, $\lambda_{\max} \neq \lambda'_{\max}$: it is well-known that parameterization by frequency implies a different maximal wavelength than parameterization by wavelength.

Then $x_i = \dfrac{\lambda_{\max}}{\lambda_i}\cdot 5.0$, and

$$k_B = \frac{hc}{5\lambda_{\max}T_\odot} \approx 1.4\cdot 10^{-23}\ \mathrm{J/K}.$$

Actually, the exact expression for $F$ in this model is

$$F = \frac{R_\odot^2\sigma T_\odot^4}{d^2}\cdot\left(\frac{\int\limits_0^\infty\frac{x^3\,dx}{e^x-1}}{\int\limits_0^\infty\frac{x^2\,dx}{e^x-1}}k_BT_\odot\right)^{-1}\frac{\int\limits_{x_1}^{x_2}\frac{x^2\,dx}{e^x-1}}{\int\limits_0^\infty\frac{x^2\,dx}{e^x-1}} = \frac{R_\odot^2T_\odot^3}{d^2k_B}\cdot\frac{2\pi k_B^4}{h^3c^2}\cdot 3!\cdot\zeta(4)\cdot\frac{2!\cdot\zeta(3)}{3!\cdot\zeta(4)}\cdot\frac{\int\limits_{x_1}^{x_2}\frac{x^2\,dx}{e^x-1}}{2!\cdot\zeta(3)} =$$

$$= \frac{2\pi R_\odot^2c}{d^2}\cdot\left(\frac{k_BT_\odot}{hc}\right)^3\cdot\int\limits_{x_1}^{x_2}\frac{x^2\,dx}{e^x-1} = \frac{2\pi R_\odot^2c}{125d^2\lambda_{\max}^3}\cdot\int\limits_{x_1}^{x_2}\frac{x^2\,dx}{e^x-1} \simeq 0.83\cdot 10^{-7}\ \mathrm{m^{-2}\cdot s^{-1}}\times\int\limits_{x_1}^{x_2}\frac{x^2\,dx}{e^x-1}.$$

The last integral can be calculated numerically, although this was not required for the presented solution. One could also estimate that integral as

$$\int\limits_{x_1}^{x_2}\frac{x^2\,dx}{e^x-1} \approx \int\limits_0^\infty\frac{x^2\,dx}{e^x-1} \approx \int\limits_0^\infty x^2e^{-x}\,dx = 2! = 2 \sim 1.$$

How long does it take for the telescope ($A = 25\ \mathrm{m^2}$) to accumulate 10 photons from the star?

$$\tau = \frac{10}{FA} = 2.0\cdot 10^6\ \mathrm{s} \approx 23\ \mathrm{d}.$$

**Marking Scheme:**

- Distance to the galaxy — **2 pt.**
- Illuminance — **3 pt.**
- Photon flux (taking into account the wavelength coverage and redshift) — **3 pt.**
- Time interval — **2 pt.**
