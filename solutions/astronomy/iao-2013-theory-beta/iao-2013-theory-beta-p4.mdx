## Условие

(Dubingiai is the nearest town to the accommodation place of XVIII IAO.).

There were different systems of units of measurement in the history of science. This problem is to use historical (at present obsolete) units of measurement.

**Units of power**

1 horse-power (hp) = 735.49875 W.

**Local units of length in the end of XVIII century**

- 1 arshin (arsh) = 0.711187 m
- 1 span (sp) = 1/4 arshin
- 1 vershok (vrsh) = 1/4 span
- 1 sajene (sj) = 3 arshin
- 1 verst (vrst) = 500 sajene

**4.1.** Calculate the capacity of the solar energy that in the end of the XVIII century fell on the unit of area of the territory in the outskirts of Dubingiai at midday time:

in winter, in spring, in autumn, and in summer.

The answer must be given using only the «new» physical units, which were coming into operation in those days in this area: horse-powers per square verst.

## Решение

<Spoiler title="Покажи официалното решение">

<ProblemSection id="solution-solar-constant">

### Solar constant in historical units

Horse-powers per square verst is a power per unit of area, the quantity that dimension is similar to the solar constant. In physics, it is properly called the “power flux”.

Let us calculate the solar constant in units, which were coming into operation in the end of the XVIII century.

**I method — visual.**

The energy-release of the Sun has not changed since the end of the XVIII century and in units of the horse-power is

$$
L=3.86\cdot10^{26}\ \mathrm W/(735.5\ \mathrm{W/hp})=5.25\cdot10^{23}\ \text{horse-powers}.
$$

The distance from the Sun to Earth has not changed since the end of the XVIII century as well, and in versts is

$$
a=1\ \mathrm{AU}=1.496\cdot10^{11}\ \mathrm m/(3\cdot500\cdot0.7112\ \mathrm{m/vrst})=1.402\cdot10^8\ \mathrm{vrst}.
$$

Thus, the solar constant in units of the end of the XVIII century is

$$
A_{\mathrm{XIX}}=L/(4\pi a^2)=\frac{5.25\cdot10^{23}\ \mathrm{hp}}{4\pi(1.402\cdot10^8\ \mathrm{vrst})^2}=2.12\cdot10^6\ \mathrm{hp/vrst^2}.
$$

**II method — formal.**

$$
A_{\mathrm{XIX}}=A_0\,k_{(\mathrm{hp/W})}/k_{(\mathrm{vrst/m})}^{2}=\frac{1367\ \mathrm{W/m^2}}{735.5\ \mathrm{W/hp}}(1066.78\ \mathrm{m/vrst})^2=2.12\cdot10^6\ \mathrm{hp/vrst^2}.
$$

*Source note: the key’s subscript “XIX” is retained although the problem concerns the XVIII century.*

</ProblemSection>

<ProblemSection id="solution-seasons">

### 4.1. Midday power flux

The outskirts of Dubingiai in the XVIII century were located on the same latitude as where they are located in the XXI century, that is, at about latitudes 54.7°–55.2° (latitudes of Vilnius and Moletai respectively, may be found from the map). The value of 23.5°, the inclination of the equatorial plane of the ecliptics, also almost has not changed (strictly speaking, it had declined by about one minute). Thus, at the middays of the winter solstice in the XVIII century in the outskirts of the town Dubingiai the sun was at an altitude about

$$
h_W=90^\circ-55.0^\circ-23.5^\circ=11.5^\circ,
$$

at the middays of spring and autumn — at an altitude about

$$
h_0=90^\circ-55.0^\circ=35.0^\circ,
$$

at the middays of the summer solstice — at an altitude about

$$
h_S=90^\circ-55.0^\circ+23.5^\circ=58.5^\circ.
$$

While the altitude of the Sun is h, the power flux to the unit of the territory is

$$
W=A\sin h.
$$

Thus, the target values are:

- in winter — $W_W=A\sin h_W\approx420\,000\ \mathrm{hp/vrst^2}$;
- in spring and autumn — $W_0=A\sin h_0\approx1\,220\,000\ \mathrm{hp/vrst^2}$;
- in summer — $W_S=A\sin h_S\approx1\,810\,000\ \mathrm{hp/vrst^2}$.

</ProblemSection>

<ProblemSection id="solution-horse">

### 4.2. Solar power incident on a horse

And what is the capacity of the solar energy incident at those times on a local horse? This value largely depends on the orientation of the horse relative to the Sun than the altitude of the sun above the horizon. Let us assume that the cross-sectional area of a horse perpendicular to the sun-rays is 1–3 arsh² (depending on the mentioned above orientation of the horse) and recalculate the value of the solar constant into horse-powers per square arshin.

Distance from the Sun

$$
a=1\ \mathrm{AU}=1.496\cdot10^{11}\ \mathrm m/(0.7112\ \mathrm{m/arsh})=2.103\cdot10^{14}\ \mathrm{arsh}.
$$

The solar constant in these units equals to

$$
A=L/(4\pi a^2)=\frac{5.25\cdot10^{23}\ \mathrm{hp}}{4\pi(2.103\cdot10^{11}\ \mathrm{arsh})^2}=0.94\ \mathrm{hp/arsh^2}.
$$

Thus, a horse standing in the sun, receives about one to three horse-powers of solar radiation! Just this can be a surprise.

Answer: 1–3 hp.

*Note. In solving the problem we emphasize very often that many of the physical parameters are not changed in the last two centuries: the solar constant, the distance from Earth to the Sun, the latitude of location, inclination of the ecliptic to the equatorial plane. Students who also record this constancy, should be encouraged to add points.*

*Source note: the distance conversion prints $2.103\cdot10^{14}$ arsh, but the following formula uses $2.103\cdot10^{11}$ arsh. Both printed exponents are retained.*

</ProblemSection>
