## Условие

Hungarian astronomers significantly contributed to the study of pulsating variable stars of RR Lyrae type, which show cyclic amplitude modulation in their light variation (Blazhko effect)

The light variation of an RR Lyrae star is observed at two different wavelengths: $\lambda_1 = 500\ \mathrm{nm}$ and $\lambda_2 = 2000\ \mathrm{nm}$. At each wavelength, we see into the star at a different depth. We refer to the depths as layer 1 and layer 2. One can approximate the light intensity of a star at a given wavelength by the radius and the black-body radiation intensity at the appropriate depth. Moreover, the Wien approximation can be used for the black-body radiation to calculate the emitted power from unit surface area:

$$
F(\lambda, T) \propto \frac{1}{\lambda^5}\exp\left(-\frac{hc}{k\lambda T}\right),
$$

where $h$ is the Planck constant, $k$ is the Boltzmann constant, and $c$ is the speed of light. To simplify the calculations we introduce a new constant $C_b = hc/k \approx 0.0144\ \mathrm{m\ K}$.

**a)** Assume that the temperature varies between $T_1 = 6000\ \mathrm{K}$ and $T_2 = 7400\ \mathrm{K}$ in each of the layers and ignoring radius variation, what is the ratio of the amplitudes of variation in magnitudes at the two wavelengths? (5 p) **[5 т.]**

**b)** What is the peak to peak amplitude of the light curve at $\lambda_1$? Use the magnitude scale. (3 p) **[3 т.]**

**c)** Ignoring temperature variation, what is the contribution of the radius variation to the light-curve amplitude for a given wavelength, if $R_{\min} = 0.9\ \langle R \rangle$ and $R_{\max} = 1.05\ \langle R \rangle$? $\langle R \rangle$ is the mean radius of the given layer. (3 p) **[3 т.]**

**d)** Recent observations and models show that the radius of the photosphere is only minimally modulated during the Blazhko cycle; however, the temperature variation is significant. As a result, the amplitude of light curve itself keeps varying. Let us assume that during the minimum amplitude of pulsation the temperature variation is reduced to $T_{\min} = 6100\ \mathrm{K}$ and $T_{\max} = 6900\ \mathrm{K}$. What is the modulation amplitude at the two wavelengths? For the maximum amplitude, use the temperature values given in part a). (5 p) **[5 т.]**

**e)** Which statement is correct? (There can be more than one correct selection.) **Mark the letter of your answer with × on the answer sheet.** (4 p)

(A) It is easier to observe the Blazhko effect in the infrared band.  
(B) The temperature variation dominates the visual light curve.  
(C) If we neglect the radius variation, the amplitude is inversely proportional to the wavelength.  
(D) Multi-colour observations are not useful in understanding the Blazhko effect. **[4 т.]**

## Решение

<Spoiler title="Покажи официалното решение">

a) The total power of the star in the given lambda:

$$
\text{(8.1)}\quad I(\lambda, T, R) = C_1 R^2 F(\lambda, T) = C_1 R^2 \frac{1}{\lambda^5}\exp\left(-\frac{C_b}{\lambda T}\right)\qquad \text{(1 p)}
$$

Converting to magnitudes:

$$
\text{(8.2)}\quad m(\lambda, T, R) = -2.5\log I(\lambda, T) = C_2 + 12.5\log\lambda - 5\log R - 2.5\log e\,\frac{C_b}{\lambda T}\qquad \text{(1 p)}
$$

Then:

$$
\text{(8.3)}\quad A(\lambda) = |m(\lambda, T_{\max}) - m(\lambda, T_{\min})| = \frac{2.5\log e\, C_b}{\lambda}\left(\frac{1}{T_{\min}} - \frac{1}{T_{\max}}\right)\qquad \text{(1 p)}
$$

Thus:

$$
\text{(8.4)}\quad \frac{A(\lambda_1)}{A(\lambda_2)} = \frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1}\qquad \text{(1 p)}
$$

With numerical values:

$$
\frac{A(\lambda_1)}{A(\lambda_2)} = \frac{2\times 10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m}}{5\times 10^{-7}\ \mathrm{m}} = \boxed{4}\qquad \text{(1 p)}
$$

b) From Eq. (8.3):

$$
\text{(8.5)}\quad A_{\max}(\lambda_1) = \frac{2.5\log e\, C_b}{\lambda_1}\left(\frac{1}{T_{\min}} - \frac{1}{T_{\max}}\right)\qquad \text{(2 p)}
$$

With numerical values:

$$
A_{\max}(\lambda_1) = \frac{2.5\log e\times 0.0144\ \mathrm{m\ K}}{5\times 10^{-7}\ \mathrm{m}}\left(\frac{1}{6000\ \mathrm{K}} - \frac{1}{7400\ \mathrm{K}}\right) = \boxed{1^{\mathrm{m}}}\qquad \text{(1 p)}
$$

c) If we ignore the temperature variation, then:

$$
\text{(8.6)}\quad A(\lambda) = |m(\lambda, R_{\max}) - m(\lambda, R_{\min})| = 5\log\frac{R_{\max}}{R_{\min}}\qquad \text{(2 p)}
$$

With numerical values:

$$
A(\lambda) = 5\log\frac{1.05}{0.9} = \boxed{0.33^{\mathrm{m}}}\qquad \text{(1 p)}
$$

d) From Eq. (8.4):

$$
\text{(8.7)}\quad A_{\max}(\lambda_2) = A_{\max}(\lambda_1)\,\frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_2} = 1^{\mathrm{m}}\times\frac{1}{4} = 0.25^{\mathrm{m}}\qquad \text{(1 p)}
$$

Similarly:

$$
A_{\min}(\lambda_1) = \frac{2.5\log e\times 0.0144\ \mathrm{m\ K}}{5\times 10^{-7}\ \mathrm{m}}\left(\frac{1}{6100\ \mathrm{K}} - \frac{1}{6900\ \mathrm{K}}\right) = 0.6^{\mathrm{m}}\qquad \text{(2 p)}
$$

and

$$
A_{\min}(\lambda_2) = A_{\min}(\lambda_1)\,\frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_2} = 0.6^{\mathrm{m}}\times\frac{1}{4} = \boxed{0.15^{\mathrm{m}}}\qquad \text{(2 p)}
$$

At $\lambda_1 = 500\ \mathrm{nm}$ the amplitude changes by $1^{\mathrm{m}} - 0.6^{\mathrm{m}} = 0.4^{\mathrm{m}}$, while at $\lambda_2 = 2000\ \mathrm{nm}$ it is reduced to $0.25^{\mathrm{m}} - 0.15^{\mathrm{m}} = 0.1^{\mathrm{m}}$, that is a significant difference.

e) B and C (4 p)
