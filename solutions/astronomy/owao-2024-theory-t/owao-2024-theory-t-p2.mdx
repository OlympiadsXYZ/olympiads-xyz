## Условие

A satellite is moving in a circular orbit around the Earth with an inclination of $20^{\circ}$ and an orbital radius of 30 000 km. At noon, the satellite passes through the zenith at a point $P_1$ with coordinates $(0^{\circ}\,\mathrm{N},\ 10^{\circ}\,\mathrm{E})$.

**a)** At which point $P_2$ of the Earth will the satellite pass through the zenith after 3 hours?

**b)** At what astronomical altitude will it be for the observer at $P_1$ while passing over $P_2$?

## Отговори

<Spoiler title="Покажи отговорите">

- **a)** (19° N, 39° E) or (19° S, 39° E)
- **b)** 47 °

</Spoiler>

## Решение

<Spoiler title="Покажи официалното решение">

a) Since the inclination is positive and does not exceed $90^{\circ}$, the orbital motion of the satellite occurs in the same direction as the rotation of the Earth around its axis. We should consider two cases, since it is not known whether the satellite rises above the plane of the equator or falls below the equator.

Let us consider the case when the satellite rises above the equator. First, we will determine by Kepler’s third law how many degrees of its orbit ($r = 3.0 \cdot 10^4$ km) the satellite will fly during the time $t = 3$ h:

$$
\frac{T^2}{r^3} = \frac{4\pi^2}{G\mathfrak{M}_\oplus} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{r^3}{G\mathfrak{M}_\oplus}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{r^3}{g_\oplus R_\oplus^2}} =
$$

$$
= 2 \times 3.14 \times \sqrt{\frac{(3.0 \cdot 10^7\ \mathrm{m})^3}{9.81\ \mathrm{m/s^2} \times (6.371 \cdot 10^6)^2}} = 5.17 \cdot 10^4\ \mathrm{s} = 14.4^{\mathrm{h}};
$$

$$
S_1S_2 = \frac{t}{T} \cdot 360^{\circ} = \frac{3^{\mathrm{h}}}{14.4^{\mathrm{h}}} \times 360^{\circ} = 75^{\circ}.
$$

The satellite has completed less than a quarter of its orbit.

Now we assume that the Earth does not rotate — we will take this effect into account when obtaining the final longitude of the second point.

Let $\tilde P_1$ and $\tilde P_2$ be the projections of positions of the satellites $S_1$ and $S_2$ on the non-rotating Earth (see Figure 2). The goal is to find the coordinates $(\varphi_2;\ \tilde\lambda_2)$ of the point $\tilde P_2$.

We draw a perpendicular from the point $\tilde P_2$ to the equator. Using spherical laws for $\triangle\tilde P_1\tilde P_2\tilde P_\perp$ one can write

$$
\begin{aligned} &\cos\tilde P_1\tilde P_2 = \cos\tilde P_1\tilde P_\perp\cos\tilde P_2\tilde P_\perp + \sin\tilde P_1\tilde P_\perp\sin\tilde P_2\tilde P_\perp\cos\tilde P_\perp\\ &\Longrightarrow\quad \cos\tilde P_1\tilde P_2 = \cos(\tilde\lambda_2 - \tilde\lambda_1)\cos\varphi_2; \end{aligned}
$$

$$
\frac{\sin\tilde P_1\tilde P_2}{\sin\tilde P_\perp} = \frac{\sin\tilde P_2\tilde P_\perp}{\sin\tilde P_1} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sin\tilde P_1\tilde P_2 \cdot \sin i = \sin\varphi_2;
$$

$$
\begin{aligned} &\sin\tilde P_2\tilde P_\perp\cos\tilde P_\perp = \sin\tilde P_1\tilde P_\perp\cos\tilde P_1\tilde P_2 - \cos\tilde P_1\tilde P_\perp\sin\tilde P_1\tilde P_2\cos\tilde P_1\\ &\Longrightarrow\quad \tan(\tilde\lambda_2 - \tilde\lambda_1) = \tan\tilde P_1\tilde P_2 \cdot \cos i. \end{aligned}
$$

The longitude $\tilde\lambda_2$ is determined unambiguously

$$
\tilde\lambda_2 = \tilde\lambda_1 + \arctan(\tan\tilde P_1\tilde P_2 \cdot \cos i) = 10^{\circ} + \arctan(\tan 75^{\circ} \cdot \cos 20^{\circ}) = 84^{\circ}.
$$

In 3 hours, the Earth will turn in the same direction as the satellite, at an angle

$$
\Delta\lambda = \frac{3^{\mathrm{h}}}{24^{\mathrm{h}}} \times 360^{\circ} = 45^{\circ}.
$$

Then the actual longitude of the point $P_2$ is $\lambda_2 = \tilde\lambda_2 - \Delta\lambda = 84^{\circ} - 45^{\circ} = 39^{\circ}\,\mathrm{E}$.

The latitude of $P_2$ is $\sin\varphi_2 = \arcsin(\sin\tilde P_1\tilde P_2 \cdot \sin i) = \arcsin(\sin 75^{\circ}\sin 20^{\circ}) = 19.3^{\circ}$.

Recalling that there are two cases of satellite motion (the starting point could be an ascending or descending node of the orbit), we write down the possible coordinates of point $P_2$:

$$
\begin{aligned} &(19^{\circ}\,\mathrm{N},\ 39^{\circ}\,\mathrm{E}),\\ \text{or}\quad &(19^{\circ}\,\mathrm{S},\ 39^{\circ}\,\mathrm{E}). \end{aligned}
$$

b) First, we determine the angular distance between the observation points:

$$
\begin{aligned} \cos P_1P_2 &= \sin\varphi_1\sin\varphi_2 + \cos\varphi_1\cos\varphi_2\cos(\lambda_2 - \lambda_1) =\\ &= \sin 0^{\circ}\sin 19^{\circ} + \cos 0^{\circ}\cos 19^{\circ}\cos(39^{\circ} - 10^{\circ}) = 0.827 \end{aligned}
$$

$$
P_1P_2 = \arccos 0.827 = 34^{\circ}.
$$

Next, we determine the distance from point $P_1$ to the satellite (point $S_2$) by the law of cosines (see Figure 3):

$$
\begin{aligned} P_1S &= \sqrt{P_1O^2 + SO^2 - 2P_1O \cdot SO \cdot \cos P_1OS} =\\ &= \sqrt{6\,371^2 + 30\,000^2 - 2 \times 6\,371 \cdot 30\,000 \times \cos 34^{\circ}}\ [\mathrm{km}] = 2.5 \cdot 10^4\ \mathrm{km}. \end{aligned}
$$

Finally, we determine the angle $OP_1S$:

$$
\begin{aligned} OP_1S &= \arccos\frac{OP_1^2 + P_1S^2 - OS^2}{2OP_1 \cdot P_1S} =\\ &= \arccos\frac{6\,371^2 + 2.5 \cdot 10^4 - 30\,000^2}{2 \times 6\,371 \times 2.5 \cdot 10^4} = \arccos(-0.736) \approx 137^{\circ}. \end{aligned}
$$

We conclude that from the point $P_1$, the satellite is observed at the altitude of $137^{\circ} - 90^{\circ} = 47^{\circ}$.
