## Условие

Hanny’s Voorwerp (Dutch for ‘object’) is a rare type of astronomical object discovered in 2007 by the school teacher Hanny van Arkel whilst participating as a volunteer in the Galaxy Zoo project. When inspecting the image of the galaxy IC 2497 in the constellation Leo Minor, she observed a bright green blob close to the galaxy.

<figure className="problem-figure problem-figure--sized" style={{'--fig-w': '35.5%', '--fig-max': '227px'}}>
<img src="https://pub-43290baaaff14857b5dd59610ea438c7.r2.dev/problems/baao-2017-x/p4-fig1.png" alt="Hubble photograph on a black background: a spiral galaxy with a bright pinkish core near the top and, below it, an irregular glowing green cloud (the Voorwerp)." width="710" height="987" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
<figcaption>Figure 5: HST image of galaxy IC 2497 and the glowing Voorwerp below it. Credit: Keel et al. (2012) & Galaxy Zoo.</figcaption>
</figure>

Subsequent observations have shown that the galaxy IC 2497 is at a redshift of $z = 0.05$, with the Voorwerp at a similar distance and with a projected angular separation of 20 arcseconds from the centre of the galaxy (3600 arcseconds = $1^{\circ}$). Radio observations suggest that the Voorwerp is a massive cloud of gas, made of ionized hydrogen, with a size of 10 kpc and a mass of $10^{11}\ \mathrm{M}_{\odot}$. It is probably a cloud of gas that was stripped from the galaxy during a merger with another nearby galaxy.

In this question you will explore the cause of the ‘glow’ of the Voorwerp and will learn about a new type of an astronomical object; a quasar.

**a.** Given that Hubble’s constant is measured as $H_0 = 70\ \mathrm{km\ s^{-1}\ Mpc^{-1}}$, calculate the distance to the galaxy (in Mpc). **[2 т.]**

The rate of ionizing photons from a source (in photons per second) can be expressed as:

$$
S_* = Vn^2\alpha
$$

where $V$ is the volume of the ionized region, $n$ is the number density of the ionized gas and $\alpha$ is the ionization coefficient, $\alpha = 2.6\times10^{-13}\ \mathrm{cm^3\ s^{-1}}$.

**b.** Calculate the power (luminosity) of the source required to completely ionize the Voorwerp (assumed to be spherical), given that the mass of a hydrogen atom is $1.67\times10^{-27}$ kg and the ionization energy of hydrogen is 13.6 eV, where 1 eV = $1.60\times10^{-19}$ J. **[5 т.]**

One possible source of ionizing radiation is the jet arising from the accretion of material onto the supermassive black hole (SMBH) situated in the centre of the galaxy. This produces an enormous amount of energy, greatly brightening the galaxy; a galaxy shining due to this process is known as a quasar.

**c.** The gravitational potential energy of the material falling to radius $R$, which in this case is a black hole with radius equal to the Schwarzschild radius, $R_{\mathrm{S}} = 2GM/c^2$, at a mass accretion rate $\dot{m} \equiv \delta m/\delta t$, is converted into radiation with an efficiency of $\eta$. Show that the power (luminosity) output of the SMBH is given by:

$$
L = \frac{1}{2}\eta\dot{m}c^2\,.
$$
**[2 т.]**

**d.** The typical mass accretion rate onto an active SMBH is $\sim 2\ \mathrm{M}_{\odot}\ \mathrm{yr}^{-1}$ and the typical efficiency is $\eta = 0.1$. Calculate the typical luminosity of a quasar. Compare the luminosity of the quasar with the power needed to ionize the Voorwerp. **[3 т.]**

Detailed astronomical observations have shown than the nucleus of the galaxy has a modest luminosity of $L < 10^{33}$ W, thus the black hole in IC 2497 is not currently active (i.e. the accretion rate is very low). Quasars are thought to ignite every time the black hole starts accreting a fresh source of matter, and switch off once that supply is exhausted. Therefore, this might be the first evidence of a quasar switching off recently (by astronomical standards), with the Voorwerp reflecting the light emitted by the quasar whilst it was still active. This would make the Voorwerp a ‘quasar ionization echo’ and IC 2497 the nearest galaxy to us to host a quasar.

**e.** Calculate the projected physical separation, $r_{\mathrm{p}}$, between the galaxy and the Voorwerp. **[2 т.]**

**f.** Derive an expression for the difference in the light travel time between photons travelling directly to Earth from the galaxy and photons reflected off the Voorwerp first. Give your formula as a function of $r_{\mathrm{p}}$ and $\theta$, where $\theta$ is the angle between the lines of sight to the Earth and to the centre of the Voorwerp as measured by an observer at the centre of IC 2497. (For example $\theta = 90^{\circ}$ would correspond to the galaxy and Voorwerp both being the exact same distance from the Earth, and so the projected distance $r_{\mathrm{p}}$ is therefore also the true distance between them.) **[5 т.]**

**g.** High precision measurements showed that the Voorwerp is slightly further away than the galaxy, and so $\theta = 125^{\circ}$. Use this with your expression from the previous part of the question to estimate an upper limit for the number of years that have passed since the quasar was last active. **[1 т.]**

## Решение

<Spoiler title="Покажи официалното решение">

**Q4 - Hanny's Voorwerp** **[Total = 20]**

*a. Given that Hubble’s constant is measured as* $H_0$ *= 70 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹, calculate the distance to the galaxy (in Mpc).*

Need to turn the redshift into a recessional velocity and then combine with Hubble's Law

$z = \frac{v}{c}\ (\text{for small } z) \quad \therefore \quad v = zc = 0.05\times3.00\times10^{5} = 15\,000\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ [1]

$d = \frac{v}{H_0} = \frac{15\,000}{70} = 214\ \mathrm{Mpc}$ [1] **[2]**

*b. Calculate the power (luminosity) of the source required to completely ionize the Voorwerp (assumed to be spherical), given that the mass of a hydrogen atom is 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg and the ionization energy of hydrogen is 13.6 eV, where 1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.*

Given we know the radius of the cloud (10 kpc) and the mass ($10^{11}\ \mathrm{M}_{\odot}$) we can work out the number density of hydrogen atoms

$S_* = Vn^2\alpha \qquad \text{and} \qquad n = \frac{M/m_{\mathrm{H}}}{V} \qquad \text{and} \qquad V = \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3$

$\therefore S_* = \left(\frac{M}{m_{\mathrm{H}}}\right)^2\frac{\alpha}{V}$ [1]

$= \left(\frac{10^{11}\ \mathrm{M}_{\odot}}{m_{\mathrm{H}}}\right)^2\frac{\alpha}{\frac{4}{3}\pi R^3} = \left(\frac{10^{11}\times1.99\times10^{30}}{1.67\times10^{-27}}\right)^2\frac{2.6\times10^{-19}}{\frac{4}{3}\pi\times(10\times10^{3}\times3.09\times10^{16})^3}$ [1]

$= 2.99\times10^{55}\ \mathrm{photons\ s^{-1}}$ [1]

(watch that the units of α are converted correctly to SI)

The luminosity can then be calculated as we know the energy of each photon

$L = S_*E_{\mathrm{photon}} = 2.99\times10^{55}\times13.6\times1.60\times10^{-19}$ [1]

$= 6.50\times10^{37}\ \mathrm{W}$ [1] **[5]**

(Allow full credit for interpreting the 10 kpc 'size' of the cloud to mean its diameter rather than its radius, giving S* = 2.39 × 10⁵⁶ photons s⁻¹ and L = 5.20 × 10³⁸ W)

[Working out S* directly may prove difficult for some calculators as (M/m$_{\mathrm{H}}$)² may exceed their largest power of ten, in which case students should work out √S* and then square it later.]

*c. The gravitational potential energy of the material falling to radius R, which in this case is a black hole with radius equal to the Schwarzschild radius,* $R_S = 2GM/c^2$*, at a mass accretion rate* $\dot{m} \equiv \delta m/\delta t$ *, is converted into radiation with an efficiency of . Show that the power (luminosity) output of the SMBH is given by* $L = \frac{1}{2}\eta\dot{m}c^2$.

We know the gravitational potential energy of a particle of mass m at the Schwarzschild radius is the same as the kinetic energy it has gained moving from infinity to that point, so

$E = \frac{GMm}{R_S} = \frac{GMm}{\frac{2GM}{c^2}} = \frac{1}{2}mc^2$ [1]

Given that a fraction η is converted into radiation and the given mass accretion rate then

$L = \eta\frac{\delta E}{\delta t} = \eta\times\frac{1}{2}\frac{\delta m}{\delta t}c^2 = \frac{1}{2}\eta\dot{m}c^2$ [1] **[2]**

(So the maximum energy you can get from a black hole is half the rest mass energy of the material falling in – this is a much more efficient process for generating energy than the 0.7% you get from nuclear fusion in stars, which in themselves are much more efficient than chemical reactions!)

*d. The typical mass accretion rate onto an active SMBH is ∼2* $\mathrm{M}_{\odot}$ *yr⁻¹ and the typical efficiency is η = 0.1. Calculate the typical luminosity of a quasar. Compare the luminosity of the quasar with the power needed to ionize the Voorwerp.*

Need to convert the mass accretion rate into kg s⁻¹ and then put into the formula

$L = \frac{1}{2}\eta\dot{m}c^2 = \frac{1}{2}\times0.1\times\left(\frac{2\times1.99\times10^{30}}{365.25\times24\times60\times60}\right)\times(3.00\times10^{8})^2$ [1]

$= 5.68\times10^{38}\ \mathrm{W}$ [1] **[2]**

The luminosity of the quasar is high enough to ionize the Voorwerp [1] **[1]**

*e. Calculate the projected physical separation,* $r_p$*, between the galaxy and the Voorwerp.*

Since the angle is so small, we can use the small angle approximation for tan θ ≈ θ

$20\ \mathrm{arcseconds} = 9.70\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{radians}$ [0.5]

$r_{\mathrm{p}} = \theta d = 9.70\times10^{-5}\times214\ \mathrm{Mpc}$ [0.5]

$= 20.8\ \mathrm{kpc}\ (= 6.42\times10^{20}\ \mathrm{m})$ [1] **[2]**

*f. Derive an expression for the difference in the light travel time between photons travelling directly to Earth from the galaxy and photons reflected off the Voorwerp first. Give your formula as a function of* $r_p$ *and θ, where θ is the angle between the lines of sight to the Earth and to the centre of the Voorwerp as measured by an observer at the centre of IC 2497. (For example θ = 90° would correspond to the galaxy and Voorwerp both being the exact same distance from the Earth, and so the projected distance* $r_p$ *is therefore also the true distance between them.)*

Given the small angular separation we can treat the light rays from the galaxy to Earth and from the Voorwerp to Earth as essentially parallel, and so the difference in light travel time comes from the extra distance travelled in being reflected off the Voorwerp

<figure className="problem-figure problem-figure--sized" style={{'--fig-w': '36.6%', '--fig-max': '235px'}}>
<img src="https://pub-43290baaaff14857b5dd59610ea438c7.r2.dev/problems/baao-2017-x/p4-sol-fig1.png" alt="Diagram: from the Galaxy a horizontal ray goes right towards Earth; a line x runs down-left from the Galaxy to the Voorwerp, making angle θ with the horizontal ray; from the Voorwerp a parallel horizontal ray goes right, with the segment y between the Voorwerp and the foot of the vertical r_p dropped from the Galaxy." width="733" height="513" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
</figure>

Relevant diagram, suitably labelled [2]

Extra distance = x + y

$x = \frac{r_p}{\cos(\theta-90^{\circ})} = \frac{r_p}{\sin\theta}$ [1]

$y = r_p\tan(\theta-90^{\circ}) = \frac{r_p}{-\tan\theta}$ [1]

$\therefore \text{extra time} = \frac{\text{extra distance}}{c} = \frac{r_p}{c}\left(\frac{1}{\sin\theta}-\frac{1}{\tan\theta}\right)$ [1] **[5]**

$= \frac{r_p}{c\sin\theta}(1-\cos\theta)$ (alternative form)

[Allow any equivalent formula, for example expressing it in terms of csc θ and cot θ, so long as some attempt has been made to simplify it. It is quicker and simpler to derive if θ is assumed to be acute – we show it this way in case students see that the angle is obtuse from the next part of the question and want to have a consistent picture throughout]

*g. High precision measurements showed that the Voorwerp is slightly further away than the galaxy, and so θ = 125°. Use this with your expression from the previous part of the question to estimate an upper limit for the number of years that have passed since the quasar was last active.*

$\Delta t = \frac{r_p}{c\sin\theta}(1-\cos\theta) = \frac{20.8\times10^{3}\times3.09\times10^{16}}{3.00\times10^{8}\times\sin125^{\circ}}(1-\cos125^{\circ})$

$= 4.11\times10^{12}\ \mathrm{s}$ [0.5]

$\approx 130\,000\ \mathrm{years}$ [0.5] **[1]**

(This is remarkably recent on astronomical timescales!)


</Spoiler>
