Most of the time, planets move from west to east relative to the background stars. Occasionally, however, they change direction and temporarily undergo retrograde motion. After a few weeks, the direction is changed again. The apparent motion of a planet was observed in Hangzhou this year. Table 1 lists the values of the 24 hour angular displacement of the planet relative to the background stars (simplified as ADRS) on the day that the observation was made. Modified Julian Day (MJD = JD − 2400000.5) are used for the dates. Minus means the direction is from east to west.

<ProblemSection id="part-6-1">

### 6.1.

Find the date (MJD) when the planet is at stationary point (simplified as STP) and at opposition (simplified as OPP) graphically, respectively. Accurate to one day. Write your answer in table 2.

</ProblemSection>

<ProblemSection id="part-6-2">

### 6.2.

What's the value of 24 hour angular displacement of the planet per day when it is at opposition? Write your answer in table 2.

</ProblemSection>

## Решение

<Spoiler title="Покажи официалното решение">

<ProblemSection id="solution-part-6-1">

### 1)

The plotted figure is shown as below.

$\sim 2\ \mathrm p$

At stationary points, the planet's angular displacement is zero. It's easy to find the two days of stationary points: MJD 54998 and MJD 55118.

$\sim 2\ \mathrm p$

From one stationary point to another, the opposition of the planet happens when the angular displacement of the planet relative to the background stars peaks. The date can be read out through figure 1: MJD 55058, i.e. the lowest point of figure 1.

$\sim 2\ \mathrm p$

</ProblemSection>

<ProblemSection id="solution-part-6-2">

### 2)

The angular displacement on MJD 55058 is about $\Delta=-0.132^\circ(\pm0.002^\circ)$.

$\sim 1\ \mathrm p$

| MJD of STP | MJD of OPP | ADRS on the day of OPP |
|---|---|---|
| $54998\pm2$ & $55118\pm2$ | $55058\pm2$ | $-0.132^\circ\pm0.002^\circ$ |

</ProblemSection>

<ProblemSection id="solution-part-6-3">

### 3)


<figure className="problem-figure problem-figure--sized" style={{'--fig-w': '100%', '--fig-max': '782px'}}>
<img src="https://pub-43290baaaff14857b5dd59610ea438c7.r2.dev/problems/iao-2009-practical-alpha-en/p6-sol-fig2.webp" alt="Official opposition geometry: planet, Earth, Sun and angular displacement." width="1222" height="755" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
</figure>


As shown in the figure above, when the planet is on opposition:

$$
\frac{v_E-v_p}{a_p-a_E}\times t=\tan\Delta\qquad (1)
$$

where $v$ and $a$ are the orbital speed and radius, $t$ is a short time interval from the moment of opposition. If we set the value of $t$ to one day, since $\Delta=0.132^\circ=0.002304\ll1$ (the value of $\Delta$ is found from figure 1), (1) can be rewritten as:

$$
v_E-v_p=(a_p-a_E)\cdot\Delta/t\qquad (2)
$$

According to Kepler's Law:

$$
a_E^3/T_E^2=a_p^3/T_p^2
$$

and

$$
v=2\pi\cdot a/T\ \Rightarrow\ a_p/a_E=v_E^2/v_p^2\qquad (3)
$$

Substitute the value of $a_E=1\ \mathrm{AU}$ into (2) and (3), we can find that:

$$
(v_p/v_E+1)\cdot\Delta=(v_p/v_E)^2\cdot v_E
$$

where $\Delta=0.002304$, $v_E=0.0172\ \mathrm{AU/day}$. Thus, $v_p=0.439v_E$, $a_p=5.2\ \mathrm{AU}\ (\pm0.2\ \mathrm{AU})$.

$\sim 3\ \mathrm p$

</ProblemSection>
