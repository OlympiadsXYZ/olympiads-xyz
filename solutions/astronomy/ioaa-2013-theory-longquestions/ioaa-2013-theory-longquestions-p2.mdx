*$7^{th}$ International Olympiad on Astronomy & Astrophysics · 27 July – 5 August 2013, Volos Greece · Theoretical Exam - Long Questions · 27 юли 2013 г.*

## Условие

Question 2

A spacecraft is orbiting the Near Earth Asteroid (2608) *Seneca* (staying continuously very close to the asteroid), transmitting pulsed data to the *Earth*. Due to the relative motion of the two bodies (the asteroid and the Earth) around the *Sun*, the time it takes for a pulse to arrive at the ground station varies approximately between 2 and 39 minutes. The orbits of the Earth and *Seneca* are coplanar. Assuming that the *Earth* moves around the *Sun* on a circular orbit (with radius $a_{\mathrm{Earth}}=1\ \mathrm{AU}$ and period $T_{\mathrm{Earth}}=1\ \mathrm{yr}$) and that the orbit of *Seneca* does not intersect the orbit of the *Earth*, calculate:

(1) the semi-major axis, $a_{\mathrm{Sen}}$ the eccentricity, $e_{\mathrm{Sen}}$ of *Seneca*’s orbit around the *Sun*

(2) the period of *Seneca*’s orbit, $T_{\mathrm{Sen}}$ and the average period between two consecutive oppositions, $T_{\mathrm{syn}}$ of the *Earth-Seneca* couple

(3) an approximate value for the mass of the planet Jupiter, $M_{\mathrm{Jup}}$ (assuming this is the only planet of our Solar system with non-negligible mass compared to the *Sun*). Assume that the presence of Jupiter does not influence the orbit of *Seneca*.

## Решение

<Spoiler title="Покажи официалното решение">

Answer :

(1) For $\Delta t_b=2\ \mathrm{min}=120\ \mathrm{sec}$, the distance travelled by a light pulse is $R_1=c\times\Delta t$ or $R_1=0.24\ \mathrm{AU}$, while for $\Delta t_a=39\ \mathrm{min}$ the maximum distance is $R_2=c\times\Delta t$ or $R_2=4.67\ \mathrm{AU}$.

(4 points)

Since the orbits do not intersect and the $R_2$ exceeds by far 1 AU, the orbit of *Seneca* is exterior to that of the *Earth*. $R_1$ corresponds to the minimum relative distance of the two bodies (i.e. at opposition), while $R_2$ corresponds to the maximum relative distance (i.e. at conjunction).

If $q=a\times(1-e)$ is the perihelion and $Q=a\times(1+e)$ the aphelion distance of *Seneca*, then $R_1=q-1\ \mathrm{AU}$ (the minimum distance of *Seneca* from the *Sun* minus the semi-major axis of the *Earth*’s orbit), while $R_2=Q+1\ \mathrm{AU}$ (the maximum distance of *Seneca* from the *Sun* plus the semi-major axis of the *Earth*’s orbit).

(6 points)

Thus,

$$
a_{\mathrm{Sen}}(1-e_{\mathrm{Sen}})=1+R_1=1.24\ \mathrm{AU}
$$
$$
a_{\mathrm{Sen}}(1+e_{\mathrm{Sen}})=R_2-1=3.67\ \mathrm{AU}
$$

from which one finds $a_{\mathrm{Sen}}\sim2.46\ \mathrm{AU}$

(2 point)

and $e_{\mathrm{Sen}}\sim0.49$

(2 points)

(figures rounded to 2 dec. digits)

(2) The period, $T_{\mathrm{Sen}}$, of *Seneca*’s orbit can be found by using Kepler’s 3rd law for *Seneca* and the *Earth* (ignoring their small masses, compared to the Sun’s):

$$
\frac{a_{\mathrm{Sen}}^3}{T_{\mathrm{Sen}}^2}=\frac{a_{\mathrm{Earth}}^3}{T_{\mathrm{Earth}}^2}=1
$$

(6 points)

from which one finds $T_{\mathrm{Sen}}\sim3.87\ \mathrm{yr}$

(2 point)

(Alternatively one can use Kepler’s law for *Seneca* only and use natural units for the mass of the *Sun* and $G$, i.e. $[\mathrm{mass}]=1\ M_{\mathrm{Sun}}$, $[t]=1\ \mathrm{yr}$ and $[r]=1\ \mathrm{AU}$, in which case $T_{\mathrm{Sen}}=a_{\mathrm{Sen}}^{3/2}=3.86\ \mathrm{yr}$)

(8 points)

Assuming non-retrogate orbit, the synodic period, $T_{\mathrm{syn}}$ of *Seneca* and Earth is given by

$$
1/T_{\mathrm{syn}} = 1/T_{\mathrm{Earh}} - 1/T_{\mathrm{Sen}} \quad (\textit{Seneca} \text{ is superior})
$$

(6 points)

which gives $T_{\mathrm{syn}} = 1.35\ \mathrm{yr}$

(2 point)

(3) From Kepler's law, we can calculate the mass of the $\textit{Sun}$

$$
4\pi^2 a_{\mathrm{Sen}}^3/T_{\mathrm{Sen}}^2 = G M_{\mathrm{Sun}},\ \text{i.e. } M_{\mathrm{Sun}} \sim 1.988\times10^{30}\ \mathrm{kg}
$$

(depending on accuracy)

(4 points)

Then, using Kepler's $3^{\mathrm{rd}}$ law for $\textit{Seneca}$ and $\textit{Jupiter}$, one gets

$$
\frac{a_{\mathrm{Jup}}^3/T_{\mathrm{Jup}}^2}{a_{\mathrm{Sen}}^3/T_{\mathrm{Sen}}^2}=\frac{M_{\mathrm{Sun}}+M_{\mathrm{Jup}}}{M_{\mathrm{Sun}}}=1+x
$$

(4 points)

where $x =$ is the mass ratio of $\textit{Jupiter}$ to the $\textit{Sun}$. Then, solving for $x$ we get (depending on the accuracy used in determining the elements of $\textit{Seneca's}$ orbit)

$$
x = 0.0016,\ \text{thus } M_{\mathrm{Jup}} = 3.2\times10^{27}\ \mathrm{kg}
$$

(4 points)


</Spoiler>
