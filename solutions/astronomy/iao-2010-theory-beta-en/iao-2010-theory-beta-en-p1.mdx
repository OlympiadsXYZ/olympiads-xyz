Usually we consider that there are about 6000 stars in the whole sky which are visible to the naked eye. The refraction near the horizon is $35'$. Find, how many more stars visible to the naked eye become circumpolar (which means that they never set) due to refraction:

<ProblemSection id="part-1-1">

### 1.1. (4 т.)

for observer on the North Pole at zero height position (like Polar Bear in a position with its eyes at zero height).

</ProblemSection>

<ProblemSection id="part-1-2">

### 1.2. (3 т.)

for observer on the Equator at zero height position (like Giraffe with the eyes lowered to zero level).

</ProblemSection>

<ProblemSection id="picture">

### Picture. (1 т.)

In your solution include a picture with an image of the Bear on the ices of North Pole and the Giraffe on the Equator, and necessary dimensions and/or angular dimensions used.

</ProblemSection>

## Решение

<Spoiler title="Покажи официалното решение">

Obviously, the problem is of estimation. So we will not take into account the effect of diminishing the number of visible stars due to weak transparency of the atmosphere near the horizon. Also, at first we will consider that stars are distributed more or less regularly in the sky.

<ProblemSection id="solution-part-1-1">

### 1.1.

The number of circumpolar stars at the North pole equals to a half of 6000, i.e. 3000. It is those stars whose declination is above $0^\circ$. Due to refraction the number of circumpolar stars is increased by those ones whose declination is below $0^\circ$ but above $-35'$. Let us estimate the ratio between the number of stars which are located in the range of declinations from $-35'$ to $0'$ to the number of stars of all the sphere. It is the same as the ratio between the solid angle of the belt between the declinations $-35'$ and $0'$ and the solid angle of whole sphere ($4\pi$). [It is good to draw a figure.]

It is more visual not to use solid angles but areas on an imagined sphere of radius $R_0$, and calculate the above ratio as the ratio between the area of the belt near the celestial equator with $35'$ width and the area of the whole sphere ($4\pi R_0^2$). The area of the belt is its length along the celestial equator ($2\pi R_0$), multiplied by its width $R_0\cdot35\cdot\pi/(180\cdot60)$ (where $\pi/(180\cdot60)$ is the value of an arc minute expressed in radians). The ratio between these areas is

$$
2\pi R_0\cdot R_0\cdot35\cdot\pi/(180\cdot60)/4\pi R_0^2=35\pi/21600.
$$

The number of stars in this belt may be estimated as

$$
6000\cdot35\pi/21600\approx30.5,
$$

but the accuracy even to one star here is obviously excessive, therefore it is necessary to say that about 30 stars become circumpolar due to refraction.

</ProblemSection>

<ProblemSection id="solution-part-1-2">

### 1.2.

One may use a similar procedure to find the answer for the question 1.2. of the problem. Due to refraction the stars placed closer $35'$ to the poles (both Northern and Southern), that is, with declinations above $+89^\circ25'$ or below $-89^\circ25'$, become circumpolar. The ratio between the area of these slices of imagined heavenly sphere (area of each slice is $\pi\cdot(35')^2=\pi\cdot(35\pi/(180\cdot60))^2$) and the area of the whole sphere $4\pi$ is $5.2\cdot10^{-5}$. By multiplying this value by 6000 we may get approximately 0.31 star. [And some participants just have written: there will be about 0.3 new circumpolar stars.] But what does this value mean? It means that the model of uniform distribution of stars on heavenly sphere does not work any more. Any stars ("one star", "two stars" etc.) may be present in these areas of the sky, or may not be present, but it can not be “half present”. And the right answer may be given at once (without any calculations!): **no star**, as we know that there are no stars visible with the naked eye star in radius $35'$ near to poles. The nearest one is Polaris, it is situated approximately $45'$ from the North Pole.

</ProblemSection>
