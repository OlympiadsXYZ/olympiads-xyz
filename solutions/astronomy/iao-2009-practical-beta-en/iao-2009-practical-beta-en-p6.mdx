Light echoes are produced when the initial flash from a rapidly brightening object (such as a nova) is reflected by the surrounding interstellar dusts which may or may not be associated with the object. Light from the initial flash arrives at the viewer first, while light reflected by the dusts begins to arrive shortly afterward. The variable star V838 Monocerotis was seen to have a violent outburst in March 09, 2002 (Modified Julian Day 52342).

Then the Hubble Space Telescope (HST) captured its light echoes from the surrounding dusts in the next few months. Figure 1 presents two photos observed by HST. The black box shows a field of view of $97''\times97''$.

<ProblemSection id="solution-part-6-1">

### 1)

The intrinsic color of the stars is $B_0-V_0=(B-A_B)-(V-A_V)$, and the $M_V$ can be obtained through the H-R diagram.

| Number | Spectral type | V | B | $(B-V)_0$ | $V_0$ | $M_V$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| N1 | B6 V | 16.02 | 16.73 | -0.15 | 13.42 | $-0.5\pm0.2$ |
| N2 | B4 V | 15.00 | 15.63 | -0.21 | 12.40 | $-1.1\pm0.2$ |
| N3 | B3 V | 14.79 | 15.41 | -0.22 | 12.19 | $-1.3\pm0.2$ |

$\sim 1\ \mathrm p$

According to $m-M=5\lg r-5$, we can get the distance modules of the three stars. And the distance of the cluster is the mean value of these stars, i.e. $d\approx18\ \mathrm{kly}\ (\pm2\ \mathrm{kly})$.

$\sim 2\ \mathrm p$

</ProblemSection>

<ProblemSection id="solution-part-6-2">

### 2)

This question is a hint to remind the students that the shells in figure 1 are not envelopes of V838 Mon but interstellar dusts that brightened by the outburst of V838 Mon.

<figure className="problem-figure problem-figure--sized" style={{'--fig-w': '100%', '--fig-max': '1041px'}}>
<img src="https://pub-43290baaaff14857b5dd59610ea438c7.r2.dev/problems/iao-2009-practical-beta-en/p6-sol-fig1-v3.webp" alt="Official fitted velocity curve for V838 Mon." width="1627" height="818" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
</figure>


$\sim 2\ \mathrm p$

The graph is plotted here. The black solid line is a fitted curve through the data in table 2.

The area of the curve in figure 1 indicates the distance that the envelope expanded. The area of a unit rectangle is:

$$
A=25\ \mathrm{km/s}*50\ \mathrm{day}=1.08*10^8\ \mathrm{km}=0.72\ \mathrm{AU}
$$

The julian day of May 20, 2002 and Sep. 2, 2002 is 52414 and 52519. It can be counted in the figure that on May 20, 2002 and Sep. 2, 2002, the envelope expands about $34A(25\ \mathrm{AU})$ and $78A(56\ \mathrm{AU})$. Thus, the angular diameters of the envelopes are respectively:

$$
\beta_1=2*25\ \mathrm{AU}/18000\ \mathrm{ly}\approx0.01''
$$

$$
\beta_2=2*56\ \mathrm{AU}/18000\ \mathrm{ly}\approx0.02''
$$

$\sim 2\ \mathrm p$

</ProblemSection>

<ProblemSection id="solution-part-6-3">

### 3)

Let "0" be the time of the observation of the original outburst, i.e. March 09, 2002, and $t$ be the time when the outermost light echoes were observed. The light echos delayed because of the speed of light is limited, as displayed by the following figure.

<figure className="problem-figure problem-figure--sized" style={{'--fig-w': '100%', '--fig-max': '922px'}}>
<img src="https://pub-43290baaaff14857b5dd59610ea438c7.r2.dev/problems/iao-2009-practical-beta-en/p6-sol-fig2.webp" alt="Official light-echo geometry: V838, dusts and Earth." width="1440" height="299" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
</figure>


From the figure, we get:

$$
ct+d=r+x\qquad (1)
$$

$$
r^2=x^2+d^2-2xd\cdot\cos\alpha\qquad (2)
$$

Then we can find:

$$
r=\frac{d(d+ct)(1-\cos\alpha)+c^2t^2/2}{d(1-\cos\alpha)+ct}
$$

where $\alpha$ is the visual radius of the outermost dusts shell and can be measured from the HST figure, $c$ is the speed of light, $d$ is the distance of the star. $2\alpha_1=(21.9-12.0)/22.4\times97''=42.9''$, $2\alpha_2=(59.1-45.1)/22.4\times97''=60.6''$. The final result is listed in the following table.

| time (days from 3/9/2002) | Diameter of stellar envelope (″) | Visual diameter of dust shell (″) | Radius of dust shell (ly) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 72 (0.1971 year) | 0.01 | 40 (±5) | 9.0 |
| 177 (0.4846 year) | 0.02 | 60 (±5) | 7.5 |

$\sim 3\ \mathrm p$

</ProblemSection>
