### A. Group β — rotational velocity

Spectrum of Jupiter (Fig. 1) was obtained when the slit of a spectrograph was aligned along the planet’s equator. Wavelengths ($\boldsymbol{\lambda}$) of several recognized lines are shown. Due to Jupiter rotation the reflected Sunlight was affected by the Doppler effect. The spectral lines become inclined, because the spectrum of light reflected from the receding part of Jupiter is red-shifted, and of light reflected from the approaching part is blue-shifted. Non-inclined lines, which are visible in the spectrum, were formed in the Earth atmosphere.

## Решение

<Spoiler title="Покажи официалното решение">

Official solution tables and formulae. Part A calculations apply to group β; group α was given the same approximate rotational speed directly. Parts B and C are shared. Millimetre measurements refer to the original printed sheets, not resized screen images.

<ProblemSection id="solution-solution-a">

### A. Rotational velocity — group β

**Table A**

$$
N\ [\mathrm{nm/mm}]=(659.39-654.62)/215.5\approx0.0221.
$$

| $\lambda$ (nm) | dx (mm) | $d\lambda$ (nm) | $v_r$ (km/s) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 654.62 | ~5 | ~0.1105 | ~12.65 |
| 656.92 | ~5 | ~0.1105 | ~12.61 |
| 659.26 | ~5 | ~0.1105 | ~12.56 |

$$
v_{r\_avg}\ [\mathrm{km/s}]=\boldsymbol{\sim12.6}.
$$

**Formulae**

Because of the Doppler effect the wavelength shift, $d\lambda$, observed at the edge of Jupiter’s disk can be obtained through the following formula:

$$
v_r=\frac14\cdot\frac{d\lambda}{\lambda}\cdot c,
$$

where c — speed of light in the vacuum.

Coefficient $\frac14$ comes into effect due to the fact that:

- the sunlight, illuminating the planet, is reflected, therefore it was affected by Doppler effect twice;
- measurements are done at the edges of the disk and not relative to the non-shifted center, thus doubling the measured shift value.

</ProblemSection>

**Formulae**

$$
\varphi=\arcsin\left(\frac{x_1}{L_x}\right)+\arcsin\left(\frac{x_2}{L_x}\right),
$$

$$
P=\frac{360^\circ}{3600\ \mathrm s}\times\frac{\mathrm{dt}}\varphi,
$$

$$
R_{Je}=\frac{v_r\cdot P_{Je}}{2\pi}.
$$

*Source note: the table labels the period in hours and velocity in km/s; the final radius formula requires consistent time units. The supplied formula is retained without adding an unprinted conversion factor.*

### C. Mass and density

**Table C**

| Moon | $P_m$ (h) | $a_{Je}$ | a (m) | $M_J$ (kg) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | ~43 | ~5.9 | ~$4.22\times10^8$ | ~$1.86\times10^{27}$ |
| 2 | ~85 | ~9.4 | ~$6.70\times10^8$ | ~$1.90\times10^{27}$ |
| 3 | ~172 | ~14.9 | ~$10.65\times10^8$ | ~$1.86\times10^{27}$ |

$$
M_{J\_avg}\ [\mathrm{kg}]=\boldsymbol{\sim1.87\times10^{27}}.
$$

| Quantity | Result |
| --- | --- |
| $R_p/R_e$ | 174 mm / 186 mm ≈ 0.9355 |
| $R_{J\_avg}$ (m) | ~$69900\times10^3$ |
| V (m³) | ~$1.43\times10^{24}$ |
| $\rho_J$ (kg/m³) | ~1310 |

**Formulae**

In case of a moon orbiting a much more massive planet, the mass of the central body (planet) can be obtained from Kepler’s Third Law:

$$
M_J=\frac{4\pi^2}{G}\cdot\frac{a^3}{P^2},
$$

where G — gravitational constant, a — semi-major axis of moon orbit in meters, P — period in seconds.

The mean radius for a slightly oblate ellipsoid is:

$$
R_{J\_avr}=\sqrt[3]{\frac{R_p}{R_e}R_eR_e^2}=R_e\times\sqrt[3]{R_p/R_e}.
$$

*Source note: the blank answer form asks for the mean radius in km, while the solution table gives it in m. Both unit labels are preserved. The formula uses the printed suffix “avr”, while the tables use “avg”.*
