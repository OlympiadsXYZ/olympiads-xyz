## Условие

On July 14, 2023, India’s lunar mission satellite, *Chandrayaan-3*, was successfully launched by the Indian Space Research Organization (ISRO). *Chandrayaan-3* (mass $m=3900\ \mathrm{kg}$) was taken to the Moon through a series of Earth Bound Manoeuvres (elliptical) orbits (EBNs) as depicted in the figure below. In this problem, we will explore the physics governing some part of its journey, employing a simplified model. For all parts of this problem except part (f), we consider *Chandrayaan-3* to be moving only under the influence of Earth’s gravity (a central force).

**a)** Upon launch, *Chandrayaan-3* entered an elliptical orbit around Earth, with Earth at one of the foci (E) as shown below. The points P and A are the perigee (nearest point from the Earth) and apogee (farthest point from the Earth), respectively. We introduce the polar coordinate system $(r,\theta)$, where $\vec r$ is the vector from the centre of the Earth (origin) to the satellite, and $\theta$ is the angle that $\vec r$ makes with the major axis (PA = $2a$). The directions of unit vectors $\hat r$ and $\hat\theta$ are shown in the figure.

<figure className="problem-figure problem-figure--sized" style={{'--fig-w': '44.6%', '--fig-max': '286px'}}>
<img src="https://pub-43290baaaff14857b5dd59610ea438c7.r2.dev/problems/inpho-2024-question/p3-orbit.png" alt="WholeellipsewithP,E,A,r,r-vector,theta,radialandtangentialunitvectorarrows." width="893" height="632" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
</figure>


The equation of the ellipse can be written in polar coordinates as

$$
r=\frac{r_0}{(1-e\cos\theta)}
$$

where $e$ is eccentricity of the orbit $(0<e<1)$ and $r_0$ is called the latus rectum. The velocity $\vec v$ of the satellite in polar coordinates can be written as

$$
\vec v=v_r\hat r+v_t\hat\theta=\dot r\hat r+r\dot\theta\hat\theta
$$

where $v_r=\dot r$ is the “radial” speed and $v_t=r\dot\theta$ is the “tangential” speed.

Make schematic plots of the speeds $v_r$ and $v_t$ as functions of $\theta$ over one full orbit. Mark any significant points in the plots in terms of $a,e$, and other variables. **[6 т.]**

**b)** Obtain an expression for the total energy $(E)$ of the orbiting satellite in terms of $a$ and other constants. **[1,5 т.]**

**c)** Plot the kinetic energy (KE) of the satellite as a function of $\theta$ over one full orbit. Mark any significant points in terms of $a,e$, and other variables. **[1 т.]**

**d)** The perigee and apogee of the elliptical orbit in part (a) are 200 km and 36500 km, respectively. It is generally described as a (200×36500) km orbit. Here the distances are defined from the surface of the Earth. Calculate the period of rotation $T$ (in hr) of *Chandrayaan-3* in this orbit. **[1,5 т.]**

**e)** To move *Chandrayaan-3* from the first orbit (in part (d)) to another elliptical orbit EBN-1, an instantaneous boost was applied at perigee by changing the velocity by $\Delta v$, without altering the direction. This changed the apogee to 41800 km above Earth’s surface while keeping the perigee unchanged. Calculate $\Delta v$. **[2,5 т.]**

**f)** After a series of manoeuvres, *Chandrayaan-3* was placed in an elliptical orbit of (100 × 1437) km around the Moon. Here, the distances are calculated from the surface of the Moon. Calculate the change in velocity $\Delta v'$, applied at the perigee, that is required to bring *Chandrayaan-3* from this elliptical orbit to a circular orbit at a distance of 100 km from the surface of the Moon. For this part, assume that *Chandrayaan-3* is only under the influence of the Moon’s gravitational field. **[1,5 т.]**

## Решение

<Spoiler title="Покажи официалното решение">

**(a) Solution:** It is given that

$$
r=\frac{r_0}{1-e\cos\theta}\qquad (3.1)
$$

$$
\therefore v_r=\dot r=-\frac{r_0e\sin\theta\dot\theta}{(1-e\cos\theta)^2}\qquad (3.2)
$$

Since the force is central, angular momentum $l$ is conserved. The conserved angular momentum is given by

$$
l=mr^2\dot\theta\qquad (3.3)
$$

$$
\implies\dot\theta=\frac l{mr^2}\qquad (3.4)
$$

Substituting above equation and expression of $r$ in $v_r$, after simplifying, we get

$$
v_r=-\frac{el\sin\theta}{mr_0}\qquad (3.5)
$$

We know that

$$
v_t=\frac l{mr}\qquad (3.6)
$$

Substituting the value of $r$, we get

$$
v_t=\frac{l(1-e\cos\theta)}{mr_0}\qquad (3.7)
$$

From above equation, the value of $v_t$ is maximum, when $\cos\theta$ is minimum. i.e. $\theta=-\pi$. The maximum value of $v_t$

$$
v_{t\max}=\frac{l(1+e)}{mr_0}\qquad (3.8)
$$

From Eq. (3.7), $v_t$ is minimum, when $\cos\theta$ is maximum. i.e $\theta=0$. The minimum value of $v_t$

$$
v_{t\min}=\frac{l(1-e)}{mr_0}\qquad (3.9)
$$

Since the velocities at P and A are purely tangential, and P is closer to earth as compared to A, hence the tangential speed is maximum at P. Let the velocity of the satellite in the given orbit at P be $v_p$ and velocity at A be $v_a$. Conservation of energy gives:

$$
\frac12mv_a^2-\frac{GMm}{r_a}=\frac12mv_p^2-\frac{GMm}{r_p}\qquad (3.10)
$$

Conservation of angular momentum gives

$$
mv_ar_a=mv_pr_p\qquad (3.11)
$$

$$
v_p=v_a\frac{r_a}{r_p}\qquad (3.12)
$$

Substituting the above equation into the energy conservation equation, we get

$$
v_p=\sqrt{\frac{GM}a\frac{1+e}{1-e}}\qquad (3.13)
$$

Similarly

$$
v_a=\sqrt{\frac{GM}a\frac{1-e}{1+e}}\qquad (3.14)
$$

Hence the conserved angular momentum can be written as

$$
l=mv_ar_a=mv_ar_a\qquad (3.15)
$$

On simplification, we get

$$
l=mr_o\sqrt{\frac{GM}{a(1-e^2)}}\qquad (3.16)
$$

$v_r$ and $v_t$ can also be written as

$$
v_r=-e\sqrt{\frac{GM}{a(1-e^2)}}\sin\theta\qquad (3.17)
$$

$$
v_t=\sqrt{\frac{GM}{a(1-e^2)}}(1-e\cos\theta)\qquad (3.18)
$$

Similarly $v_{r\max}$, $v_{r\min}$, $v_{t\max}$ and $v_{t\min}$ can also be written as

$$
v_{r\max}=e\sqrt{\frac{GM}{a(1-e^2)}}\quad\text{at }\theta=3\pi/2\qquad (3.19)
$$

$$
v_{r\min}=-e\sqrt{\frac{GM}{a(1-e^2)}}\quad\text{at }\theta=\pi/2\qquad (3.20)
$$

$$
v_{t\max}=\sqrt{\frac{GM}{a(1-e^2)}}(1+e)=\sqrt{\frac{GM(1+e)}{a(1-e)}}\quad\text{at }\theta=\pi\qquad (3.21)
$$

$$
v_{t\min}=\sqrt{\frac{GM}{a(1-e^2)}}(1-e)=\sqrt{\frac{GM(1-e)}{a(1+e)}}\quad\text{at }\theta=0\text{ and }2\pi\qquad (3.22)
$$

**(b) Solution:** Let

$$
\frac{v_a}{r_p}=\frac{v_p}{r_a}=C\qquad (3.23)
$$

Putting Eq. (3.23) in Eq. (3.10), we get

$$
\frac12mC^2r_p^2-\frac{GMm}{r_a}=\frac12mC^2r_a^2-\frac{GMm}{r_p}\qquad (3.24)
$$

solving above equation, we get

$$
C^2=\frac{2GM}{(r_p+r_a)r_ar_p}\qquad (3.25)
$$

Again using Eq. (3.23) and value of $C^2$ from above equation in LHS of the Eq. (3.24), we get

$$
E=\frac12m\frac{2GMr_p^2}{r_ar_p(r_a+r_a)}-\frac{GMm}{r_a}\qquad (3.26)
$$

Solving above equation, and substituting $r_a+r_p=2a$, we get

$$
E=-\frac{GMm}{2a}\qquad (3.27)
$$

**(c) Solution:**

$$
KE=\frac12mv^2\qquad (3.28)
$$

where $v^2=v_r^2+v_t^2$. Substituting the value of $v_r$ and $v_t$ in the above equation, we get

$$
KE=\frac{GMm(1+e^2-2e\cos\theta)}{a(1-e^2)}\qquad (3.29)
$$

For $\theta=0$, $KE=KE_{\min}=\frac{GMm(1-e)}{a(1+e)}$, and for $\theta=\pi$ $KE=KE_{\max}=\frac{GMm(1+e)}{a(1-e)}$.

**(d) Solution:** The period of the orbit is given by

$$
T^2=\frac{4\pi^2}{GM}a^3\qquad (3.30)
$$

where $a=\frac{r_p+r_a}2$, where $r_p$ is perigee distance given by $r_p=200\ \mathrm{km}+R_E$ and $r_a$ is apogee distance given by $r_a=18350\ \mathrm{km}+R_E$ and i.e. $a=6371+\frac{(200+36500)\ \mathrm{km}}2=24721\ \mathrm{km}$.

$$
T=\sqrt{\frac{4\times\pi^2}{6.67\times10^{-11}\times5.972\times10^{24}}24721^3\times10^9}\qquad (3.31)
$$

$$
=10.75\ \mathrm{hr}\qquad (3.32)
$$

**(e) Solution:** since the velocities at P and A are purely tangential, and P is closer to earth as compared to A, hence the tangential speed is maximum at P. Let the velocity of the satellite in the given orbit at P be $v_p$ and velocity at A be $v_a$. Conservation of energy gives:

$$
\frac12mv_a^2-\frac{GMm}{r_a}=\frac12mv_p^2-\frac{GMm}{r_p}\qquad (3.33)
$$

Conservation of angular momentum gives

$$
mv_ar_a=mv_pr_p\qquad (3.34)
$$

$$
v_p=v_a\frac{r_a}{r_p}\qquad (3.35)
$$

Substituting the above equation into the energy conservation equation, we get

$$
v_p=\sqrt{\frac{2GMr_a}{r_p(r_p+r_a)}}\qquad (3.36)
$$

$$
v_p=\sqrt{\frac{GMr_a}{ar_p}}\qquad (3.37)
$$

$$
v_p=\sqrt{\frac{GM}a\frac{1+e}{1-e}}\qquad (3.38)
$$

Similarly

$$
v_a=\sqrt{\frac{2GMr_p}{r_a(r_p+r_a)}}\qquad (3.39)
$$

$$
v_a=\sqrt{\frac{GMr_p}{ar_a}}\qquad (3.40)
$$

$$
v_a=\sqrt{\frac{GM}a\frac{1-e}{1+e}}\qquad (3.41)
$$

From Eq. (3.36)

$$
v_p=\sqrt{\frac{2GMr_a}{r_p(r_p+r_a)}}\qquad (3.42)
$$

$$
v_p=10.251\ \mathrm{km/s}\qquad (3.43)
$$

In the new orbit, the perigee distance is kept the same, and the apogee distance changed to 41800 km, hence $r_a=41800+R_E=48171\ \mathrm{km}$ and $r_p$ remains as it is. Hence, the new velocity at the perigee distance is $v'_p$ Using the expression Eq. (3.36), the $v'_p$ can be written as

$$
v'_p=\sqrt{\frac{2GMr_a}{r_p(r'_a+r_p)}}\qquad (3.44)
$$

$$
v'_p=10.327\ \mathrm{km/s}\qquad (3.45)
$$

Now the boost required $\Delta v$ at this position would be

$$
\Delta v=v'_p-v_p\qquad (3.46)
$$

$$
\Delta v=0.076\ \mathrm{km/s}\qquad (3.47)
$$

**(f) Solution:** Let the apogee distance be $r_{ma}=R_m+1437\ \mathrm{km}$ and perigee distance be $r_{mp}=R_m+100\ \mathrm{km}$ The velocity of the satellite at perigee, when it is in the elliptic orbit, is $v_{mp}$ and the velocity when it is in the circular orbit is $v_m$ we know that

$$
v_{mp}=\sqrt{\frac{2GM_Mr_{ma}}{r_{mp}(r_{ma}+r_{mp})}}\qquad (3.48)
$$

$$
v_{mp}=1.838\ \mathrm{km/s}\qquad (3.49)
$$

Similarly here apogee distance be $r_{ma}=R_m+100\ \mathrm{km}$ and perigee distance be $r_{mp}=R_m+100\ \mathrm{km}$. Let $r_m=r_{ma}=r_{mp}$

$$
v_m=\sqrt{\frac{2GM_mr_m}{r_m(r_m+r_m)}}\qquad (3.50)
$$

$$
v_m=1.633\ \mathrm{km/s}\qquad (3.51)
$$

(3.52)

The boost required $\Delta v'$

$$
\Delta v'=v_m-v_{mp}\qquad (3.53)
$$

$$
\Delta v'=-0.205\ \mathrm{km/s}\qquad (3.54)
$$
