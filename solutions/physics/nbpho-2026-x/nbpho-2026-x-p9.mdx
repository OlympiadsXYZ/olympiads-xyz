## Условие

— *Jaan Kalda.* A fire extinguisher contains liquid CO$_2$ in equilibrium with its saturated vapour at room temperature $T_0 = 298\ \mathrm{K}$. Consider two scenarios: **(a)** the container is held upside-down so that the liquid phase flows to the nozzle; **(b)** it is held upright so that the saturated vapour flows to the nozzle. The nozzle has the shape of a converging–diverging channel (see figure), and the flow through it can be modelled as reversible and adiabatic. Atmospheric pressure is $p_{\mathrm{atm}} = 1.0 \times 10^5\ \mathrm{Pa}$; the CO$_2$ triple point is at $(T_{\mathrm{t}}, p_{\mathrm{t}}) = (216.6\ \mathrm{K}, 0.518\ \mathrm{MPa})$.

**i)** Identify the phase or phases present and their temperature in the stream emerging from the nozzle. **[1,5 т.]**

**ii)** Find the mass fraction $x$ of solid CO$_2$ in the stream for both scenarios. **[3,5 т.]**

**iii)** Now consider an arbitrary pure substance in equilibrium with its saturated vapour at temperature $T$, and suppose that only the vapour (not the liquid) escapes through a nozzle, undergoing reversible adiabatic expansion. Under what condition on the vaporisation latent heat $L$ (per unit mass), the isobaric specific heat of the vapour $c_p$, and the temperature $T$ does a fraction of the escaping vapour condense into droplets, even for a vanishingly small pressure drop? Does condensation occur for water vapour at $T = 373\ \mathrm{K}$ ($L \approx 2260\ \mathrm{kJ \cdot kg^{-1}}$, $c_p \approx 2.0\ \mathrm{kJ \cdot kg^{-1} \cdot K^{-1}}$)? **[3 т.]**

## Решение

<Spoiler title="Покажи официалното решение">

— *Solution by Jaan Kalda.*

<ProblemSection id="solution-part-i">

### i) (1,5 т.)

At atmospheric pressure $p_{\mathrm{atm}} = 1.0 \times 10^5\ \mathrm{Pa}$, which is below the triple-point pressure $p_t = 5.18 \times 10^5\ \mathrm{Pa}$, no liquid CO$_2$ can exist. The exit stream is therefore either pure vapour, pure solid, or a solid–vapour mixture. Pure solid is impossible: a rigid (incompressible) solid cannot expand or accelerate through a converging–diverging nozzle. So the exit is either pure vapour, or a solid–vapour mixture; in the latter case the temperature must be the sublimation temperature at 1 atm, $T_{\mathrm{sub}} \approx 195\ \mathrm{K}$ ($\approx -78\ {}^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$).

Tracing the isentrope from the initial state to $p = 1\ \mathrm{bar}$ on the $T$-$s$ diagram (provided): for both scenarios (a) and (b), the trajectory crosses the saturation boundary into the two-phase region. Hence the stream is a solid–vapour mixture at $T \approx 195\ \mathrm{K}$ in both cases.

**Grading.**

- Recognising that liquid is impossible at $p_{\mathrm{atm}} < p_t$, so the stream is solid, vapour, or a mixture **0.5 pts**
- Excluding pure solid (cannot adiabatically expand through nozzle) **0.2 pts**
- If solid–vapour coexistence, identifying $T = T_{\mathrm{sub}}(1\ \mathrm{atm}) \approx 195\ \mathrm{K}$ ($\approx -78\ {}^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$), within $\pm 10\ \mathrm{K}$ **0.3 pts**
- Tracing the isentrope on the $T$-$s$ diagram and concluding the stream is in the two-phase region for one scenario **0.3 pts**
- Two-phase for both scenarios **0.2 pts**

</ProblemSection>

<ProblemSection id="solution-part-ii">

### ii) (3,5 т.)

The expansion through the nozzle is reversible and adiabatic, so it is *isentropic*: the entropy per unit mass $s$ is conserved between the inlet and the exit. On the $T$–$s$ diagram, the trajectory is therefore vertical (constant $s$) until it crosses a phase boundary or a chosen isobar.

The exit pressure is $p_{\mathrm{atm}} = 1\ \mathrm{bar}$, well below the triple-point pressure $p_t = 5.18\ \mathrm{bar}$. From part (i), the exit state is in the two-phase (solid+vapour) region at $T_{\mathrm{sub}}(1\ \mathrm{bar}) \approx 195\ \mathrm{K}$ ($-78\ {}^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$).

**Lever rule.** In the two-phase region at fixed $p$, $T$, the total entropy per unit mass for a mixture with mass fraction $x$ of solid (and $1 - x$ vapour) is

$$
s_{\mathrm{end}} = x\,s_{\mathrm{sol}} + (1 - x)\,s_{\mathrm{vap}},
$$

$$
x = \frac{s_{\mathrm{vap}} - s_{\mathrm{end}}}{s_{\mathrm{vap}} - s_{\mathrm{sol}}},
$$

where $s_{\mathrm{sol}}$ and $s_{\mathrm{vap}}$ are the entropies of pure solid and pure vapour at the boundaries of the two-phase region at $p = 1\ \mathrm{bar}$.

**Reading entropies from the $T$–$s$ diagram.** At $p = 1\ \mathrm{bar}$ (sublimation isobar at $T \approx -78\ {}^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$), the boundaries are

$$
s_{\mathrm{sol}} \approx 400(50)\,\frac{\mathrm{J}}{\mathrm{kg \cdot K}}, \quad s_{\mathrm{vap}} \approx 3250(50)\,\frac{\mathrm{J}}{\mathrm{kg \cdot K}}.
$$

The initial entropies for the two scenarios are read at $T_0 = 298\ \mathrm{K}$ ($25\ {}^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$) on the saturation dome:

- Scenario (a), saturated liquid at $T_0$: $s_a \approx 2100(50)\,\frac{\mathrm{J}}{\mathrm{kg \cdot K}}$.
- Scenario (b), saturated vapour at $T_0$: $s_b \approx 2550(50)\,\frac{\mathrm{J}}{\mathrm{kg \cdot K}}$.

**Mass fractions.** Applying the lever rule to each:

$$
x_a = \frac{3250 - 2100}{3250 - 400} = \frac{1150}{2850} \approx 0.40,
$$

$$
x_b = \frac{3250 - 2550}{3250 - 400} = \frac{700}{2850} \approx 0.25.
$$

So the liquid-fed extinguisher (a) produces a stream that is $\sim 40\%$ dry ice (and $60\%$ vapour) by mass, while the vapour-fed extinguisher (b) produces a stream that is only $\sim 25\%$ dry ice. The liquid scenario yields about 1.6 times more solid CO$_2$ per unit mass discharged — which is why fire extinguishers are designed to draw liquid.

**Grading.**

- Recognising that “reversible adiabatic” means isentropic ($s = \mathrm{const}$), so the endpoint is found by tracing vertically downward on the $T$–$s$ diagram **0.6 pts**
- Deriving the lever rule for mass fraction $x$ in the two-phase region: $x = (s_{\mathrm{vap}} - s_{\mathrm{end}})/(s_{\mathrm{vap}} - s_{\mathrm{sol}})$ **0.7 pts**
- Reading the initial entropy $s_a$ from the saturated-liquid line at $T_0 = 298\ \mathrm{K}$ (scenario a) **0.5 pts**
- Reading the initial entropy $s_b$ from the saturated-vapour line at $T_0$ (scenario b) **0.5 pts**
- Reading $s_{\mathrm{sol}}$ at the 1-bar solid boundary **0.3 pts**
- Reading $s_{\mathrm{vap}}$ at the 1-bar vapour boundary **0.3 pts**
- Computing $x_a \approx 0.40 \pm 0.05$ for scenario (a) **0.3 pts**
- Computing $x_b \approx 0.25 \pm 0.05$ for scenario (b) **0.3 pts**

</ProblemSection>

<ProblemSection id="solution-part-iii">

### iii) (3 т.)

Consider an infinitesimal isentropic (reversible adiabatic) expansion of the saturated vapour from $(T, p_{\mathrm{sat}}(T))$. Two trajectories meet at this point: the *saturation curve* $p_{\mathrm{sat}}(T)$, and the *adiabat* (constant entropy). The expansion follows the adiabat. If the adiabat falls *below* the saturation curve in $T$-$p$ space (i.e., the vapour cools more slowly than the saturation temperature drops with pressure), then a finite fraction of the vapour condenses into droplets. Otherwise the expansion remains in the pure-vapour region.

**Saturation slope (Clausius–Clapeyron).** The saturated vapour above a liquid has number density $n_{\mathrm{vap}}$ in thermal equilibrium with the liquid. By the Boltzmann distribution, the probability of a molecule occupying the vapour state (energy $\ell$ per molecule above the liquid) versus the liquid state is

$$
\frac{n_{\mathrm{vap}}}{n_{\mathrm{liq}}} \propto \exp\left(-\frac{\ell}{k_B T}\right).
$$

Treating $n_{\mathrm{liq}}$ as $T$-independent and using $p_{\mathrm{sat}} = n_{\mathrm{vap}} k_B T \approx (\mathrm{const}) \cdot T \cdot \exp(-\ell/k_B T)$, the dominant temperature dependence is exponential. Per unit mass, $\ell/k_B = L/R_s$ where $R_s$ is the specific gas constant of the vapour. Differentiating $\ln p_{\mathrm{sat}}$:

$$
\frac{1}{p_{\mathrm{sat}}}\frac{\mathrm{d}p_{\mathrm{sat}}}{\mathrm{d}T} = \frac{L}{R_s T^2}.
$$

Equivalently,

$$
\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}T}{\mathrm{d}p}\right|_{\mathrm{sat}} = \frac{R_s T^2}{pL}.
$$

**Adiabat slope.** For the ideal-gas adiabat $Tp^{(1-\gamma)/\gamma} = \mathrm{const}$, with $R_s = c_p - c_v$ and $\gamma = c_p/c_v$:

$$
\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}T}{\mathrm{d}p}\right|_{\mathrm{adiab}} = \frac{R_s T}{c_p p}.
$$

**Condensation condition.** The vapour condenses on expansion iff the adiabat cools less than the saturation curve, i.e. $\frac{\mathrm{d}T}{\mathrm{d}p}|_{\mathrm{adiab}} < \frac{\mathrm{d}T}{\mathrm{d}p}|_{\mathrm{sat}}$:

$$
\frac{R_s T}{c_p p} < \frac{R_s T^2}{pL},
$$

$$
\boxed{L > c_p T}.
$$

**Numerical check (water at $T = 373\ \mathrm{K}$).** $L = 2260\ \frac{\mathrm{kJ}}{\mathrm{kg}}$, $c_p = 2.0\ \frac{\mathrm{kJ}}{\mathrm{kg \cdot K}}$:

$$
\frac{L}{c_p T} = \frac{2260}{2.0 \cdot 373} \approx 3.0 > 1.
$$

So water vapour does condense on isentropic expansion. This is precisely why steam from a kettle appears as a visible white plume of fine droplets, rather than as invisible pure water vapour.

</ProblemSection>
