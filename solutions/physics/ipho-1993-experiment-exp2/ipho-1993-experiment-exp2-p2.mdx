## Условие

This experiment has two parts:

**Part 1:** To determine the absolute magnitude $\mu_X$ of the magnetic moment of a small cylindrical permanent magnet, contained in the envelope marked “X”. (A similar magnet, also needed for the experiment, is contained in the envelope marked “A”.)

**Part 2:** To investigate the magnetic field of a given axially symmetric distribution of magnets, contained in the envelope marked “B”.

In your experiments, you should make use of the following facts:

1. The magnetic field $\mathbf B$ produced by a dipole magnet at a point along its axis at distance $x$ from its center is parallel to that axis and of strength given by:

$$
B(x)=\frac{2\mu K}{x^3},
$$

where $B$ is in Tesla [= N/(A m)], $K=10^{-7}\ \mathrm{Tesla\ m/A}$, $x$ is in m, and $\mu$ is in $\mathrm{A\ m^2}$.

2. The period of small torsional (angular) oscillations of a horizontal freely suspended magnet, such as a compass needle in the Earth's magnetic field, is given by:

$$
T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac I{\mu B_h}},
$$

where $B_h$ is the horizontal component of the net field at the magnet, and $I$ is the moment of inertia of the magnet about a vertical axis through its center.

A magnet (“X” or “A”) can be attached to the bottom end of the thread. A copper plate can be placed on the lower shelf, just below the suspended magnet, to damp out its motion if desired. ### Part 1: Determination of μX — Basic Insight

**Basic Insight**

The idea which enables one to “see into” the problem is contained in the following remark: The oscillation period of a given suspended magnet depends on the **product** of its moment and the (horizontal component of) the Earth's field, while the extent to which that magnet can influence the direction of another magnet used as a compass depends on the **ratio** of those two quantities.

It follows that by making measurements of **both** types, **both** the unknown moment and the horizontal component of the Earth's field can be determined. We suspect that this idea goes historically back to Gauss.

</ProblemSection>

<ProblemSection id="solution-part1-turn">

### First Solution: The “Turn-Around Method”


<figure className="problem-figure problem-figure--sized" style={{'--fig-w': '86.7%', '--fig-max': '555px'}}>
<img src="https://pub-43290baaaff14857b5dd59610ea438c7.r2.dev/problems/ipho-1993-experiment-exp2/p2-sol-fig1-v2.png" alt="Complete Turn-Around Method two arrangements, directions/equations1 and2, compass damping/irrelevantmoment notes, combining diagram and final magnetic-moment expression." width="1735" height="1402" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
</figure>


**Experimental Arrangement #1:** X oscillates with period $T_X$.

$$
\mu_XB_h=I_X(2\pi/T_X)^2.\qquad (1)
$$

**Experimental Arrangement #2:** A abruptly turns around when $R=R_0$.

$$
\mu_X\frac{2K}{R_0^3}=B_h.\qquad (2)
$$

Use copper damping plate beneath compass. Note that the $\mu$ and $I$ values of the compass magnet do not matter.

Combining (1) and (2) one easily finds:

$$
\mu_X=\frac{R_0^{3/2}}{(2K)^{1/2}}\frac{2\pi}{T_X}(I_X)^{1/2}.
$$

</ProblemSection>

<ProblemSection id="solution-part1-dynamic">

### Second Solution: Dynamic Method with 3 Unknowns

The experience from our tests was that the “Turn-Around” method did not occur naturally to most students. They were much more comfortable with the idea of using one magnet to influence the **period** of another. Since the magnetic moments are not necessarily equal, it is clear that two measurements will no longer suffice. Our guess is that the following 3-measurement scheme will be the most common student choice.

<figure className="problem-figure problem-figure--sized" style={{'--fig-w': '88.7%', '--fig-max': '568px'}}>
<img src="https://pub-43290baaaff14857b5dd59610ea438c7.r2.dev/problems/ipho-1993-experiment-exp2/p2-sol-fig2.png" alt="All three original Dynamic Method arrangements, poles, periods, fixed-X distance,slow-compass arrows and equations1–3." width="1775" height="1514" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
</figure>


**Arrangement #1:** X oscillates with period $T_X$.

$$
\mu_XB_h=I_X(2\pi/T_X)^2.\qquad (1)
$$

**Arrangement #2:** A oscillates with period $T_A$.

$$
\mu_AB_h=I_A(2\pi/T_A)^2.\qquad (2)
$$

**Arrangement #3:** A oscillates with period $T_R>T_A$; X is fixed at $R>R_0$.

$$
\mu_A\left[B_h-\mu_X\frac{2K}{R^3}\right]=I_A(2\pi/T_R)^2.\qquad (3)
$$

Note that the X magnet (positioned at a distance $R$ which is somewhat larger than the turn-around distance $R_0$) is being used here to **slow** the oscillations of the A magnet on the compass.

One worries at first that there are actually 4 unknowns, since the **inertial** moment of A need not equal that of X. Inspection of equations (2) and (3) shows, however, that the **ratio** $\mu_X/B_h$ can be expressed in terms of experimentally known quantities. Since (1) gives the **product** $\mu_XB_h$, the calculational strategy is clear. One easily finds:

$$
\mu_X=\frac{R^{3/2}}{(2K)^{1/2}}\frac{2\pi}{T_X}(I_X)^{1/2}\left[1-(T_A/T_R)^2\right]^{1/2}.\qquad (4)
$$

Alternatively, by reversing its poles, one can use the X magnet to **speed-up** the oscillations of the A magnet. Then, of course we have $T_R<T_A$. In this case (which is formally equivalent to the first case, with a reversal of the sign of $K$), one finds:

$$
\mu_X=\frac{R^{3/2}}{(2K)^{1/2}}\frac{2\pi}{T_X}(I_X)^{1/2}\left[(T_A/T_R)^2-1\right]^{1/2}.\qquad (4')
$$

</ProblemSection>

### Part 1 — Sample Experiment

The Dynamic Method just outlined was used (in the case where the X magnet was used to slow down the oscillations of the A magnet in Arrangement #3). In all cases 20 oscillations were timed. The distance $R$ was $(17.0\pm0.1)\ \mathrm{cm}$. The X moment of inertia was $I_X=(4.95\pm0.1)\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{kg\ m^2}$. Using the notation given previously, the data were as follows:

| Measurements in seconds | Four measured times | Arrangement |
|---|---|---|
| $20T_X$ | 10.83, 10.99, 10.91, 10.94 | #1 |
| $20T_A$ | 10.95, 11.10, 11.01, 10.92 | #2 |
| $20T_R$ | 21.70, 21.65, 21.78, 21.59 | #3 |

Using a pocket calculator (HP32S) to obtain the averages and statistical errors gives:

$$
T_X=(0.546\pm.003)\ \mathrm{sec},\quad
T_A=(0.550\pm.004)\ \mathrm{sec},\quad
T_R=(1.084\pm.004)\ \mathrm{sec}.
$$

The “statistical errors” here are naively based on what the calculator gave for the estimated standard deviation **around** the sample mean. More carefully, one should divide this by the square root of the number of observations to give the estimated standard error of the sample mean. [Still more carefully, for such a small sample, one should apply the appropriate statistical correction factor.] For simplicity we will use the naively calculated results. This will suffice for our purposes.

Write (4) as $\mu_X=GF$, where

$$
G=\frac{R^{3/2}}{(2K)^{1/2}}\frac{2\pi}{T_X}(I_X)^{1/2},\qquad F=\left[1-(T_A/T_R)^2\right]^{1/2}.
$$

The expression for $G$ is identical for that for $\mu_X$ in the “turnaround method” when $R=R_0$. This must be true, since in that case $T_R$ goes to infinity.

Numerically

$$
G=\frac{[(0.170\pm0.001)\ \mathrm m]^{3/2}}{[2\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{N/A^2}]^{1/2}}
\frac{2\pi}{(0.546\pm.003)\ \mathrm{sec}}
[(4.95\pm0.1)\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{kg\ m^2}]^{1/2},
$$

then standard error propagation and reduction of the units give

$$
G=(0.401\pm0.006)\ \mathrm{A\ m^2},
$$

which is a 1.5% uncertainty. For $F$ we find numerically:

$$
F=\left\{1.000-\left[\frac{(0.550\pm.004)\ \mathrm{sec}}{(1.084\pm.004)\ \mathrm{sec}}\right]^2\right\}^{1/2}.
$$

The central value here is 0.862. One can easily use a pocket calculator to see the effects of the permitted statistical variations in each of the two places above. This shows that the effect of the numerator uncertainty is essentially $\pm0.0022$, while that of the denominator is $\pm0.0013$. Combining these statistically gives a net uncertainty in $F$ of 0.0026, so that the fractional uncertainty in $F$ is 0.0033. [An analysis of this by calculus is straightforward, but cumbersome.] Then the fractional uncertainty in $\mu_X$ is practically that in $G$. We find:

$$
\mu_X=(0.862\pm0.0026)(0.401\pm0.006)\ \mathrm{A\ m}=(0.346\pm.005)\ \mathrm{A\ m^2}.
$$

The source prints A m on the product and A m² on the result; both unit forms are retained.

By way of comparison, measurement of the same magnet X using Fluxgate Magnetometry (at a distance of around 16 cm) gave $\mu_X=(0.345\pm.003)\ \mathrm{A\ m^2}$.

</ProblemSection>

### Method II (Intermediate Distances): Differential 1/T² Technique

**General Relation:** $T=T_X$; $B_h$ = local (horiz.) field.

$$
(2\pi/T)^2=\frac{\mu_XB_h}{I_X}.
$$

<figure className="problem-figure problem-figure--sized" style={{'--fig-w': '93.8%', '--fig-max': '601px'}}>
<img src="https://pub-43290baaaff14857b5dd59610ea438c7.r2.dev/problems/ipho-1993-experiment-exp2/p2-sol-fig4-v3.png" alt="Complete MethodII general relation and two-position faster/slower arrangement with all dipole labels, directions/distances, full delta-field equation and master-equation derivation." width="1877" height="1481" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
</figure>


**DEFINE:**

$$
\Delta(1/T^2)\equiv(1/T^2)_{\mathrm{faster}}-(1/T^2)_{\mathrm{slower}}.
$$

**THEN:**

$$
\Delta(1/T^2)=\frac{\mu_X\Delta B_h}{4\pi^2I_X},\qquad\text{where }\Delta B_h=2B_x(x).
$$

**“Master Equation”**

$$
B_x(x)=\frac{2\pi^2I_X}{\mu_X}\Delta(1/T^2).
$$

</ProblemSection>

### Method III (Large Distances): Differential 1/T² Technique with Partial “Bucking” of the Earth’s Field


<figure className="problem-figure problem-figure--sized" style={{'--fig-w': '87.5%', '--fig-max': '560px'}}>
<img src="https://pub-43290baaaff14857b5dd59610ea438c7.r2.dev/problems/ipho-1993-experiment-exp2/p2-sol-fig5-v2.png" alt="Complete MethodIII partial-bucking arrangement: securely taped A magnet, field orientation prose, B/X faster/slower layouts, x/N/S labels and master equation." width="1751" height="1335" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
</figure>


Tape “A” magnet **VERY SECURELY** in position: oriented to produce a field contribution at the compass opposite in direction to the Earth's field.

The “Master Equation” remains the same:

$$
B_x(x)=\frac{2\pi^2I_X}{\mu_X}\Delta(1/T^2).
$$

Use only **partial** buckout — (slow natural oscillations typically by a factor of 2).

In working at a given distance $x$, $\Delta(1/T^2)$ must be constant (independent of the “bucking”).

$$
\Delta(1/T^2)=\mathrm{const.},\qquad\Delta T/T^3=\mathrm{const.}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\Delta T\propto T^3.
$$

The three different methods are nicely consistent, and the whole data set well fits the power law indicated by the drawn line. When this is done on the regular log paper (as provided), the easiest way in this case to get the slope is to use a pocket calculator to find the ratio of the log of the vertical rise ratio to that of the horizontal run ratio for the possible lines consistent with the errors. Since the line has to drop vertically through three decades in total, this is roughly

$$
\mathrm{slope}=\frac{-3}{\log_{10}\left[\frac{(0.30\pm.02)}{(.051\pm.03)}\right]}=-3.9\pm0.15.
$$

The native denominator uncertainty is printed as .03; it is retained.
