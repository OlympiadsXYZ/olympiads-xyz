*Theoretical Competition — 25 April 2010 — Question Number 2 — Theoretical Question 2: Strong Resistive Electromagnets · 25 април 2010 г.*

## Условие

*Resistive electromagnets* are magnets with coils made of a normal metal such as copper or aluminum. Modern strong resistive electromagnets can provide steady magnetic fields higher than 30 tesla. Their coils are typically built by stacking hundreds of thin circular plates made of copper sheet metal with lots of cooling holes stamped in them; there are also insulators with the same pattern. When voltage is applied across the coil, current flows through the plates along a helical path to generate high magnetic fields in the center of the magnet.

In this question we aim to assess if a cylindrical coil (or *solenoid*) of many *turns* can serve as a magnet for generating high magnetic fields. As shown in Fig. 1, the center of the magnet is at O. Its cylindrical coil consists of $N$ turns of copper wire carrying a current $I$ uniformly distributed over the cross section of the wire. The coil’s mean diameter is $D$ and its length along the axial direction $x$ is $\ell$. The wire’s cross section is rectangular with width $a$ and height $b$. The turns of the coil are so tightly wound that the plane of each turn may be taken as perpendicular to the $x$ axis and $\ell = Na$. In Table 1, data specifying physical dimensions of the coil are listed.

| Table 1 |
|---|
| $\ell = 12.0\ \mathrm{cm}$ |
| $D = 6.0\ \mathrm{cm}$ |
| $a = 2.0\ \mathrm{mm}$ |
| $b = 5.0\ \mathrm{mm}$ |

In assessing if such a magnet can serve to provide high magnetic fields, two limiting factors must not be overlooked. One is the mechanical rigidity of the coil to withstand large Lorentz force on the field-producing current. The other is that the enormous amount of Joule heat generated in the wire must not cause excessive temperature rise. We shall examine these two factors using simplified models.

The Appendix at the end of the question lists some mathematical formulae and physical data which may be used if necessary.

**(a)** **Part A. Magnetic Fields on the Axis of the Coil**

Assume $b \ll D$ so that one may regard the wire as a thin strip of width $a$. Let O be the origin of $x$ coordinates. The direction of the current flow is as shown in Fig. 1.

(a) Find the $x$-component $B(x)$ of the magnetic field on the axis of the coil as a function of $x$ when the steady current passing through the coil is $I$. [1.0 point]

**(b)** Find the steady current $I_0$ passing through the coil if $B(0)$ is $10.0\ \mathrm{T}$. Use data given in Table 1 when computing numerical values. [0.4 point]

**(c)** **Part B. The Upper Limit of Current**

In Part B, we assume **the length $\ell$ of the coil is infinite** and $b \ll D$. Consider the turn of the coil located at $x = 0$. The magnetic field exerts Lorentz force on the current passing through the turn. Thus, as Fig. 2 shows, a wire segment of length $\Delta s$ is subject to a normal force $\Delta F_\mathrm{n}$ which tends to make the turn expand.

(c) Suppose that, when the current is $I$, the mean diameter of the expanded coil remains at a constant value $D'$ larger than $D$, as shown in Fig. 2.

Find the outward normal force per unit length $\Delta F_\mathrm{n}/\Delta s$. [1.2 point]

Find the tension $F_\mathrm{t}$ acting along the wire. [0.6 point] **[1,8 т.]**

**(d)** Neglect the coil’s acceleration during the expansion. Assume the turn will break when the wire’s *unit elongation* (i.e. tensile strain or fractional change of the length) is 60 % and *tensile stress* (i.e. tension per unit cross sectional area of the unstrained wire) is $\sigma_\mathrm{b} = 455\ \mathrm{MPa}$. Let $I_\mathrm{b}$ be the current at which the turn will break and $B_\mathrm{b}$ the corresponding magnetic field at the center O.

Find an expression for $I_\mathrm{b}$ and then calculate its value. [0.8 point]

Find an expression for $B_\mathrm{b}$ and then calculate its value. [0.4 point] **[1,2 т.]**

**(e)** **Part C. The Rate of Temperature Rise**

When the current $I$ is $10.0\ \mathrm{kA}$ and the temperature $T$ of the coil is $293\ \mathrm{K}$, assume that the resistivity, the specific heat capacity at constant pressure, and the mass density of the wire of the coil are, respectively, given by $\rho_\mathrm{e} = 1.72\times10^{-8}\ \Omega\cdot\mathrm{m}$, $c_p = 3.85\times10^{2}\ \mathrm{J/(kg\cdot K)}$ and $\rho_m = 8.98\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{kg\cdot m^{-3}}$.

(e) Find an expression for the *power density* (i.e. power per unit volume) of heat generation in the coil and then calculate its value. Use data in Table 1. [0.5 point]

**(f)** Let $\dot{T}$ be the time rate of change of temperature in the coil. Find an expression for $\dot{T}$ and then calculate its value. [0.5 point]

**(g)** **Part D. A Pulsed-Field Magnet**

If the large current needed for a strong magnet lasts only for a short time, the temperature rise caused by excessive Joule heating may be greatly reduced. This idea is employed in a *pulsed-field* magnet.

Thus, as shown in Fig. 3, a capacitor bank of capacitance $C$ charged initially to a potential $V_0$ is used to drive the current $I$ through the coil. The circuit is equipped with a switch $K$. The inductance $L$ and resistance $R$ of the circuit are assumed to be *entirely* due to the coil. The construct and dimensions of the coil are the same as given in Fig. 1 and Table 1. Assume $R$, $L$, and $C$ to be independent of temperature and the magnetic field is the same as that of an infinite solenoid with $\ell \to \infty$.

(g) Find expressions for the inductance $L$ and resistance $R$. [0.6 point]

Calculate the values of $L$ and $R$. Use data given in Table 1. [0.4 point] **[1 т.]**

**(h)** At time $t = 0$, the switch $K$ is thrown to position 1 and the current starts flowing. For $t \ge 0$, the charge $Q(t)$ on the positive plate of the capacitor and the current $I(t)$ entering the positive plate are given by

$$
Q(t) = \frac{CV_0}{\sin\theta_0}e^{-\alpha t}\sin(\omega t + \theta_0), \qquad (1)
$$

$$
I(t) = \frac{dQ}{dt} = (\frac{-\alpha}{\cos\theta_0})\frac{CV_0}{\sin\theta_0}e^{-\alpha t}\sin\omega t, \qquad (2)
$$

in which $\alpha$ and $\omega$ are positive constants and $\theta_0$ is given by

$$
\tan\theta_0 = \frac{\omega}{\alpha}, \qquad 0 < \theta_0 < \frac{\pi}{2}. \qquad (3)
$$

Note that, if $Q(t)$ is expressed as a function of a new variable $t' \equiv (t + \theta_0/\omega)$, then $Q(t')$ and its time derivative $I(t)$ are identical in form except for an overall constant factor. The time derivative of $I(t)$ may therefore be obtained similarly without further differentiations.

Find $\alpha$ and $\omega$ in terms of $R$, $L$, and $C$. [0.8 point]

Calculate the values of $\alpha$ and $\omega$ when $C$ is $10.0\ \mathrm{mF}$. [0.4 point] **[1,2 т.]**

**(i)** Let $I_\mathrm{m}$ be the maximum value of $|I(t)|$ for $t > 0$. Find an expression for $I_\mathrm{m}$. [0.6 point]

If $C = 10.0\ \mathrm{mF}$, what is the maximum value $V_{0\mathrm{b}}$ of the initial voltage $V_0$ of the capacitor bank for which $I_\mathrm{m}$ will not exceed $I_\mathrm{b}$ found in Problem (d)? [0.4 point] **[1 т.]**

**(j)** Suppose the switch $K$ is moved instantly from position 1 to 2 when the absolute value of the current $|I(t)|$ reaches $I_\mathrm{m}$. Let $\Delta E$ be the total amount of heat dissipated in the coil from $t = 0$ to $\infty$ and $\Delta T$ the corresponding temperature increase of the coil. Assume the initial voltage $V_0$ takes on the maximum value $V_{0\mathrm{b}}$ obtained in Problem (i) and the electromagnetic energy loss is only in the form of heat dissipated in the coil.

Find an expression for $\Delta E$ and then calculate its value. [1.0 point]

Find an expression for $\Delta T$ and then calculate its value. Note that the value for $\Delta T$ must be compatible with the assumption of constant $R$ and $L$. [0.4 point] **[1,4 т.]**

## Решение

<Spoiler title="Покажи официалното решение">

**Part A. Magnetic Fields on the Axis of the Coil**

(a) At the point $x$ on the axis, the magnetic field due to the current $I$ passing through the turns located in the interval $(s, s+ds)$ is (see Fig. A1)

$$
d\vec{B} = (\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi})\frac{I(\pi D)}{(D/2)^2+(s-x)^2}\cdot\frac{(D/2)}{\sqrt{(D/2)^2+(s-x)^2}}\cdot\frac{ds}{a}\hat{x} \qquad (a-1)
$$

which, when summed over all turns of the coil, leads to the total magnetic field $\vec{B}(x) = B(x)\hat{x}$ with

$$
\begin{aligned} B(x) &= \frac{\mu_0 I}{2a}\,(\frac{D}{2})^2\int\limits_{-\ell/2}^{\ell/2}\frac{ds}{[(D/2)^2+(s-x)^2]^{3/2}} \\ &= \frac{\mu_0 I}{2a}\,(\frac{D}{2})^2\int\limits_{-\ell/2-x}^{\ell/2-x}\frac{ds}{[(D/2)^2+s^2]^{3/2}} \\ &= \frac{\mu_0 I}{2a}\left\{\frac{(\ell/2)-x}{\sqrt{(D/2)^2+[(\ell/2)-x]^2}} + \frac{(\ell/2)+x}{\sqrt{(D/2)^2+[(\ell/2)+x]^2}}\right\} \end{aligned} \qquad ((a-2)*)
$$

(b) From Eq. (a-2), the magnetic field at O with $x = 0$ is

$$
B(0) = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2a}\frac{2(\ell/2)}{\sqrt{(D/2)^2+(\ell/2)^2}} = \frac{\mu_0 I}{a}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+(D/\ell)^2}} \qquad (b-1)
$$

If $B(0)$ is $10.0\ \mathrm{T}$, then the current $I$ must be equal to

$$
I_0 = B(0)\frac{a}{\mu_0}\sqrt{1+(D/\ell)^2} = 1.7794\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{A} \cong 1.8\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{A} \qquad ((b-2)*)
$$

\*An equation marked with an asterisk gives key answers to the problem.

**Part B. The Upper Limit of Current**

(c) For an infinitely long coil with $\ell \to \infty$ and $b \ll D$, the magnetic field $\vec{B}$ acting on the current is the average of the fields inside and outside of the coil. The field outside is zero and the field inside is the same as that at O, i.e. $B(0)$ in Eq. (b-1) with $\ell \to \infty$. Thus we have

$$
\vec{B} = \bar{B}\hat{x} = \frac{1}{2}(0 + \frac{\mu_0 I}{a})\hat{x} = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2a}\hat{x}, \qquad (c-1)
$$

and the outward normal force on the wire segment of length $\Delta s$ is

$$
\Delta F_\mathrm{n} = I\bar{B}\Delta s = I\Delta s\left(\frac{\mu_0 I}{2a}\right) \quad \text{or} \quad \frac{\Delta F_\mathrm{n}}{\Delta s} = \frac{\mu_0}{2a}I^2. \qquad ((c-2)*)
$$

As can be seen from Fig. A2, the resultant of the pair of tension forces at the ends of the segment $\Delta s$ is given by

$$
-2F_\mathrm{t}\,\sin\,(\frac{\Delta\theta}{2}) \cong -F_\mathrm{t}\,\Delta\theta = -F_\mathrm{t}\left(\frac{2\Delta s}{D'}\right). \qquad (c-3)
$$

This must be in equilibrium with the normal force $\Delta F_\mathrm{n}$ so that, by using Eq. (c-2), we have

$$
\Delta F_\mathrm{n} = F_\mathrm{t}\left(\frac{2\Delta s}{D'}\right) \ \text{ or } \ F_\mathrm{t} = \frac{D'}{2}\left(\frac{\Delta F_\mathrm{n}}{\Delta s}\right) = \frac{\mu_0}{4a}I^2 D'. \qquad ((c-4)*)
$$

(d) At breaking, the tensile stress of the wire is, from Eq. (c-4),

$$
\frac{F_\mathrm{t}}{ab} = \frac{\mu_0}{4a^2 b}{I_\mathrm{b}}^2 D' = \sigma_\mathrm{b} = 4.55\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{Pa}, \qquad (d-1)
$$

and the tensile strain of the wire is

$$
\frac{\pi(D'-D)}{\pi D} = \frac{(D'-D)}{D} = 60\ \% \ \text{ or } \ D' = 1.60\,D. \qquad (d-2)
$$

From the last two equations, the current $I_\mathrm{b}$ at which the turn will break is

$$
I_\mathrm{b} = 2a\sqrt{\frac{b\sigma_\mathrm{b}}{\mu_0 D'}} = 2a\sqrt{\frac{b\sigma_\mathrm{b}}{\mu_0(1.60\,D)}} = 1.737\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{A} \cong 1.7\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{A}, \qquad (d-3)
$$

and the magnitude of the magnetic field at the center O, i.e. Eq. (b-1) with $\ell \to \infty$, is

$$
B_\mathrm{b} = \frac{\mu_0 I_\mathrm{b}}{a} = 2\sqrt{\frac{\mu_0 b\sigma_\mathrm{b}}{D'}} = 10.914\ \mathrm{T} = 1.1\times10^{1}\ \mathrm{T}, \qquad ((d-4)*)
$$

**Part C. Rate of Temperature Rise**

(e) When the current $I$ is $10.0\ \mathrm{kA}$, the current density $J$ is given by

$$
J = \frac{I}{ab} = \frac{1.00\times10^{4}}{(2.0\times10^{-3})(5.0\times10^{-3})} = 1.0\times10^{9}\ \mathrm{A/m^2}. \qquad (e-1)
$$

The power density is given by

$$
\rho_\mathrm{e}J^2 = \rho_\mathrm{e}\left(\frac{I}{ab}\right)^2 = 1.720\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{W/m^3} \cong 1.7\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{W/m^3}. \qquad ((e-2)*)
$$

(ALTERNATIVE)

The volume $\tau$ and resistance $R$ (appearing also in Problem (h)) of the current-carrying wire for a coil of length $\ell$ are given by

$$
\tau = \pi\left\{\left(\frac{D+b}{2}\right)^2 - \left(\frac{D-b}{2}\right)^2\right\}\ell = \pi bD\ell = N\pi abD, \qquad (e-3)
$$

$$
R = \rho_\mathrm{e}\frac{N\pi D}{ab} = \rho_\mathrm{e}\frac{\pi D\ell}{a^2 b} = 1.9453\times10^{-2}\ \Omega \cong 1.9\times10^{-2}\ \Omega. \qquad (e-4)
$$

The total power $P$ of Joule heat generated in the coil is

$$
P = I^2 R = 1.9453\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{W} = 1.9\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{W}. \qquad (e-5)
$$

Thus the power density is

$$
\frac{P}{\tau} = \frac{P}{N\pi abD} = \frac{P}{\ell\pi bD} = 1.7\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{W/m^3}. \qquad ((e-6)*)
$$

Note that, by Eqs. (e-3) to (e-5), the expression for power density may also be written as

$$
\frac{P}{\tau} = \frac{I^2 R}{\tau} = \frac{I^2}{\ell\pi bD}\rho_\mathrm{e}\frac{\pi D\ell}{a^2 b} = \rho_\mathrm{e}\left(\frac{I}{ab}\right)^2 = \rho_\mathrm{e}J^2. \qquad ((e-7)*)
$$

This is identical to that obtained in Eq. (e-2).

(f) The time rate of temperature increase of the coil is

$$
\dot{T} = \frac{\rho_\mathrm{e}J^2}{\rho_m c_p} = \frac{\rho_\mathrm{e}}{\rho_m c_p}\left(\frac{I}{ab}\right)^2. \qquad (f-1)
$$

At $T = 293\ \mathrm{K}$ and $I = 10.0\ \mathrm{kA}$, we have

$$
\dot{T} = \frac{\rho_\mathrm{e}}{\rho_m c_p}\left(\frac{I}{ab}\right)^2 = \frac{\rho_\mathrm{e}J^2}{\rho_m c_p} = 4.975\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{K/s} \cong 5.0\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{K/s}. \qquad ((f-2)*)
$$

(ALTERNATIVE)

The heat capacity of the coil is

$$
Mc_p = \rho_m(\ell\pi bD)c_p = 3.9101\times10^{2}\ \mathrm{J/K} \cong 3.9\times10^{2}\ \mathrm{J/K}. \qquad (f-3)
$$

From Eqs. (e-5) and (f-3), the time rate of temperature increase is

$$
\dot{T} = \frac{I^2 R}{Mc_p} = 4.975\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{K/s} \cong 5.0\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{K/s}. \qquad ((f-4)*)
$$

**Part D. A Pulsed-Field Magnet**

(g) The magnetic flux $\phi_B$ through each turn is, in the limit $\ell \to \infty$, given by

$$
\phi_B = \{\lim_{\ell\to\infty} B(0)\}\pi\left(\frac{D}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{\mu_0 I}{a}\pi\left(\frac{D}{2}\right)^2. \qquad (g-1)
$$

The inductance $L$ of the coil is

$$
L = \frac{N\phi_B}{I} = \frac{N\mu_0}{a}\pi\left(\frac{D}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{\ell\mu_0}{4a^2}\pi D^2 = 1.0659\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{H} \cong 1.1\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{H}. \qquad ((g-2)*)
$$

The resistance $R$ of the coil is the same as given in Eq. (e-4). Thus

$$
R = \rho_\mathrm{e}\frac{\pi DN}{ab} = \rho_\mathrm{e}\frac{\pi D\ell}{a^2 b} = 1.9453\times10^{-2}\ \Omega \cong 1.9\times10^{-2}\ \Omega. \qquad ((g-3)*)
$$

(h) According to Kirchhoff’s circuit law, the change of electric potential around a closed circuit must be zero and we have

$$
L\frac{dI}{dt} + RI + \frac{Q}{C} = 0. \qquad (h-1)
$$

In this question, we are given

$$
Q(t) = \frac{CV_0}{\sin\theta_0}e^{-\alpha t}\sin(\omega t + \theta_0) = \left[e^{\alpha(\frac{\theta_0}{\omega})}\right]\frac{CV_0}{\sin\theta_0}e^{-\alpha(t+\frac{\theta_0}{\omega})}\sin\omega(t + \frac{\theta_0}{\omega}), \qquad (1)
$$

$$
I(t) = \frac{dQ}{dt} = \left[(\frac{-\alpha}{\cos\theta_0})\right]\frac{CV_0}{\sin\theta_0}e^{-\alpha t}\sin\omega t, \qquad (2)
$$

$$
\tan\theta_0 = \frac{\omega}{\alpha}. \qquad (3)
$$

Comparing the right sides of Eqs. (1) and (2), one sees that the current $I(t) = dQ/dt$ is obtained from $Q(t)$ by changing the latter’s time variable $t$ to $(t - \theta_0/\omega)$ or, equivalently, changing $(t + \theta_0/\omega)$ to $t$, and then multiplying its amplitude constant by a factor

$$
\left\{e^{-\alpha\frac{\theta_0}{\omega}}\,(\frac{-\alpha}{\cos\theta_0})\right\}.
$$

Since $I(t)$ in Eq. (2) has the same form as $Q(t)$ in Eq. (1), we may apply the same rule again to obtain its derivative $dI/dt$ as

$$
\begin{aligned} \frac{dI}{dt} &= \left\{e^{-\alpha\frac{\theta_0}{\omega}}(\frac{-\alpha}{\cos\theta_0})\right\}\left[(\frac{-\alpha}{\cos\theta_0})\frac{CV_0}{\sin\theta_0}\right]e^{-\alpha(t-\frac{\theta_0}{\omega})}\sin\omega(t - \frac{\theta_0}{\omega}) \\ &= (\frac{\alpha}{\cos\theta_0})^2\frac{CV_0}{\sin\theta_0}e^{-\alpha t}\sin(\omega t - \theta_0) \end{aligned} \qquad (h-2)
$$

Making use of Formula 2 given in Appendix, we may express the left side of Eq. (h-1) as a linear combination of $\cos\omega t$ and $\sin\omega t$ so that

$$
L\frac{dI}{dt} + RI + \frac{Q}{C} = \left(\frac{CV_0}{\sin\theta_0}\right)e^{-\alpha t}(A\cos\theta_0\sin\omega t + B\sin\theta_0\cos\omega t) = 0, \qquad (h-3)
$$

which can be satisfied if and only if

$$
A \equiv L(\frac{\alpha}{\cos\theta_0})^2 - R(\frac{\alpha}{\cos^2\theta_0}) + \frac{1}{C} = 0, \qquad (h-4)
$$

$$
B \equiv -L(\frac{\alpha}{\cos\theta_0})^2 + \frac{1}{C} = 0, \qquad (h-5)
$$

Note that Eqs. (h-4) and (h-5) may be obtained more simply by considering Eq. (h-1) at the moments when $\sin\omega t = 1$ and 0, respectively. Subtracting Eq. (h-5) from Eq. (h-4), we obtain

$$
\alpha = \frac{R}{2L}, \qquad (h-6)
$$

If we use the expressions given in Eqs. (g-2) and (g-3), we obtain

$$
\alpha = \frac{R}{2L} = \frac{\rho_e\dfrac{\pi D\ell}{a^2 b}}{\pi D^2\dfrac{\ell\mu_0}{2a^2}} = \frac{2\rho_e}{\mu_0 bD} = 9.1249\times10^{1}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}} \cong 9.1\times10^{1}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}. \qquad ((h-7)*)
$$

Adding up Eqs. (h-4) and (h-5), we have, by Eq. (h-6) and Eq. (3),

$$
\frac{1}{LC} = \frac{R\alpha}{2L\cos^2\theta_0} = \frac{\alpha^2}{\cos^2\theta_0} = \alpha^2(1 + \tan^2\theta_0) = \alpha^2 + \omega^2. \qquad (h-8)
$$

This may be rewritten as

$$
\omega^2 = {\omega_0}^2 - \alpha^2 = \frac{1}{LC} - (\frac{R}{2L})^2 \quad \text{with} \quad \omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}} = 9.7\times10^{2}\ \mathrm{rad/s}, \qquad ((h-9)*)
$$

and we obtain

$$
\omega = \sqrt{{\omega_0}^2 - \alpha^2} = 9.6428\times10^{2}\ \mathrm{rad/s} \cong 9.6\times10^{2}\ \mathrm{rad/s}. \qquad ((h-10)*)
$$

(i) From Eq. (h-2), the maximum value of $|I(t)|$ appears at $dI/dt = 0$ when the time is

$$
t_\mathrm{m} = \frac{\theta_0}{\omega}. \qquad (i-1)
$$

From Eq. (2), the maximum value of $|I(t)|$ is then given by

$$
I_\mathrm{m} = |I(t_\mathrm{m})| = (\frac{\alpha}{\cos\theta_0})CV_0 e^{-\frac{\alpha}{\omega}\theta_0}. \qquad ((i-2)*)
$$

From Eqs. (3), (h-7) and (h-10), we have

$$
\tan\theta_0 = \frac{\omega}{\alpha} = 10.568, \qquad \theta_0 = 1.4764\ \mathrm{rad}, \qquad t_\mathrm{m} = \frac{\theta_0}{\omega} = 1.531\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{s}. \qquad (i-3)
$$

If $I_\mathrm{m}$ does not exceed $I_\mathrm{b}$ found in Problem (d), we must have

$$
I_\mathrm{m} = |I(t_\mathrm{m})| \le I_\mathrm{b} \ \text{ or } \ (\frac{\alpha}{\cos\theta_0})\,C\,V_0 e^{-\frac{\alpha}{\omega}\theta_0} \le I_\mathrm{b}, \qquad (i-4)
$$

which implies that the maximum value $V_{0\mathrm{b}}$ of $V_0$ occurs when the equality holds and is given by

$$
V_{0\mathrm{b}} = \frac{I_\mathrm{b}}{\alpha C}e^{\frac{\alpha}{\omega}\theta_0}\,\cos\theta_0 = 2.0623\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{V} \cong 2.1\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{V}. \qquad ((i-5)*)
$$

[j] When $|I(t)|$ reaches its maximum at $t = t_\mathrm{m} = \theta_0/\omega$, the voltage of the capacitor has dropped from the initial voltage $V_0 = V_{0\mathrm{b}}$ to

$$
V(t_\mathrm{m}) = \frac{Q(t_\mathrm{m})}{C} = \frac{V_{0\mathrm{b}}}{\sin\theta_0}e^{-\frac{\alpha}{\omega}\theta_0}\,\sin(2\theta_0) = 2V_{0\mathrm{b}}e^{-\frac{\alpha}{\omega}\theta_0}\cos\theta_0. \qquad (j-1)
$$

From $t = 0$ to $t = t_\mathrm{m}$, the energy supplied by the capacitor bank to the circuit, in the form of Joule heat and magnetic energy in the field of the coil, is

$$
E_C = \frac{1}{2}C\{{V_{0\mathrm{b}}}^2 - [V(t_\mathrm{m})]^2\} = \frac{1}{2}C{V_{0\mathrm{b}}}^2\left\{1 - 4e^{-\frac{2\alpha}{\omega}\theta_0}\,\cos^2\theta_0\right\}. \qquad (j-2)
$$

By the law of conservation of energy, this entire amount of energy is eventually turned into heat in the coil and we have

$$
\Delta E = E_C = \frac{1}{2}C{V_{0\mathrm{b}}}^2\left\{1 - 4e^{-\frac{2\alpha}{\omega}\theta_0}\,\cos^2\theta_0\right\} = 2.0694\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{J} \cong 2.1\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{J}. \qquad ((j-3)*)
$$

If the heat capacity (as computed in Eq. (f-3) remains about the same as that at $T = 293\ \mathrm{K}$, then the temperature increase $\Delta T$ is

$$
\Delta T = \frac{\Delta E}{Mc_p} = \frac{\Delta E}{\rho_m(\ell\pi bD)c_p} = 53\ \mathrm{K}. \qquad ((j-4)*)
$$

With such a temperature increase, the thermal and electrical properties of a metal such as copper do not change substantially.
