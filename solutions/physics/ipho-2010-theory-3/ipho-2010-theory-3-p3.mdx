## Условие

**Introduction**

Although atomic nuclei are quantum objects, a number of phenomenological laws for their basic properties (like radius or binding energy) can be deduced from simple assumptions: (i) nuclei are built from nucleons (i.e. protons and neutrons); (ii) strong nuclear interaction holding these nucleons together has a very short range (it acts only between neighboring nucleons); (iii) the number of protons ($Z$) in a given nucleus is approximately equal to the number of neutrons ($N$), i.e. $Z \approx N \approx A/2$, where $A$ is the total number of nucleons ($A \gg 1$). **Important: Use these assumptions in Tasks 1-4 below.**

**1a** **Task 1 - Atomic nucleus as closely packed system of nucleons**

In a simple model, an atomic nucleus can be thought of as a ball consisting of closely packed nucleons [see Fig. 1(a)], where the nucleons are hard balls of radius $r_N = 0.85$ fm (1 fm = $10^{-15}$ m). The nuclear force is present only for two nucleons in contact. The volume of the nucleus $V$ is larger than the volume of all nucleons $AV_N$, where $V_N = \frac{4}{3} r_N^3 \pi$. The ratio $f = AV_N/V$ is called the packing factor and gives the percentage of space filled by the nuclear matter.

a) Calculate what would be the packing factor $f$ if nucleons were arranged in a “simple cubic” (SC) crystal system, where each nucleon is centered on a lattice point of an infinite cubic lattice [see Fig. 1(b)]. *(0.3 points)* **[0,3 т.]**

**Important:** In all subsequent tasks, assume that the actual packing factor for nuclei is equal to the one from Task 1a. If you are not able to calculate it, in subsequent tasks use $f = 1/2$.

**1b** b) Estimate the average mass density $\rho_m$, charge density $\rho_c$, and the radius $R$ for a nucleus having $A$ nucleons. The average mass of a nucleon is $1.67\cdot 10^{-27}$ kg. *(1.0 points)* **[1 т.]**

**2** **Task 2 - Binding energy of atomic nuclei – volume and surface terms**

Binding energy of a nucleus is the energy required to disassemble it into separate nucleons and it essentially comes from the attractive nuclear force of each nucleon with its neighbors. If a given nucleon is not on the surface of the nucleus, it contributes to the total binding energy with $a_V$= 15.8 MeV (1 MeV = $1.602\cdot 10^{-13}$ J). The contribution of one surface nucleon to the binding energy is approximately $a_V/2$. Express the binding energy $E_b$ of a nucleus with $A$ nucleons in terms of $A$, $a_V$, and $f$, and by including the surface correction. *(1.9 points)* **[1,9 т.]**

**3a** **Task 3 - Electrostatic (Coulomb) effects on the binding energy**

The electrostatic energy of a homogeneously charged ball (with radius $R$ and total charge $Q_0$) is $U_c = \dfrac{3Q_0^2}{20\pi\varepsilon_0 R}$, where $\varepsilon_0 = 8.85\cdot 10^{-12}\ \mathrm{C}^2 N^{-1} m^{-2}$.

a) Apply this formula to get the electrostatic energy of a nucleus. In a nucleus, each proton is not acting upon itself (by Coulomb force), but only upon the rest of the protons. One can take this into account by replacing $Z^2 \to Z(Z-1)$ in the obtained formula. Use this correction in subsequent tasks. *(0.4 points)* **[0,4 т.]**

**3b** b) Write down the complete formula for binding energy, including the main (volume) term, the surface correction term and the obtained electrostatic correction. *(0.3 points)* **[0,3 т.]**

**4a** **Task 4 - Fission of heavy nuclei**

Fission is a nuclear process in which a nucleus splits into smaller parts (lighter nuclei). Suppose that a nucleus with $A$ nucleons splits into only two equal parts as depicted in Fig. 2.

a) Calculate the total kinetic energy of the fission products $E_{kin}$ when the centers of two lighter nuclei are separated by the distance $d \ge 2R(A/2)$, where $R(A/2)$ is their radius. The large nucleus was initially at rest. *(1.3 points)* **[1,3 т.]**

**4b** b) Assume that $d = 2R(A/2)$ and evaluate the expression for $E_{kin}$ obtained in part a) for $A$= 100, 150, 200 and 250 (express the results in units of MeV). Estimate the values of $A$ for which fission is possible in the model described above? *(1.0 points)* **[1 т.]**

**5a** **Task 5 – Transfer reactions**

a) In modern physics, the energetics of nuclei and their reactions is described in terms of masses. For example, if a nucleus (with zero velocity) is in an excited state with energy $E_{exc}$ above the ground state, its mass is $m = m_0 + E_{exc}/c^2$, where $m_0$ is its mass in the ground state at rest. The nuclear reaction $^{16}\mathrm{O}+{}^{54}\mathrm{Fe}\to{}^{12}\mathrm{C}+{}^{58}\mathrm{Ni}$ is an example of the so-called “transfer reactions”, in which a part of one nucleus (“cluster”) is transferred to the other (see Fig. 3). In our example the transferred part is a $^{4}$He-cluster ($\alpha$ -particle). The transfer reactions occur with maximum probability if the velocity of the projectile-like reaction product (in our case: $^{12}$C) is equal both in magnitude and direction to the velocity of projectile (in our case: $^{16}$O). The target $^{54}$Fe is initially at rest. In the reaction, $^{58}$Ni is excited into one of its higher-lying states. Find the excitation energy of that state (and express it units of MeV) if the kinetic energy of the projectile $^{16}$O is 50 MeV. The speed of light is c= $3\cdot 10^{8}$ m/s. *(2.2 points)*

## Решение

<Spoiler title="Покажи официалното решение">

**Solution - model of an atomic nucleus**

**Solution of Task 1**

a) In the SC-system, in each of 8 corners of a given cube there is one unit (atom, nucleon, etc.), but it is shared by 8 neighboring cubes – this gives a total of one nucleon per cube. If nucleons are touching, as we assume in our simplified model, then $a = 2r_N$ is the cube edge length a. The volume of one nucleon is then

$$
V_N = \frac{4}{3} r_N^3 \pi = \frac{4}{3}\left(\frac{a}{2}\right)^3 \pi = \frac{4a^3}{3\cdot 8}\pi = \frac{\pi}{6}a^3 \qquad (1)
$$

from which we obtain

$$
f = \frac{V_N}{a^3} = \frac{\pi}{6} \approx 0.52 \qquad (2)
$$

b) The mass density of the nucleus is:

$$
\rho_m = f\frac{m_N}{V_N} = 0.52\cdot\frac{1.67\cdot 10^{-27}}{4/3\cdot\left(0.85\cdot 10^{-15}\right)^3\pi} \approx 3.40\cdot 10^{17}\ \frac{\mathrm{kg}}{\mathrm{m}^3}. \qquad (4)
$$

c) Taking into account the approximation that the number of protons and neutrons is approximately equal, for charge density we get:

$$
\rho_c = \frac{f}{2}\frac{e}{V_N} = \frac{0.52}{2}\cdot\frac{1.6\cdot 10^{-19}}{4/3\cdot\left(0.85\cdot 10^{-15}\right)^3\pi} \approx 1.63\cdot 10^{25}\ \frac{\mathrm{C}}{\mathrm{m}^3} \qquad (5)
$$

The number of nucleons in a given nucleus is *A*. The total volume occupied by the nucleus is:

$$
V = \frac{AV_N}{f}, \qquad (6)
$$

which gives the following relation between radii of nucleus and the number of nucleons:

$$
R = r_N\left(\frac{A}{f}\right)^{1/3} = \frac{r_N}{f^{1/3}}A^{1/3} = \frac{0.85}{0.52^{1/3}}A^{1/3} = 1.06\ \mathrm{fm}\cdot A^{1/3}. \qquad (7)
$$

The numerical constant (1.06 fm) in the equation above will be denoted as $r_0$ in the sequel.

**Solution of Task 2**

First one needs to estimate the number of surface nucleons. The surface nucleons are in a spherical shell of width $2r_N$ at the surface. The volume of this shell is

$$
\begin{aligned} V_{surface} &= \frac{4}{3}R^3\pi - \frac{4}{3}\left(R-2r_N\right)^3\pi = \\ &= \frac{4}{3}R^3\pi - \frac{4}{3}R^3\pi + \frac{4}{3}\pi 3R^2 2r_N - \frac{4}{3}\pi 3R4r_N^2 + \frac{4}{3}\pi 8r_N^3 \\ &= 8\pi Rr_N\left(R-2r_N\right) + \frac{4}{3}\pi 8r_N^3 = \\ &= 8\pi(R^2r_N - 2Rr_N^2 + \frac{4}{3}r_N^3) \end{aligned} \qquad (8)
$$

The number of surface nucleons is:

$$
\begin{aligned} A_{surface} &= f\frac{V_{surface}}{V_N} = f\frac{8\pi(R^2r_N - 2Rr_N^2 + \frac{4}{3}r_N^3)}{\frac{4}{3}r_N^3\pi} = \\ &= f6\left(\left(\frac{R}{r_N}\right)^2 - 2\left(\frac{R}{r_N}\right) + \frac{4}{3}\right) = \\ &= f6\left(\left(\frac{A}{f}\right)^{2/3} - 2\left(\frac{A}{f}\right)^{1/3} + \frac{4}{3}\right) = \\ &= 6f^{1/3}A^{2/3} - 12f^{2/3}A^{1/3} + 8f = \\ &= 6^{2/3}\pi^{1/3}A^{2/3} - 2\cdot 6^{1/3}\pi^{2/3}A^{1/3} + \frac{4}{3}\pi \approx \\ &\approx 4.84A^{2/3} - 7.80A^{1/3} + 4.19. \end{aligned} \qquad (9)
$$

The binding energy is now:

$$
\begin{aligned} E_b &= \left(A - A_{surface}\right)a_V + A_{surface}\frac{a_V}{2} = \\ &= Aa_V - A_{surface}\frac{a_V}{2} = \\ &= Aa_V - (3f^{1/3}A^{2/3} - 6f^{2/3}A^{1/3} + 4f)a_V = \\ &= Aa_V - 3f^{1/3}A^{2/3}a_V + 6f^{2/3}A^{1/3}a_V - 4fa_V = \\ &= \left(15.8A - 38.20A^{2/3} + 61.58A^{1/3} - 33.09\right)\mathrm{MeV} \end{aligned} \qquad (10)
$$

**Solution of Task 3 - Electrostatic (Coulomb) effects on the binding energy**

a) Replacing $Q_0$ with $Ze$ gives the electrostatic energy of the nucleus as:

$$
U_c = \frac{3\left(Ze\right)^2}{20\pi\varepsilon_0 R} = \frac{3Z^2e^2}{20\pi\varepsilon_0 R} \qquad (12)
$$

The fact that each proton is not acting upon itself is taken into account by replacing $Z^2$ with $Z(Z-1)$:

$$
U_c = \frac{3Z(Z-1)e^2}{20\pi\varepsilon_0 R} \qquad (13)
$$

b) In the formula for the electrostatic energy we should replace $R$ with $r_N f^{-1/3}A^{1/3}$ to obtain

$$
\begin{aligned} \Delta E_b &= -\frac{3e^2f^{1/3}}{20\pi\varepsilon_0 r_N}\frac{Z(Z-1)}{A^{1/3}} = -\frac{Z(Z-1)}{A^{1/3}}\cdot 1.31\times 10^{-13}\ \mathrm{J} \\ &= -\frac{Z(Z-1)}{A^{1/3}}\cdot 0.815\ \mathrm{MeV} \approx -0.204A^{5/3}\mathrm{MeV} + 0.409A^{2/3}\ \mathrm{MeV} \end{aligned} \qquad (14)
$$

where $Z \approx A/2$ has been used. The Coulomb repulsion reduces the binding energy, hence the negative sign before the first (main) term. The complete formula for binding energy now gives:

$$
E_b = Aa_V - 3f^{1/3}A^{2/3}a_V + 6f^{2/3}A^{1/3}a_V - 4fa_V - \frac{3e^2f^{1/3}}{20\pi\varepsilon_0 r_N}\left(\frac{A^{5/3}}{4} - \frac{A^{2/3}}{2}\right) \qquad (15)
$$

**Solution of Task 4 - Fission of heavy nuclei**

a) The kinetic energy comes from the difference of binding energies (2 small nuclei – the original large one) and the Coulomb energy between two smaller nuclei (with $Z/2=A/4$ nucleons each):

$$
\begin{aligned} E_{kin}(d) &= 2E_b\left(\frac{A}{2}\right) - E_b\left(A\right) - \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{A^2e^2}{4\cdot 4\cdot d} = \\ &= -3f^{1/3}A^{2/3}a_V(2^{1/3}-1) + 6f^{2/3}A^{1/3}a_V(2^{2/3}-1) \\ &\quad - 4fa_V - \frac{3e^2f^{1/3}}{20\pi\varepsilon_0 r_N}\left[\frac{A^{5/3}}{4}\left(2^{-2/3}-1\right) - \frac{A^{2/3}}{2}\left(2^{1/3}-1\right)\right] \\ &\quad - \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{A^2e^2}{16d} \end{aligned} \qquad (16)
$$

(notice that the first term, $Aa_v$, cancels out).

b) The kinetic energy when $d = 2R(A/2)$ is given with:

$$
\begin{aligned} E_{kin} &= 2E_b\left(\frac{A}{2}\right) - E_b\left(A\right) - \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{2^{1/3}A^2e^2}{16\cdot 2r_NA^{1/3}f^{-1/3}} = \\ &= -3f^{1/3}A^{2/3}a_V(2^{1/3}-1) + 6f^{2/3}A^{1/3}a_V(2^{2/3}-1) \\ &\quad - 4fa_V - \frac{e^2f^{1/3}}{\pi\varepsilon_0 r_N}\left[\frac{3}{80}(2^{-2/3}-1) + \frac{2^{1/3}}{128}\right]A^{5/3} - \frac{e^2f^{1/3}}{\pi\varepsilon_0 r_N}\left[\frac{3}{40}(2^{1/3}-1)\right]A^{2/3} = \\ &= (0.02203A^{5/3} - 10.0365A^{2/3} + 36.175A^{1/3} - 33.091)\mathrm{MeV} \end{aligned} \qquad (17)
$$

Numerically one gets:

$A$=100 … $E_{kin}$= -33.95 MeV,

$A$=150 … $E_{kin}$= -30.93 MeV,

$A$=200 … $E_{kin}$= -14.10 MeV,

$A$=250 … $E_{kin}$= +15.06 MeV.

In our model, fission is possible when $E_{kin}(d = 2R(A/2)) \ge 0$. From the numerical evaluations given above, one sees that this happens approximately halfway between $A$=200 and $A$=250 – a rough estimate would be $A$≈225. Precise numerical evaluation of the equation:

$$
E_{kin} = (0.02203A^{5/3} - 10.0365A^{2/3} + 36.175A^{1/3} - 33.091)\mathrm{MeV} \ge 0 \qquad (18)
$$

gives that for $A \ge 227$ fission is possible.

**Solution of Task 5 – Transfer reactions**

**Task 5a)** This part can be solved by using either non-relativistic or relativistic kinematics.

*Non-relativistic solution*

First one has to find the amount of mass transferred to energy in the reaction (or the energy equivalent, so-called Q-value):

$$
\begin{aligned} \Delta m &= \left(\mathrm{total\ mass}\right)_{\mathrm{after\ reaction}} - \left(\mathrm{total\ mass}\right)_{\mathrm{before\ reaction}} = \\ &= (57.93535 + 12.00000)\ \mathrm{a.m.u.} - (53.93962 + 15.99491)\ \mathrm{a.m.u.} = \\ &= 0.00082\ \mathrm{a.m.u.} = \\ &= 1.3616\cdot 10^{-30}\ \mathrm{kg.} \end{aligned} \qquad (19)
$$

Using the Einstein formula for equivalence of mass and energy, we get:

$$
\begin{aligned} Q &= \left(\mathrm{total\ kinetic\ energy}\right)_{\mathrm{after\ reaction}} - \left(\mathrm{total\ kinetic\ energy}\right)_{\mathrm{before\ reaction}} = \\ &= -\Delta m\cdot c^2 = \\ &= -1.3616\cdot 10^{-30}\cdot 299792458^2 = -1.2237\cdot 10^{-13}\ \mathrm{J} \end{aligned} \qquad (20)
$$

Taking into account that 1 MeV is equal to $1.602\cdot 10^{-13}$ J, we get:

$$
Q = -1.2237\cdot 10^{-13}/1.602\cdot 10^{-13} = -0.761\ \mathrm{MeV} \qquad (21)
$$

This exercise is now solved using the laws of conservation of energy and momentum. The latter gives (we are interested only for the case when $^{12}$C and $^{16}$O are having the same direction so we don’t need to use vectors):

$$
m(^{16}\mathrm{O})v(^{16}\mathrm{O}) = m(^{12}\mathrm{C})v(^{12}\mathrm{C}) + m(^{58}\mathrm{Ni})v(^{58}\mathrm{Ni}) \qquad (22)
$$

while the conservation of energy gives:

$$
E_k(^{16}\mathrm{O}) + Q = E_k(^{12}\mathrm{C}) + E_k(^{58}\mathrm{Ni}) + E_x(^{58}\mathrm{Ni}) \qquad (23)
$$

where $E_x(^{58}\mathrm{Ni})$ is the excitation energy of $^{58}$Ni, and $Q$ is calculated in the first part of this task. But since $^{12}$C and $^{16}$O have the same velocity, conservation of momentum reduced to:

$$
\left[m(^{16}\mathrm{O}) - m(^{12}\mathrm{C})\right]v(^{16}\mathrm{O}) = m(^{58}\mathrm{Ni})v(^{58}\mathrm{Ni}) \qquad (24)
$$

Now we can easily find the kinetic energy of $^{58}$Ni:

$$
\begin{aligned} E_k(^{58}\mathrm{Ni}) &= \frac{m(^{58}\mathrm{Ni})v^2(^{58}\mathrm{Ni})}{2} = \frac{\left[m(^{58}\mathrm{Ni})v(^{58}\mathrm{Ni})\right]^2}{2m(^{58}\mathrm{Ni})} = \\ &= \frac{\left[\left[m(^{16}\mathrm{O}) - m(^{12}\mathrm{C})\right]v(^{16}\mathrm{O})\right]^2}{2m(^{58}\mathrm{Ni})} = \\ &= E_k(^{16}\mathrm{O})\frac{\left[m(^{16}\mathrm{O}) - m(^{12}\mathrm{C})\right]^2}{m(^{58}\mathrm{Ni})m(^{16}\mathrm{O})} \end{aligned} \qquad (25)
$$

and finally the excitation energy of $^{58}$Ni:

$$
\begin{aligned} E_x(^{58}\mathrm{Ni}) &= E_k(^{16}\mathrm{O}) + Q - E_k(^{12}\mathrm{C}) - E_k(^{58}\mathrm{Ni}) = \\ &= E_k(^{16}\mathrm{O}) + Q - \frac{m(^{12}\mathrm{C})v^2(^{16}\mathrm{O})}{2} - E_k(^{16}\mathrm{O})\frac{\left[m(^{16}\mathrm{O}) - m(^{12}\mathrm{C})\right]^2}{m(^{58}\mathrm{Ni})m(^{16}\mathrm{O})} = \\ &= Q + E_k(^{16}\mathrm{O}) - E_k(^{16}\mathrm{O})\cdot\frac{m(^{12}\mathrm{C})}{m(^{16}\mathrm{O})} - E_k(^{16}\mathrm{O})\frac{\left[m(^{16}\mathrm{O}) - m(^{12}\mathrm{C})\right]^2}{m(^{58}\mathrm{Ni})m(^{16}\mathrm{O})} = \\ &= Q + E_k(^{16}\mathrm{O})\left[1 - \frac{m(^{12}\mathrm{C})}{m(^{16}\mathrm{O})} - \frac{\left[m(^{16}\mathrm{O}) - m(^{12}\mathrm{C})\right]^2}{m(^{58}\mathrm{Ni})m(^{16}\mathrm{O})}\right] = \\ &= Q + E_k(^{16}\mathrm{O})\frac{\left[m(^{16}\mathrm{O}) - m(^{12}\mathrm{C})\right]\cdot\left[m(^{58}\mathrm{Ni}) - m(^{16}\mathrm{O}) + m(^{12}\mathrm{C})\right]}{m(^{58}\mathrm{Ni})m(^{16}\mathrm{O})} \end{aligned} \qquad (26)
$$

Note that the first bracket in numerator is approximately equal to the mass of transferred particle (the $^{4}$He nucleus), while the second one is approximately equal to the mass of target nucleus $^{54}$Fe. Inserting the numbers we get:

$$
\begin{aligned} E_x(^{58}\mathrm{Ni}) &= -0.761 + 50\cdot\frac{(15.99491 - 12.)(57.93535 - 15.99491 + 12.)}{57.93535\cdot 15.99491} = \\ &= 10.866\ \mathrm{MeV} \end{aligned} \qquad (27)
$$

*Relativistic solution*

In the relativistic version, solution is found starting from the following pair of equations (the first one is the law of conservation of energy and the second one the law of conservation of momentum):

$$
\begin{gathered} m(^{54}\mathrm{Fe})\cdot c^2 + \frac{m(^{16}\mathrm{O})\cdot c^2}{\sqrt{1 - v^2(^{16}\mathrm{O})/c^2}} = \frac{m(^{12}\mathrm{C})\cdot c^2}{\sqrt{1 - v^2(^{12}\mathrm{C})/c^2}} + \frac{m^*(^{58}\mathrm{Ni})\cdot c^2}{\sqrt{1 - v^2(^{58}\mathrm{Ni})/c^2}} \\ \frac{m(^{16}\mathrm{O})\cdot v(^{16}\mathrm{O})}{\sqrt{1 - v^2(^{16}\mathrm{O})/c^2}} = \frac{m(^{12}\mathrm{C})\cdot v(^{12}\mathrm{C})}{\sqrt{1 - v^2(^{12}\mathrm{C})/c^2}} + \frac{m^*(^{58}\mathrm{Ni})\cdot v(^{58}\mathrm{Ni})}{\sqrt{1 - v^2(^{58}\mathrm{Ni})/c^2}} \end{gathered} \qquad (28)
$$

All the masses in the equations are the rest masses; the $^{58}$Ni is NOT in its ground-state, but in one of its excited states (having the mass denoted with $m^*$). Since $^{12}$C and $^{16}$O have the same velocity, this set of equations reduces to:

$$
\begin{gathered} m(^{54}\mathrm{Fe}) + \frac{m(^{16}\mathrm{O}) - m(^{12}\mathrm{C})}{\sqrt{1 - v^2(^{16}\mathrm{O})/c^2}} = \frac{m^*(^{58}\mathrm{Ni})}{\sqrt{1 - v^2(^{58}\mathrm{Ni})/c^2}} \\ \frac{\left(m(^{16}\mathrm{O}) - m(^{12}\mathrm{C})\right)\cdot v(^{16}\mathrm{O})}{\sqrt{1 - v^2(^{16}\mathrm{O})/c^2}} = \frac{m^*(^{58}\mathrm{N}i)\cdot v(^{58}\mathrm{Ni})}{\sqrt{1 - v^2(^{58}\mathrm{Ni})/c^2}} \end{gathered} \qquad (29)
$$

Dividing the second equation with the first one gives:

$$
v(^{58}\mathrm{Ni}) = \frac{\left(m(^{16}\mathrm{O}) - m(^{12}\mathrm{C})\right)\cdot v(^{16}\mathrm{O})}{\left(m(^{16}\mathrm{O}) - m(^{12}\mathrm{C})\right) + m(^{54}\mathrm{Fe})\sqrt{1 - v^2(^{16}\mathrm{O})/c^2}} \qquad (30)
$$

The velocity of projectile can be calculated from its energy:

$$
\begin{gathered} E_{kin}(^{16}\mathrm{O}) = \frac{m(^{16}\mathrm{O})\cdot c^2}{\sqrt{1 - v^2(^{16}\mathrm{O})/c^2}} - m(^{16}\mathrm{O})\cdot c^2 \\ \sqrt{1 - v^2(^{16}\mathrm{O})/c^2} = \frac{m(^{16}\mathrm{O})\cdot c^2}{E_{kin}(^{16}\mathrm{O}) + m(^{16}\mathrm{O})\cdot c^2} \\ v^2(^{16}\mathrm{O})/c^2 = 1 - \left(\frac{m(^{16}\mathrm{O})\cdot c^2}{E_{kin}(^{16}\mathrm{O}) + m(^{16}\mathrm{O})\cdot c^2}\right)^2 \\ v(^{16}\mathrm{O}) = \sqrt{1 - \left(\frac{m(^{16}\mathrm{O})\cdot c^2}{E_{kin}(^{16}\mathrm{O}) + m(^{16}\mathrm{O})\cdot c^2}\right)^2}\cdot c \end{gathered} \qquad (31)
$$

For the given numbers we get:

$$
\begin{aligned} v(^{16}\mathrm{O}) &= \sqrt{1 - \left(\frac{15.99491\cdot 1.6605\cdot 10^{-27}\cdot\left(2.9979\cdot 10^{8}\right)^2}{50\cdot 1.602\cdot 10^{-13} + 15.99491\cdot\left(2.9979\cdot 10^{8}\right)^2}\right)^2}\cdot c = \\ &= \sqrt{1 - 0.99666^2}\cdot c = 0.08172\cdot c = 2.4498\cdot 10^{7}\ \mathrm{km/s} \end{aligned} \qquad (32)
$$

Now we can calculate:

$$
v(^{58}\mathrm{Ni}) = \frac{(15.99491 - 12.0)\cdot 2.4498\cdot 10^{7}\ \mathrm{km/s}}{(15.99491 - 12.0) + 53.93962\sqrt{1 - 0.08172^2}} = 1.6946\cdot 10^{6}\ \mathrm{km/s} \qquad (33)
$$

The mass of $^{58}$Ni in its excited state is then:

$$
\begin{aligned} m^*(^{58}\mathrm{N}i) &= \left(m(^{16}\mathrm{O}) - m(^{12}\mathrm{C})\right)\frac{\sqrt{1 - v^2(^{58}\mathrm{Ni})/c^2}}{\sqrt{1 - v^2(^{16}\mathrm{O})/c^2}}\cdot\frac{v(^{16}\mathrm{O})}{v(^{58}\mathrm{Ni})} = \\ &= (15.99491 - 12.0)\frac{\sqrt{1 - \left(1.6945\cdot 10^{6}/2.9979\cdot 10^{8}\right)^2}}{\sqrt{1 - 0.08172^2}}\cdot\frac{2.4498\cdot 10^{7}}{1.6945\cdot 10^{6}}\ \mathrm{a.m.u.} = \\ &= 57.9470\ \mathrm{a.m.u.} \end{aligned} \qquad (34)
$$

The excitation energy of $^{58}$Ni is then:

$$
\begin{aligned} E_x &= \left[m^*(^{58}\mathrm{Ni}) - m(^{58}\mathrm{Ni})\right]\cdot c^2 = \left(57.9470 - 57.93535\right)\cdot 1.6605\cdot 10^{-27}\left(2.9979\cdot 10^{8}\right)^2 = \\ &= 2.00722\cdot 10^{-12}/1.602\cdot 10^{-13}\ \mathrm{MeV/J} = 10.8636\ \mathrm{MeV} \end{aligned} \qquad (35)
$$

The relativistic and non-relativistic results are equal within 2 keV so both can be considered as correct –we can conclude that at the given beam energy, relativistic effects are not important.

**Task 5b)** For gamma-emission from the static nucleus, laws of conservation of energy and momentum give:

$$
\begin{gathered} E_x(^{58}\mathrm{Ni}) = E_\gamma + E_{\mathrm{recoil}} \\ p_\gamma = p_{\mathrm{recoil}} \end{gathered} \qquad (36)
$$

Gamma-ray and recoiled nucleus have, of course, opposite directions. For gamma-ray (photon), energy and momentum are related as:

$$
E_\gamma = p_\gamma\cdot c \qquad (37)
$$

In part a) we have seen that the nucleus motion in this energy range is not relativistic, so we have:

$$
E_{\mathrm{recoil}} = \frac{p_{\mathrm{recoil}}^2}{2m(^{58}\mathrm{Ni})} = \frac{p_\gamma^2}{2m(^{58}\mathrm{Ni})} = \frac{E_\gamma^2}{2m(^{58}\mathrm{Ni})\cdot c^2} \qquad (38)
$$

Inserting this into law of energy conservation Eq. (36), we get:

$$
E_x(^{58}\mathrm{Ni}) = E_\gamma + E_{\mathrm{recoil}} = E_\gamma + \frac{E_\gamma^2}{2m(^{58}\mathrm{Ni})\cdot c^2} \qquad (39)
$$

This reduces to the quadratic equation:

$$
E_\gamma^2 + 2m(^{58}\mathrm{Ni})c^2\cdot E_\gamma + 2m(^{58}\mathrm{Ni})c^2E_x(^{58}\mathrm{Ni}) = 0 \qquad (40)
$$

which gives the following solution:

$$
\begin{aligned} E_\gamma &= \frac{-2m(^{58}\mathrm{Ni})c^2 + \sqrt{4\left(m(^{58}\mathrm{Ni})c^2\right)^2 + 8m(^{58}\mathrm{Ni})c^2E_x(^{58}\mathrm{Ni})}}{2} = \\ &= \sqrt{\left(m(^{58}\mathrm{Ni})c^2\right)^2 + 2m(^{58}\mathrm{Ni})c^2E_x(^{58}\mathrm{Ni})} - m(^{58}\mathrm{Ni})c^2 \end{aligned} \qquad (41)
$$

Inserting numbers gives:

$$
E_\gamma = 10.8633\ \mathrm{MeV} \qquad (42)
$$

The equation (37) can also be reduced to an approximate equation before inserting numbers:

$$
E_\gamma = E_x\left(1 - \frac{E_x}{2m(^{58}\mathrm{Ni})c^2}\right) = 10.8633\ \mathrm{MeV} \qquad (43)
$$

The recoil energy is now easily found as:

$$
E_{\mathrm{recoil}} = E_x(^{58}\mathrm{Ni}) - E_\gamma = 1.1\ \mathrm{keV} \qquad (44)
$$

Due to the fact that nucleus emitting gamma-ray ($^{58}$Ni) is moving with the high velocity, the energy of gamma ray will be changed because of the Doppler effect. The relativistic Doppler effect (when source is moving towards observer/detector) is given with this formula:

$$
f_{\mathrm{detector}} = f_{\gamma,\mathrm{emitted}}\sqrt{\frac{1+\beta}{1-\beta}} \qquad (45)
$$

and since there is a simple relation between photon energy and frequency *(E=hf)*, we get the similar expression for energy:

$$
E_{\mathrm{detector}} = E_{\gamma,\mathrm{emitted}}\sqrt{\frac{1+\beta}{1-\beta}} \qquad (46)
$$

where $\beta = v/c$ and v is the velocity of emitter (the $^{58}$Ni nucleus). Taking the calculated value of the $^{58}$Ni velocity (equation 29) we get:

$$
E_{\mathrm{detector}} = E_{\gamma,\mathrm{emitted}}\sqrt{\frac{1+\beta}{1-\beta}} = 10.863\sqrt{\frac{1+0.00565}{1-0.00565}} = 10.925\ \mathrm{MeV} \qquad (47)
$$
