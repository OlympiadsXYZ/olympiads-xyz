## Условие

A very long solenoid has radius $b$, $n$ turns per unit length, and carries current $I$ as shown in the accompanying cross-sectional view.  The solenoid is firmly fixed in place and cannot rotate.

**a.** What is the magnetic field $\vec{B}$ inside the solenoid, far from its ends? **[5 т.]**

**b.** What is the magnetic energy density $u_\mathrm{B}$ stored in the solenoid? **[5 т.]**

There are two long cylindrical shells of length $h$ and mass $m$ coaxial to the solenoid – one, inside the solenoid at a radius $a$, carries a charge $+Q$, uniformly distributed over its surface;  the other, outside the solenoid at radius $c$, carries charge $–Q$.  $h \gg c$.  Use $r$ to represent the distance from the cylinders’ axis.  The cylinders are free to rotate about their axes.

**c.** What is the electric field $\vec{E}$ between the cylindrical shells, far from their ends? **[5 т.]**

**d.** What is the electric energy density $u_\mathrm{E}$ stored between the shells? **[5 т.]**

The current in the solenoid is gradually reduced from $I$ to zero at a constant rate, $\Delta I/\Delta t$.  The cylindrical shells begin to rotate.  Assume the magnetic field generated by the rotating cylindrical shells is negligible.

**e.** Calculate the gain in angular momentum of each cylinder about its axis when the current in the solenoid is reduced from $I$ to zero. **[15 т.]**

**f.** In addition to storing energy, fields can also store linear momentum and angular momentum. The density of linear momentum stored in electromagnetic fields is

$$
\varepsilon_0\vec{E} \times \vec{B}.
$$

Calculate the change in angular momentum of the electromagnetic fields about the cylinders’ axis as the current in the solenoid is reduced from $I$ to zero. **[15 т.]**

*$\{$Suggested by a problem in Introduction to Electrodynamics, 3rd Ed., by David J. Griffiths (Prentice Hall, Upper Saddle River, New Jersey, 1999), page 359.$\}$*

## Отговори

<Spoiler title="Покажи отговорите">

- **a.** $\vec{B} = \mu_0 nI\hat{z}$
- **b.** $u_B = \frac{\mu_0 n^2 I^2}{2}$
- **c.** $\vec{E} = +\frac{Q}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 hr}\hat{r}$
- **d.** $u_E = \frac{Q^2}{8\pi^2\varepsilon_0 h^2 r^2}$
- **e.** $\Delta\vec{L}_a = \tfrac{1}{2}\mu_0 nIa^2Q\hat{z},\quad \Delta\vec{L}_c = -\tfrac{1}{2}\mu_0 nIb^2Q\hat{z}$
- **f.** $\vec{L}_{EM} = -\tfrac{1}{2}Q\mu_0 nI(b^2 - a^2)\hat{z}$

</Spoiler>

## Решение

<Spoiler title="Покажи официалното решение">

B2. a. Using $\hat{z}$ to represent a unit vector in the $z$-direction, $\vec{B} = \mu_0 nI\hat{z}$ for $r < b$. (It is not necessary to derive this result from Ampere’s Law.)

b.
$$
u_B = \frac{1}{2\mu_0}B^2 = \frac{\mu_0 n^2 I^2}{2}.
$$

It is also possible to derive this result from $U = \frac{1}{2}LI^2$.

If edge effects are ignored $U = u_B hA$

where $h$ is the length and $A$ is the cross-sectional area of the solenoid. Combining this with
$$
LI = N\Phi = (nh)(BA)
$$

yields
$$
u_B hA = \tfrac{1}{2}nhBAI,
$$

or
$$
u_B = \tfrac{1}{2}nBI = \tfrac{1}{2}\mu_0 n^2 I^2
$$

c. The charge per unit length on the inner cylindrical shell is $\lambda = +Q/h$.

Letting $\hat{r}$ represent a unit vector in the $r$-direction, Gauss’s Law for cylindrical symmetry yields

$$
\vec{E} = +\frac{Q}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 hr}\hat{r}.
$$

d.
$$
u_E = \tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon_0 E^2 = \frac{Q^2}{8\pi^2\varepsilon_0 h^2 r^2}.
$$

e. Use Faradays Law to find the induced electric field at the location of each charged cylinder.

$$
\oint \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}.
$$

$\vec{E}$ is constant around a circular path, the cross-section of the cylinder, $\oint \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} = E2\pi r$.

The magnetic flux is $\Phi_B = BA$

where $A$ is the enclosed area that has a magnetic field. Thus

$$
E2\pi r = -A\frac{dB}{dt} = -A\mu_0 n\frac{dI}{dt}.
$$

Since $I$ is decreasing at a constant rate
$$
E2\pi r = -A\mu_0 n\frac{\Delta I}{\Delta t}
$$

For the inner cylinder, $r = a$ and $A = \pi a^2$, and
$$
E_a 2\pi a = -\pi a^2\mu_0 n\frac{\Delta I}{\Delta t}.
$$

Therefore
$$
E_a = -\frac{\mu_0 na}{2}\frac{\Delta I}{\Delta t}.
$$

In order to oppose the decrease in $I$, $E$ is in the same direction as $I$

$$
\vec{E}_a = -\frac{\mu_0 na}{2}\frac{\Delta I}{\Delta t}\hat{\phi}
$$

For the outer cylinder, $r = c$ and $A = \pi b^2$, since the field $B = 0$ for $r > b$

and
$$
E_c 2\pi c = -\pi b^2\mu_0 n\frac{\Delta I}{\Delta t}.
$$

Therefore
$$
E_c = -\frac{\mu_0 nb^2}{2c}\frac{\Delta I}{\Delta t}.
$$

with
$$
\vec{E}_c = -\frac{\mu_0 nb^2}{2c}\frac{\Delta I}{\Delta t}\hat{\phi}
$$

Each cylinder experiences a torque caused by the electric force.

$$
\frac{d\vec{L}}{dt} = \vec{\tau} = \vec{r} \times \vec{F}_E.
$$

For the inner cylinder
$$
\frac{d\vec{L}_a}{dt} = a\hat{r} \times Q\left(-\frac{\mu_0 na}{2}\frac{\Delta I}{\Delta t}\hat{\phi}\right) = -\frac{\mu_0 na^2Q}{2}\frac{\Delta I}{\Delta t}\hat{z}.
$$

Since $I$ decreases at a constant rate, $L$ also changes at a constant rate.

$$
\frac{\Delta\vec{L}_a}{\Delta t} = -\frac{\mu_0 na^2Q}{2}\frac{(0 - I)}{\Delta t}\hat{z}
$$

$$
\Delta\vec{L}_a = \tfrac{1}{2}\mu_0 nIa^2Q\hat{z}.
$$

For the outer cylinder
$$
\frac{d\vec{L}_c}{dt} = c\hat{r} \times (-Q)\left(-\frac{\mu_0 nb^2}{2c}\frac{\Delta I}{\Delta t}\hat{\phi}\right) = +\frac{\mu_0 nb^2Q}{2}\frac{\Delta I}{\Delta t}\hat{z}.
$$

and
$$
\frac{\Delta\vec{L}_c}{\Delta t} = +\frac{\mu_0 nb^2Q}{2}\frac{(0 - I)}{\Delta t}\hat{z}
$$

$$
\Delta\vec{L}_c = -\tfrac{1}{2}\mu_0 nIb^2Q\hat{z}.
$$

f. $E = 0$ for $r < a$ and $B = 0$ for $r > b$. Using $\vec{\pi}_{EM}$ to represent the linear momentum density

$$
\vec{\pi}_{EM} = \varepsilon_0\vec{E} \times \vec{B} = \varepsilon_0\left(\frac{Q}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 hr}\right)\hat{r} \times (\mu_0 nI)\hat{z} = -\frac{Q\mu_0 nI}{2\pi hr}\hat{\phi} \quad \text{for } a < r < b
$$

and
$$
\vec{\pi}_{EM} = 0 \quad \text{for } r < a \text{ and } b < r
$$

Using $\vec{\ell}_{EM}$ to represent the angular momentum density

$$
\vec{\ell}_{EM} = \vec{r} \times \vec{\pi}_{EM}.
$$

In the region $a < r < b$,
$$
\vec{\ell}_{EM} = \vec{r} \times \vec{\pi}_{EM} = (r\hat{r}) \times \left(-\frac{Q\mu_0 nI}{2\pi hr}\hat{\phi}\right) = -\frac{Q\mu_0 nI}{2\pi h}\hat{z}.
$$

The angular momentum density is constant in the region $a < r < b$ and zero outside. Therefore the total angular momentum originally stored in the fields is

$$
\vec{L}_{EM} = (\text{volume containing field})\,\vec{\ell}_{EM}
$$

$$
\vec{L}_{EM} = \left[h\pi(b^2 - a^2)\right]\left(-\frac{Q\mu_0 nI}{2\pi h}\hat{z}\right) = -\tfrac{1}{2}Q\mu_0 nI(b^2 - a^2)\hat{z}
$$

When the current is reduced to zero, this angular momentum goes to zero. Therefore the change in angular momentum is

$$
\vec{L}_{EM} = -\tfrac{1}{2}Q\mu_0 nI(b^2 - a^2)\hat{z}.
$$


</Spoiler>
