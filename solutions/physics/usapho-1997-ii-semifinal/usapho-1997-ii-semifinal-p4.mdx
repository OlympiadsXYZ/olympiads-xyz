## Условие

An atom in an excited state typically decays by emitting a photon. The atom is originally in some excited state $S'$, and after the decay it is in the ground state $S$. We usually think of the photon as carrying away the energy difference between states $S'$ and $S$. In this problem we examine this process closely.

**(a)** Consider a stationary, isolated atom in some excited state $S'$. Before decay the mass of the atom is $M'$. The atom decays into the ground state $S$. In the ground state the mass of the atom is $M$. Thus the energy $(M'-M)c^2$, which we denote here as $E_o$, is the energy that is released by the atom, and is therefore an upper limit on the energy that the photon could possibly carry away ($c$ is the speed of light). Let $E$ denote the energy of the outgoing photon. Without solving any equation, by merely giving an qualitative argument, explain why $E$ must be strictly less than $E_o$ in the case of an isolated excited atom undergoing radiative decay. **[4 т.]**

**(b)** Quantify your answer to part (a) by showing that, approximately,

$E \approx E_o\,[\,1 - (\,E_o / 2Mc^2\,)\,]$, where $E_o / 2Mc^2 \ll 1$. **[6 т.]**

**(c)** Consider an atom decaying from the excited state $S'$ to the ground state $S$, where $Mc^2 = 2\times10^{11}$ eV and $E_o = 4\times10^{5}$ eV. A photon energy corresponds to a radiation frequency. Thus the energy difference $E_o - E$ corresponds to a frequency shift $\Delta f = f_o - f$. Calculate the fractional frequency shift $\Delta f / f_o$. **[5 т.]**

**(d)** Another way to produce a frequency shift is the Doppler effect, produced by relative motion between source and observer. The atom of part (c) emits the photon energy $E$, which corresponds to light frequency $f$. With what speed and in what direction would an observer have to move relative to this photon, to Doppler-shift its frequency back to the value $f_o$ corresponding to the energy $E_o$? The observer’s speed $v$ is $\ll c$. **[5 т.]**

<Spoiler title="Покажи отговорите">

- **(a)** To conserve momentum the atom must recoil, so it carries some kinetic energy K, leaving less than Eo for the photon.
- **(c)** 0.000001
- **(d)** 300 m/s

## Решение

<Spoiler title="Покажи официалното решение">

**(a)** To conserve momentum the atom must recoil. Thus after emitting the photon, the atom carries some kinetic energy $K$, leaving *less* than $E_o$ for photon.

**(b)** Before decay: $(\text{Energy, momentum}) = (M'c^2, 0)$. After decay: atom $(Mc^2 + K, -p)$, photon $(E, E/c)$.

*Conservation of Energy*

$$
M'c^2 = Mc^2 + K + E
$$

$$
(M' - M)c^2 = K + E
$$

$$
E_o = K + E
$$

*Cons. of Momentum*

$$
0 = \frac{E}{c} - p
$$

$$
p = \frac{E}{c}
$$

[since the atom is heavy, $v \ll c$ and $K = p^2/2m$, or $p \approx \sqrt{2mK}$ for *atom*]

$$
\therefore\ \sqrt{2mK} = \frac{E}{c} \ \Rightarrow\ K = \frac{E^2}{2mc^2}
$$

$\{$ or, relativistically,

$$
(Mc^2)^2 = (K + Mc^2)^2 - (pc)^2
$$

$$
\Rightarrow\ K = Mc^2\left[\sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{E}{Mc^2}\right)^2} - 1\right] \approx E^2/2mc^2 \ \}
$$

[given $\dfrac{E_o}{2mc^2} \ll 1$ since $K$ and $E$ are $< E_o$, $\dfrac{K}{2mc^2}$ and $\dfrac{E}{2mc^2}$ are $\ll 1$ too]

Conservation of Energy Equation becomes

$$
E_o = \frac{E^2}{2mc^2} + E
$$

$$
\begin{aligned} \Rightarrow\ E &= E_o\left(1 + \frac{E}{2mc^2}\right)^{-1} \\ &\approx E_o\left(1 - \frac{E}{2mc^2}\right) \qquad \text{Iterate} \\ &\approx E_o\left[1 - \frac{E_o(1 - E/2mc^2)}{2mc^2}\right] \end{aligned}
$$

[Alternate: Quadratic formula —

$$
E^2 + \gamma E + \gamma E_o = 0 \quad \text{where } \gamma = 2mc^2
$$

$$
\Rightarrow\ E = \frac{\gamma}{2}\left[-1 \pm \left(1 + \frac{4E_o}{\gamma}\right)^{1/2}\right] \approx \frac{\gamma}{2}\left[-1 \pm \left(1 + \frac{2E_o}{\gamma} - \frac{2E_o^2}{\gamma^2}\right)\right]
$$

since $E > 0$ take $+$

$$
E = E_o(1 - \gamma)
$$

Note: 2nd order needed in Binomial]

If $\delta \equiv \dfrac{E_o}{2mc^2} \ll 1$ then $\dfrac{EE_o}{(2mc^2)^2} \approx \delta^2$ is negligible

$$
\Rightarrow\ \boxed{E \approx E_o\left(1 - \frac{E_o}{2mc^2}\right)}
$$

**(c)** From part (b),

$$
E_o - E = \frac{E_o^2}{2mc^2}
$$

Using $E = hf$, and writing $E_o^2 = hf_oE_o$,

$$
h(f_o - f) = \frac{hf_oE_o}{2mc^2}
$$

$$
\frac{\Delta f}{f_o} = \frac{E_o}{2mc^2} = \frac{4\times10^{5}\,\mathrm{eV}}{2(2\times10^{11}\,\mathrm{eV})} = 10^{-6}
$$

**(d)** For $v \ll c$, Doppler shift says

$$
\frac{v}{c} \approx \frac{\Delta\lambda}{\lambda} \qquad \text{where } c = \lambda f
$$

Since $f_o > f$, the observer must move towards the photon

$$
\Delta\lambda = c\,\Delta\!\left(\frac{1}{f}\right) \approx -\frac{\Delta f}{f^2}\,c
$$

[Alternate: $c = \lambda f$; $dc = 0 = \lambda\,df + f\,d\lambda$; $d\lambda = -\lambda\dfrac{df}{f}$; $\dfrac{d\lambda}{\lambda} = -\dfrac{df}{f}$; minus sign means that a *decrease* in $\lambda$ corresponds to *increase* in frequency!]

$\therefore$ (in magnitude)

$$
\begin{aligned} \frac{v}{c} &\approx \frac{\Delta f}{f^2}\,\frac{c}{\lambda} \\ &= \frac{\Delta f}{f} \end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned} v &= \frac{\Delta f}{f_o}\,c \\ &= 10^{-6}\,c \qquad \left(\text{using } \frac{\Delta f}{f_o} = 10^{-6} \text{ from part (c)}\right) \\ &= 300\,\mathrm{m/s} \end{aligned}
$$
