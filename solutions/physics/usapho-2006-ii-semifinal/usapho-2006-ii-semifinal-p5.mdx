Consider a wire loop of width $X$ and height $D$. The loop is held at rest vertically. There is a uniform magnetic field $B$ passing through the loop. However, the magnetic field abruptly decreases to zero everywhere below the location of the bottom edge of the loop at $t=0$. The magnetic field is perpendicular to the plane of the loop. The mass of the loop is $m$. At time $t=0$, the loop is released from rest so that it begins to fall out of the magnetic field. Let $y(t)$ be the distance that the bottom edge has traveled out of the magnetic field at $t$ seconds after the loop has been released. $y(0)=0$.

<ProblemSection id="part-a">

### a.

Suppose that the loop has resistance $R$ but negligible inductance. Assume that $D$ is large enough that the loop very nearly reaches terminal velocity before the loop entirely falls out of the magnetic field. Express your answers to part (a) in terms of $X$, $D$, $B$, $m$, $g$, $R$, and $t$.

**i.** Find the terminal velocity, $v_{\rm term}$, of the loop. **[5 т.]**

**ii.** Answers to part (a) ii–iv may include the symbol $v_{\rm term}$ in addition to any of the symbols listed above.

Derive an expression for velocity as a function of time that is valid until the loop is entirely out of the magnetic field. **[7 т.]**

**iii.** Derive an expression for the rate at which thermal energy is dissipated due to the resistance of the loop. **[3 т.]**

**iv.** Determine the total amount of thermal energy dissipated from $t=0$ until the time that the top edge of the loop just starts to leave the magnetic field. **[5 т.]**

</ProblemSection>

<ProblemSection id="part-b">

### b.

Suppose instead that the loop is made of a superconducting material so that it has negligible resistance. In this case, we cannot neglect the self-inductance of the loop $L$. Assume that $D$ is large enough that the loop never falls entirely out of the magnetic field. Express your answers to part (b) in terms of $X$, $D$, $B$, $m$, $g$, $L$, and $t$.

**i.** Prove mathematically that the loop oscillates up and down in simple harmonic motion and determine the period of the oscillation. **[12 т.]**

**ii.** Derive an expression for $y(t)$. **[6 т.]**

**iii.** Determine the maximum kinetic energy of the loop. **[4 т.]**

**iv.** Determine the maximum energy stored in the magnetic field of the loop. **[5 т.]**

**v.** At what times is the energy stored in the magnetic field at its maximum value? **[3 т.]**

</ProblemSection>

(Based on a problem by W. M. Saslow, Am. J. Phys. 55, 986 (1987).)

## Решение

<Spoiler title="Покажи официалното решение">

Source note: the official key uses an upward coordinate with $y(0)=D$, whereas the question defines downward distance with $y(0)=0$. The key’s printed equations and signs are retained, including $-mg$ in (B1-15), the missing minus sign in (B1-21), and the positive coefficient in (B1-31). The final timing formula gives the first low point only, although the question asks for times.

<ProblemSection id="solution-part-a">

### a.

For simplicity we write $v_t$ instead of $v_{\rm term}$. Wherever possible we will solve the problem in the simplest possible manner.

**i.** The magnitude of the force of gravity on the wire is

$$
F_g=mg,\qquad (B1-1)
$$

the magnitude of the magnetic force on a current $I$ in the wire is

$$
F_m=BIX,\qquad (B1-2)
$$

the induced emf, $V$, in the wire when traveling with speed $v$ is

$$
V=-BvX,\qquad (B1-3)
$$

and finally, the relationship between the emf and current is

$$
V=IR.\qquad (B1-4)
$$

Terminal velocity occurs when there is no acceleration, so that the net force is zero. Then

$$
mg=BIX,\qquad (B1-5)
$$

$$
mg=B\frac VRX,\qquad (B1-6)
$$

$$
mg=-B\frac{BvX}{R}X,\qquad (B1-7)
$$

$$
-\frac{mgR}{B^2X^2}=v.\qquad (B1-8)
$$

It is traditional to give the terminal speed, and consequently we don’t usually write the negative sign indicating downward motion. Henceforth,

$$
v_t=\frac{mgR}{B^2X^2}.\qquad (B1-9)
$$
**[5 т.]**

**ii.** Let the above symbols, except for $v_t$, now be variables in time. Define a dot notation for time derivatives, so that velocity is given by

$$
v=\frac{dy}{dt}=\dot y,\qquad (B1-10)
$$

and

$$
a=\frac{dv}{dt}=\dot v=\ddot y.\qquad (B1-11)
$$

The acceleration of the wire is given by Newton’s second law,

$$
ma=F_m-F_g,\qquad (B1-12)
$$

$$
m\dot v=BIX-mg,\qquad (B1-13)
$$

$$
m\dot v=-B\frac{BvX}{R}X-mg,\qquad (B1-14)
$$

$$
\dot v=-\frac{B^2X^2}{mR}v-mg,\qquad (B1-15)
$$

$$
\dot v=-g\left(\frac v{v_t}+1\right).\qquad (B1-16)
$$

The source prints an empty equation number (B1-17).

We must now integrate this expression,

$$
\frac{dv}{dt}=-g\left(\frac v{v_t}+1\right),\qquad (B1-18)
$$

$$
\frac{dv}{v+v_t}=-\frac g{v_t}\,dt,\qquad (B1-19)
$$

$$
\int\frac{dv}{v+v_t}=-\int\frac g{v_t}\,dt,\qquad (B1-20)
$$

$$
\ln\left(\frac v{v_t}+1\right)=\frac{gt}{v_t}.\qquad (B1-21)
$$

Solving for $v$:

$$
v=v_t\left(e^{-gt/v_t}-1\right).\qquad (B1-22)
$$
**[7 т.]**

**iii.** Thermal energy dissipates at a rate given by

$$
P=IV=V^2/R,\qquad (B1-23)
$$

so

$$
P=\frac{B^2v^2X^2}{R}\qquad (B1-24)
$$

$$
=mgv_t\left(e^{-gt/v_t}-1\right)^2.\qquad (B1-25)
$$
**[3 т.]**

**iv.** It is tempting to integrate the above expression. Don’t do it! Instead, focus on the fact that the energy dissipated is equal to the change in energy of the wire. Note that it is moving at terminal speed when it completely leaves the field region, so

$$
E=mgD-\frac12mv_t^2.\qquad (B1-26)
$$
**[5 т.]**

</ProblemSection>

<ProblemSection id="solution-part-b">

### b.

Be warned that we never actually need to find $L$ in this problem, so don’t spend time trying to calculate it!

**i.** Equations B1-1, B1-2, and B1-3 are still true. But now the emf is related to the inductance and the change in current by

$$
V=L\dot I.\qquad (B1-27)
$$

The dot above the $I$ means time derivative. Combining,

$$
m\dot v=BIX-mg,\qquad (B1-28)
$$

$$
m\ddot v=B\dot I X,\qquad (B1-29)
$$

$$
=-B\frac VLX,\qquad (B1-30)
$$

$$
=\frac{B^2X^2}{L}v.\qquad (B1-31)
$$

This is the differential equation for a simple harmonic oscillator, with angular frequency $\omega$ given by

$$
\omega=\sqrt{\frac{B^2X^2}{mL}}.\qquad (B1-32)
$$

The period of oscillation is then

$$
T=\frac{2\pi}{\omega}=\frac{2\pi\sqrt{mL}}{BX}.\qquad (B1-33)
$$
**[12 т.]**

**ii.** Oscillatory motion is necessarily of the form

$$
y=A\cos(\omega t+\phi)+B,\qquad (B1-34)
$$

where $A$, $B$, and $\phi$ are constants. Since $y$ is a maximum when $t=0$, we can conclude that $\phi=0$. The acceleration will be given by $a=\ddot y$, and the maximum acceleration is then $a_{\max}=A\omega^2$. This maximum acceleration occurs when the object is released, and there is not yet any current through the wire, so, from Eq. B1-28,

$$
mA\omega^2=mg,\qquad (B1-35)
$$

$$
A=\frac{mgL}{B^2X^2}.\qquad (B1-36)
$$

Finally, $y=D$ when $t=0$, so

$$
y=A\cos\omega t+D-A,\qquad (B1-37)
$$

$$
=\frac{mgL}{B^2X^2}\left(\cos\left(\sqrt{\frac{B^2X^2}{mL}}\,t\right)-1\right)+D.\qquad (B1-38)
$$
**[6 т.]**

**iii.** Using $v_{\max}=A\omega$,

$$
K_{\max}=\frac12mv_{\max}^2,\qquad (B1-39)
$$

$$
=\frac12m\left(\frac g\omega\right)^2,\qquad (B1-40)
$$

$$
=\frac12\frac{g^2m^2L}{B^2X^2}.\qquad (B1-41)
$$
**[4 т.]**

**iv.** At the bottom point of the path all of the “lost” potential energy of the wire must be stored in the magnetic field, so

$$
E_B=2mgA=\frac{2m^2g^2L}{B^2X^2}.\qquad (B1-42)
$$
**[5 т.]**

**v.** At the low point of the oscillation the magnetic energy will be maximal, or

$$
t_{\max}=\frac T2=\frac{\pi\sqrt{mL}}{BX}.\qquad (B1-43)
$$
**[3 т.]**

</ProblemSection>
