In this problem, we present a simple model of seasonal temperature variation. We ignore temperature and illumination changes between day and night, considering only daily average values. We also ignore all forms of heat transfer other than radiation, including any heat transfer between areas of the surface of the Earth. Finally, we ignore the effects of the atmosphere and clouds.

We therefore consider a patch of ground with heat capacity **per unit area** $c$ and emissivity $\epsilon$, illuminated by light of varying intensity $I$. The patch therefore has a varying temperature $T$.

<ProblemSection id="part-a">

### a. (8 т.)

Develop an equation relating $T$, $\frac{dT}{dt}$, $I$, $c$, $\epsilon$, and the Stefan-Boltzmann constant $\sigma$.

</ProblemSection>

<ProblemSection id="parts-b-f">

### b.–f.

Figure 1 shows the yearly variation in average daily sunlight intensity on Earth for various latitudes. The variation is not sinusoidal, but for temperate latitudes, it may be approximated as a sinusoid. In particular, Figure 2 shows the variation in College Park, Maryland (at $39^\circ$ N latitude), together with a good sinusoidal approximation. Therefore, we assume that the intensity has the form $I=I_0+I_1\sin(\omega t)$. If the annual variation in temperature is small compared to the absolute temperature, we will likewise have $T=T_0+T_1\sin(\omega t-\phi)$, where by hypothesis $T_1\ll T_0$.

## Решение

<Spoiler title="Покажи официалното решение">

Source note: the printed signs in (B2-15) and (B2-16) differ from the preceding and following lines; they are retained as printed. The question’s Figure2 caption prints a minus sign in its fitted sinusoid.

<ProblemSection id="solution-part-a">

### a. (8 т.)

The radiation power absorbed by the patch of area $a$ is

$$
\epsilon aI,\qquad (B2-1)
$$

while the power radiated from the patch is

$$
\epsilon a\sigma T^4.\qquad (B2-2)
$$

The imbalance causes a temperature change given by

$$
ca\frac{dT}{dt}=\epsilon aI-\epsilon a\sigma T^4,\qquad (B2-3)
$$

$$
\frac c\epsilon\frac{dT}{dt}=I-\sigma T^4.\qquad (B2-4)
$$

</ProblemSection>

<ProblemSection id="solution-part-b">

### b. (14 т.)

We will need to do an expansion of $T^4$.

$$
T^4=T_0^4\left(1+\frac{T_1}{T_0}\sin(\omega t-\phi)\right)^4,\qquad (B2-5)
$$

$$
\approx T_0^4\left(1+4\frac{T_1}{T_0}\sin(\omega t-\phi)\right).\qquad (B2-6)
$$

Insert this and the given expression for $I$ into Eq. B2-4,

$$
\frac c\epsilon(T_1\omega\cos(\omega t-\phi))=\qquad (B2-7)
$$

$$
I_0+I_1\sin(\omega t)-\sigma T_0^4\left(1+4\frac{T_1}{T_0}\sin(\omega t-\phi)\right),\qquad (B2-8)
$$

or

$$
\sigma T_0^4+4\sigma T_0^3T_1\sin(\omega t-\phi)+\frac{c\omega T_1}{\epsilon}\cos(\omega t-\phi)=I_0+I_1\sin(\omega t).\qquad (B2-9)
$$

Clearly, then,

$$
A=\sigma T_0^4,\qquad (B2-10)
$$

$$
B=4\sigma T_0^3T_1,\qquad (B2-11)
$$

$$
C=\frac{c\omega T_1}{\epsilon}.\qquad (B2-12)
$$

</ProblemSection>

<ProblemSection id="solution-part-c">

### c. (9 т.)

The only way that it can work is if $A=I_0$. Of course, one would expect the average intensity to be related to the average temperature in this way.

Expand the left hand sine and cosine terms according to the angle addition formulae:

$$
\sin(\omega t-\phi)=-\cos\omega t\sin\phi+\sin\omega t\cos\phi,\qquad (B2-13)
$$

$$
\cos(\omega t-\phi)=\cos\omega t\cos\phi+\sin\omega t\sin\phi.\qquad (B2-14)
$$

Substitute into Equation (1) from the question paper.

$$
B(-\cos\omega t\sin\phi+\sin\omega t\cos\phi)+C(\cos\omega t\cos\phi-\sin\omega t\sin\phi)=I_1\sin\omega t.\qquad (B2-15)
$$

This, after collecting terms, yields

$$
(-B\sin\phi-C\cos\phi)\cos\omega t+(B\cos\phi+C\sin\phi)\sin\omega t=I_1\sin\omega t.\qquad (B2-16)
$$

The cosine term must vanish, and this only happens if

$$
-B\sin\phi+C\cos\phi=0,\qquad (B2-17)
$$

leaving the sine term, so

$$
B\cos\phi+C\sin\phi=I_1.\qquad (B2-18)
$$

Combining,

$$
B\cos\phi+B\frac{\sin\phi}{\cos\phi}\sin\phi=I_1,\qquad (B2-19)
$$

$$
B=I_1\cos\phi,\qquad (B2-20)
$$

and then

$$
C=B\frac{\sin\phi}{\cos\phi}=I_1\sin\phi.\qquad (B2-21)
$$

</ProblemSection>

<ProblemSection id="solution-part-d">

### d. (7 т.)

Evaluate $B/A$.

$$
\frac BA=\frac{I_1}{I_0}\cos\phi,\qquad (B2-22)
$$

and

$$
\frac BA=\frac{4\sigma T_0^3T_1}{\sigma T_0^4}=4\frac{T_1}{T_0},\qquad (B2-23)
$$

we have

$$
\frac{I_1}{I_0}\cos\phi=4\frac{T_1}{T_0}.\qquad (B2-24)
$$

Since $I_1/I_0=0.126/0.244=0.516$ we ought be concerned that $T_1/T_0$ might not be a sufficiently small quantity for this approximation!

</ProblemSection>

<ProblemSection id="solution-part-e">

### e. (9 т.)

Since the temperature difference is twice the oscillation amplitude,

$$
\Delta T=2T_1,\qquad (B2-25)
$$

so we are interested in graphing

$$
\Delta T=\frac{T_0}{2}\frac{I_1}{I_0}\cos\phi,\qquad (B2-26)
$$

$$
=(73.1\text{ K})\cos\left(t\frac\pi{6\text{ month}}\right).\qquad (B2-27)
$$

### f. (3 т.)

According to the data on the question paper the peak temperature happens about one month after the solstice. The predicted summer/winter temperature difference would then be about $62\,\mathrm{C}^\circ$, which is about 2.5 times that seen in the average temperature data for BWI.
