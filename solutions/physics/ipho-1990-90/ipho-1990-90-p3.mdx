## Условие

A 'millisecond pulsar' is a source of radiation in the universe that emits very short pulses with a period of one to several milliseconds. This radiation is in the radio range of wavelengths; and a suitable radio receiver can be used to detect the separate pulses and thereby to measure the period with great accuracy.

These radio pulses originate from the surface of a particular sort of star, the so-called neutron star. These stars are very compact: they have a mass of the same order of magnitude as that of the sun, but their radius is only a few tens kilometers. They spin very quickly. Because of the fast rotation, a neutron star is slightly flattened (oblate).  
Assume the axial cross-section of the surface to be an ellipse with almost equal axes.  
Let $r_p$ be the polar and $r_e$ the equatorial radii; and let us define the flattening factor by:

$$
\epsilon=\frac{r_e-r_p}{r_p}
$$

Consider a neutron star with

| Quantity | Value |
| --- | --- |
| a mass of | $2.0\cdot10^{30}\,\mathrm{kg}$ |
| an average radius of | $1.0\cdot10^4\,\mathrm m$ |
| and a rotation period of | $2.0\cdot10^{-2}\,\mathrm s$ |

**a-** Calculate the flattening factor, given that the gravitational constant is $6.67\cdot10^{-11}\,\mathrm{N\,m^2\,kg^{-2}}$. **[5 т.]**

In the long run (over many years) the rotation of the star slows down, due to energy loss, and this leads to a decrease in the flattening. The star has however a solid crust that floats on a liquid interior. The solid crust resists a continuous adjustment to equilibrium shape. Instead, starquakes occur with sudden changes in the shape of the crust towards equilibrium. During and after such a star-quake the angular velocity is observed to change according to figure 1.

## Решение

<Spoiler title="Покажи решението от архива">

<ProblemSection id="solution-a-method1">

### a- 1st method

For equilibrium we have $F_c=F_g+N$ where $N$ is normal to the surface.

<figure className="problem-figure problem-figure--sized" style={{'--fig-w': '46.4%', '--fig-max': '297px'}}>
<img src="https://pub-43290baaaff14857b5dd59610ea438c7.r2.dev/problems/ipho-1990-90/p3-sol-fig1.png" alt="Normal force N, gravity Fg, centrifugal Fc, angular velocity omega and angles alpha phi at the stellar surface." width="928" height="510" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
</figure>


Resolving into horizontal and vertical components, we find:

$$
\begin{aligned}F_g\cos(\phi)&=F_c+N\sin(\alpha)\\F_g\sin(\phi)&=N\cos(\alpha)\end{aligned}\quad\rightarrow\quad F_g\cos(\phi)=F_c+F_g\sin(\phi)\operatorname{tg}(\alpha)
$$

From:

$$
F_g=\frac{GM}{r^2},\quad F_c=\omega^2r,\quad x=r\cos(\phi),\quad y=r\sin(\phi)\quad\text{en}\quad\operatorname{tg}(\alpha)=\frac{dy}{dx}
$$

we find:

$$
y\,dy+\left(1-\frac{\omega^2r^3}{GM}\right)x\,dx=0
$$

where:

$$
\frac{\omega^2r^3}{GM}\approx7\cdot10^{-4}
$$

This means that, although $r$ depends on $x$ and $y$, the change in the factor in front of $x\,dx$ is so slight that we can take it to be constant. The solution of Eq. (1) is then an ellipse:

$$
\frac{x^2}{r_e^2}+\frac{y^2}{r_p^2}=1\quad\rightarrow\quad\frac{r_p}{r_e}=\sqrt{1-\frac{\omega^2r^3}{GM}}\approx1-\frac{\omega^2r^3}{2GM}
$$

and from this it follows that:

$$
\epsilon=\frac{r_e-r_p}{r_e}=\frac{\omega^2r^3}{2GM}\approx3{,}7\cdot10^{-4}
$$

</ProblemSection>

<ProblemSection id="solution-a-method2">

### 2nd method

For a point mass of $1\,\mathrm{kg}$ on the surface,

$$
U_{pot}=-\frac{GM}{r}\qquad U_{kin}=\frac12\omega^2r^2\cos^2(\phi)
$$

The form of the surface is such that $U_{pot}-U_{kin}=\text{constant}$. For the equator ($\Phi=0$, $r=r_e$) and for the pole ($\Phi=\pi/2$, $r=r_p$) we have:

$$
\frac{GM}{r_p}=\frac{GM}{r_e}+\frac12\omega^2r_e^2\quad\rightarrow\quad\frac{r_e}{r_p}=1+\frac{\omega^2r_e^3}{2GM}
$$

Thus:

$$
\epsilon=\frac{r_e-r_p}{r_e}=\frac{1+\frac{\omega^2r_e^3}{2GM}-1}{1+\frac{\omega^2r_e^3}{2GM}}\approx\frac{\omega^2r_e^3}{2GM}\approx3{,}7\cdot10^{-4}
$$

</ProblemSection>

<ProblemSection id="solution-b">

### b-

As a consequence of the star-quake, the moment of inertia of the crust $I_m$ decreases by $\Delta I_m$.

<figure className="problem-figure problem-figure--sized" style={{'--fig-w': '40.4%', '--fig-max': '259px'}}>
<img src="https://pub-43290baaaff14857b5dd59610ea438c7.r2.dev/problems/ipho-1990-90/p3-sol-fig2.png" alt="Repeated native angular-velocity graph in the neutron-star solution, retaining its axes and omega labels." width="810" height="478" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
</figure>


From the conservation of angular momentum, we have:

$$
I_m\omega_0=(I_m-\Delta I_m)\omega_1\quad\rightarrow\quad\Delta I_m=I_m\frac{\omega_1-\omega_0}{\omega_1}
$$

After the internal friction has equalized the angular velocities of the crust and the core, we have:

$$
(I_m+I_c)\omega_0=(I_m+I_c-\Delta I_m)\omega_2\quad\rightarrow\quad\Delta I_m=(I_m+I_c)\frac{\omega_2-\omega_0}{\omega_2}
$$

$$
\frac{I_m}{I_m+I_c}=\frac{(\omega_2-\omega_0)\omega_1}{(\omega_1-\omega_0)\omega_2}\quad\rightarrow\quad1-\frac{I_c}{I_m+I_c}=\frac{(\omega_2-\omega_0)\omega_1}{(\omega_1-\omega_0)\omega_2}
$$

$$
I\;(:)\;R^2
$$

$$
\rightarrow\quad\frac{I_c}{I_m+I_c}=\frac{r_c^2}{r^2}\quad\rightarrow\quad\frac{r_c}{r}=\sqrt{1-\frac{(\omega_2-\omega_0)\omega_1}{(\omega_1-\omega_0)\omega_2}}\approx0.95
$$

</ProblemSection>
