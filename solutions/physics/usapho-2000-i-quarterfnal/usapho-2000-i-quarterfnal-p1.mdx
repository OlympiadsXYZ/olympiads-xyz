## Условие

A 4.00-meter long catapult, hinged at ground level, is used to fire large rocks as shown in the diagram at the right. A rock starts from rest with the catapult arm horizontal and is accelerated uniformly until it is released when the catapult arm makes an angle of $(\pi/3)\ rad$ ($=60^\circ$) with respect to the horizontal and the rock has a tangential velocity of 10.0 m/s.

<figure className="problem-figure problem-figure--sized" style={{'--fig-w': '32%', '--fig-max': '205px'}}>
<img src="https://pub-43290baaaff14857b5dd59610ea438c7.r2.dev/problems/usapho-2000-i-quarterfnal/p1-fig1-astra-r6-20260925.png" alt="Catapult of length4.00m at pi/3, initial ground position X." width="641" height="615" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
</figure>

**a.** How far from the original starting position (marked with an X in the diagram) does the rock land? The ground is level. Assume air resistance is negligible. **[17 т.]**

**b.** What was the angular acceleration of the rock before its release? **[8 т.]**

## Отговори

<Spoiler title="Покажи отговорите">

- **a.** 15 m
- **b.** 2.98 rad/s²

</Spoiler>

## Решение

<Spoiler title="Покажи официалното решение">

**a.** The tangential velocity of the rock as it leaves the catapult is the initial velocity of the projectile $v_0=v_T=10.0\ m/s$ with $\theta_0=(\pi/6)$ **[1+1]**

First find the x- and y-components of the rock’s position and velocity as it leaves the catapult arm and becomes a projectile. See the accompanying figure. Taking the point marked X as the origin

<figure className="problem-figure problem-figure--sized" style={{'--fig-w': '39.8%', '--fig-max': '255px'}}>
<img src="https://pub-43290baaaff14857b5dd59610ea438c7.r2.dev/problems/usapho-2000-i-quarterfnal/p1-sol-fig1-astra-r6-20260925.png" alt="Catapult launch geometry, velocity components, angles and coordinates." width="797" height="773" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
</figure>


$x_0=R(1-\cos(\pi/3))=(4.00\ m)(1-0.5)=2.00\ m$ **[1]**

$y_0=R\sin(\pi/3)=(4.00\ m)(0.866)=3.46\ m$ **[1]**

$v_{0x}=v_0\cos(\pi/6)=(10.0\ m/s)(0.866)=8.66\ m/s$ **[1]**

$v_{0y}=v_0\sin(\pi/6)=(10.0\ m/s)(0.500)=5.00\ m/s$ **[1]**

The equations for the x and y position of the rock are:

$x=x_0+v_{0x}t$ **[2]**

$y=y_0+v_{0y}t+(\frac12)a_yt^2$ **[2]**

with $a_y=-9.8\ m/s^2$ and $y=0$ for the vertical position of the rocks landing point. **[1+1]**

Substituting $y=0$ into the y-equation,

$0=(\frac12)a_yt^2+v_{0y}t+y_0$ **[1]**

solving the quadratic equation for t and retaining only the positive t solution

$$
t=\frac{-v_{0y}\pm\sqrt{v_{0y}^2-4(\frac12)a_yy_0}}{2(\frac12)a_y}=\frac{-5.00\ m/s\pm\sqrt{(5.00\ m/s)^2-2(-9.8\ m/s^2)(3.46\ m)}}{-9.8\ m/s^2}=1.49\ s
$$

**[2]**

$x=x_0+v_{0x}t=2.00\ m+(8.66\ m/s)(1.49\ s)=15\ m$ **[2]**

**b.** The angular velocity of the rock at the point of release is:

$\omega=\frac{v_T}{R}=\frac{10.0\ m/s}{4.00\ m}=2.50\ rad/s$ **[2]**

Using $\omega^2=\omega_0^2+2\alpha\theta$ **[2]**

with: $\omega_0=0$, $\theta=(\pi/3)\ rad$ **[1+1]**

$$
\alpha=\frac{\omega^2-\omega_0^2}{2\theta}=\frac{(2.5\ rad/s)^2-0}{2(\pi/3)}=2.98\ rad/s^2
$$

**[2]**


</Spoiler>
