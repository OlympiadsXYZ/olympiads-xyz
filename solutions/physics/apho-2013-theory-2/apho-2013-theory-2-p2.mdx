*14th Asian Physics Olympiad 2013, Bogor, Indonesia — Theory Question 2: Relativistic Correction on GPS Satellite · 13 т.*

## Условие

Global Positioning System (GPS) is a navigation technology which uses signal from satellites to determine the position of an object (for example an airplane). However, due to the satellites high speed movement in orbit, there should be a special relativistic correction, and due to their high altitude, there should be a general relativistic correction. Both corrections seem to be small but are very important for precise measurement of position. We will explore both corrections in this problem.

First we will investigate the special relativistic effect on an accelerated particle. We consider two types of frame, the first one is the **rest frame** (called $S$ or Earth’s frame), where the particle is at rest initially. The other is the **proper frame** (called $S'$), a frame that instantaneously moves together with the accelerated particle. Note that this is not an accelerated frame, it is a constant velocity frame that at a particular moment has the same velocity with the accelerated particle. At that short moment, the time rate experienced by the particle is the **same** as the proper frame’s time rate. Of course this proper frame is only good for an infinitesimally short time, and then we need to define a new proper frame afterward. At the beginning we synchronize the particle’s clock with the clock in the rest frame by setting them to zero, $t = \tau = 0$ ($t$ is the time in the rest frame, and $\tau$ is the time shown by particle’s clock).

By applying **equivalence principle**, we can obtain general relativistic effects from special relativistic results which does not involve complicated metric tensor calculations. By combining the special and general relativistic effects, we can calculate the corrections needed for a GPS (global positioning system) satellite to provide accurate positioning.

Some mathematics formulas that might be useful

- $\sinh x = \frac{e^{x} - e^{-x}}{2}$
- $\cosh x = \frac{e^{x} + e^{-x}}{2}$
- $\tanh x = \frac{\sinh x}{\cosh x}$
- $1 + \sinh^2 x = \cosh^2 x$
- $\sinh(x - y) = \sinh x \cosh y - \cosh x \sinh y$
- $\int \frac{dx}{(1-x^2)^{\frac{3}{2}}} = \frac{x}{\sqrt{1-x^2}} + C$
- $\int \frac{dx}{1-x^2} = \ln \sqrt{\frac{1+x}{1-x}} + C$

**Part C. Minkowski Diagram (1.0 points)**

In many occasion, it is very useful to illustrate relativistic events using a diagram, called as Minkowski Diagram. To make the diagram, we just need to use Lorentz transformation between the rest frame S and the moving frame S’ that move with velocity $v = \beta c$ with respect to the rest frame.

$$
x = \gamma(x' + \beta ct'),
$$
$$
ct = \gamma(ct' + \beta x'),
$$
$$
x' = \gamma(x - \beta ct),
$$
$$
ct' = \gamma(ct - \beta x).
$$
$$
\gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}}}
$$

Let’s choose $x$ and $ct$ as the orthogonal axes. A point ($x’$, $ct’$) = (1,0) in the moving frame S’ has a coordinate ($x$, $ct$) = $(\gamma, \gamma\beta)$ in the rest frame S. The line connecting this point and the origin defines the $x’$ axis. Another point ($x’$, $ct’$) = (0,1) in the moving frame S’ has a coordinate ($x$, $ct$) = $(\gamma\beta, \gamma)$ in the rest frame S. The line connecting this point and the origin defines the $ct’$ axis. The angle between the $x$ and x’ axis is $\theta$, where $\tan\theta = \beta$. A unit length in the moving frame S’ is equal to $\gamma\sqrt{1+\beta^2} = \sqrt{\frac{1+\beta^2}{1-\beta^2}}$ in the rest frame S.

To get a better understanding of Minkowski diagram, let us take a look at this example. Consider a stick of proper length $L$ in a moving frame S’. We would like to find the length of the stick in the rest frame S. Consider the figure below.

The stick is represented by the segment AC. The length AC is equal to $\sqrt{\frac{1+\beta^2}{1-\beta^2}}L$ in the S frame. The stick length in the S frame is represented by the line AB.

$$
\begin{aligned} \mathrm{AB} &= \mathrm{AD} - \mathrm{BD} \\ &= AC\cos\theta - AC\sin\theta\tan\theta \\ &= L\sqrt{1-\beta^2} \end{aligned}
$$

## Решение

<Spoiler title="Покажи официалното решение">

**Part A. Single accelerated particle**

1. The equation of motion is given by

$$
\begin{aligned} F &= \frac{d}{dt}(\gamma m v) && (1) \\ &= \frac{mc\dot{\beta}}{(1-\beta^2)^{\frac{3}{2}}} \\ F &= \gamma^3 m a, && (2) \end{aligned}
$$

where $\gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\beta^2}}$ and $\beta = \frac{v}{c}$. So the acceleration is given by

$$
a = \frac{F}{\gamma^3 m}. \qquad (3)
$$

2. Eq.(3) can be rewritten as

$$
\begin{aligned} c\frac{d\beta}{dt} &= \frac{F}{\gamma^3 m} \\ \int_0^{\beta} \frac{d\beta}{(1-\beta^2)^{\frac{3}{2}}} &= \frac{F}{mc}\int_0^t dt \\ \frac{\beta}{\sqrt{1-\beta^2}} &= \frac{Ft}{mc} && (4) \\ \beta &= \frac{\frac{Ft}{mc}}{\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{Ft}{mc}\right)^2}}. && (5) \end{aligned}
$$

3. Using Eq.(5), we get

$$
\begin{aligned} \int_0^x dx &= \int_0^t \frac{F t\, dt}{m\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{Ft}{mc}\right)^2}} \\ x &= \frac{mc^2}{F}\left(\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{Ft}{mc}\right)^2} - 1\right). && (6) \end{aligned}
$$

4. Consider the following systems, a frame S’ is moving with respect to another frame S, with velocity $u$ in the $x$ direction. If a particle is moving in the S’ frame with velocity $v'$ also in $x$ direction, then the particle velocity in the S frame is given by

$$
v = \frac{u + v'}{1 + \frac{uv'}{c^2}}. \qquad (7)
$$

If the particles velocity changes with respect to the S’ frame, then the velocity in the S frame is also change according to

$$
\begin{aligned} dv &= \frac{dv'}{1+\frac{uv'}{c^2}} - \frac{u+v'}{\left(1+\frac{uv'}{c^2}\right)^2}\frac{u\,dv'}{c^2} \\ dv &= \frac{1}{\gamma^2}\frac{dv'}{\left(1+\frac{uv'}{c^2}\right)^2}. && (8) \end{aligned}
$$

The time in the S’ frame is $t'$, so the time in the S frame is given by

$$
t = \gamma\left(t' + \frac{ux'}{c^2}\right), \qquad (9)
$$

so the time change in the S’ frame will give a time change in the S frame as follow

$$
dt = \gamma dt'\left(1 + \frac{uv'}{c^2}\right). \qquad (10)
$$

The acceleration in the S frame is given by

$$
a = \frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{a'}{\gamma^3}\frac{1}{\left(1+\frac{uv'}{c^2}\right)^3}. \qquad (11)
$$

If the S’ frame is the proper frame, then by definition the velocity $v' = 0$. Substitute this to the last equation, we get

$$
a = \frac{a'}{\gamma^3}. \qquad (12)
$$

Combining Eq.(3) and Eq.(12), we get

$$
a' = \frac{F}{m} \equiv g. \qquad (13)
$$

5. Eq.(3) can also be rewritten as

$$
\begin{aligned} c\frac{d\beta}{\gamma d\tau} &= \frac{g}{\gamma^3} && (14) \\ \int_0^{\beta}\frac{d\beta}{1-\beta^2} &= \frac{g}{c}\int_0^{\tau} d\tau \\ \ln\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\beta^2}} + \frac{\beta}{\sqrt{1-\beta^2}}\right) &= \frac{g\tau}{c} && (15) \\ \sqrt{\frac{1+\beta}{1-\beta}} &= e^{\frac{g\tau}{c}} \\ \beta\left(e^{\frac{g\tau}{c}} + e^{-\frac{g\tau}{c}}\right) &= e^{\frac{g\tau}{c}} - e^{-\frac{g\tau}{c}} \\ \beta &= \tanh\frac{g\tau}{c}. && (16) \end{aligned}
$$

6. The time dilation relation is

$$
dt = \gamma d\tau. \qquad (17)
$$

From eq.(16), we have

$$
\gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\beta^2}} = \cosh\frac{g\tau}{c}. \qquad (18)
$$

Combining this equations, we get

$$
\begin{aligned} \int_0^t dt &= \int_0^{\tau} d\tau \cosh\frac{g\tau}{c} \\ t &= \frac{c}{g}\sinh\frac{g\tau}{c}. && (19) \end{aligned}
$$

**Part B. Flight Time**

1. When the clock in the origin time is equal to $t_0$, it emits a signal that contain the information of its time. This signal will arrive at the particle at time $t$, while the particle position is at $x(t)$. We have

$$
\begin{aligned} c(t - t_0) &= x(t) && (20) \\ t - t_0 &= \frac{c}{g}\left(\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{gt}{c}\right)^2} - 1\right) \\ t &= \frac{t_0}{2}\frac{2 - \frac{gt_0}{c}}{1 - \frac{gt_0}{c}}. && (21) \end{aligned}
$$

When the information arrive at the particle, the particle’s clock has a reading according to eq.(19). So we get

$$
\begin{aligned} \frac{c}{g}\sinh\frac{g\tau}{c} &= \frac{t_0}{2}\frac{2 - \frac{gt_0}{c}}{1 - \frac{gt_0}{c}} \\ 0 &= \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{gt_0}{c}\right)^2 - \frac{gt_0}{c}\left(1 + \sinh\frac{g\tau}{c}\right) + \sinh\frac{g\tau}{c} \\ \frac{gt_0}{c} &= 1 + \sinh\frac{g\tau}{c} \pm \cosh\frac{g\tau}{c}. && (22) \end{aligned}
$$

Using initial condition $t = 0$ when $\tau = 0$, we choose the negative sign

$$
\begin{aligned} \frac{gt_0}{c} &= 1 + \sinh\frac{g\tau}{c} - \cosh\frac{g\tau}{c} \\ t_0 &= \frac{c}{g}\left(1 - e^{-\frac{g\tau}{c}}\right). && (23) \end{aligned}
$$

As $\tau \to \infty$, $t_0 = \frac{c}{g}$. So the clock reading will freeze at this value.

2. When the particles clock has a reading $\tau_0$, its position is given by eq.(6), and the time $t_0$ is given by eq.(19). Combining this two equation, we get

$$
x = \frac{c^2}{g}\left(\sqrt{1 + \sinh^2\frac{g\tau_0}{c}} - 1\right). \qquad (24)
$$

The particle’s clock reading is then sent to the observer at the origin. The total time needed for the information to arrive is given by

$$
\begin{aligned} t &= \frac{c}{g}\sinh\frac{g\tau_0}{c} + \frac{x}{c} && (25) \\ &= \frac{c}{g}\left(\sinh\frac{g\tau_0}{c} + \cosh\frac{g\tau_0}{c} - 1\right) \\ t &= \frac{c}{g}\left(e^{\frac{g\tau_0}{c}} - 1\right) && (26) \\ \tau_0 &= \frac{c}{g}\ln\left(\frac{gt}{c} + 1\right). && (27) \end{aligned}
$$

The time will not freeze.

The length AB is equal to $\sqrt{\frac{1-\beta^2}{1+\beta^2}}L$ in the S’ frame.

**Part D. Two Accelerated Particles**

1. $\tau_B = \tau_A$.

2. From the diagram, we have

$$
\tan\theta = \beta = \frac{ct_2 - ct_1}{x_2 - x_1}. \qquad (29)
$$

Using eq.(6), and eq.(19) along with the initial condition, we get

$$
x_1 = \frac{c^2}{g}\left(\cosh\frac{g\tau_1}{c} - 1\right), \qquad (30)
$$

$$
x_2 = \frac{c^2}{g}\left(\cosh\frac{g\tau_2}{c} - 1\right) + L. \qquad (31)
$$

Using eq.(16), eq.(19), eq.(30) and eq.(31), we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned} \tanh\frac{g\tau_1}{c} &= \frac{c\left(\frac{c}{g}\sinh\frac{g\tau_2}{c} - \frac{c}{g}\sinh\frac{g\tau_1}{c}\right)}{L + \frac{c^2}{g}\left(\cosh\frac{g\tau_2}{c} - 1\right) - \frac{c^2}{g}\left(\cosh\frac{g\tau_1}{c} - 1\right)} \\ &= \frac{\sinh\frac{g\tau_2}{c} - \sinh\frac{g\tau_1}{c}}{\frac{gL}{c^2} + \cosh\frac{g\tau_2}{c} - \cosh\frac{g\tau_1}{c}} \\ \frac{gL}{c^2}\sinh\frac{g\tau_1}{c} &= \sinh\frac{g\tau_2}{c}\cosh\frac{g\tau_1}{c} - \cosh\frac{g\tau_2}{c}\sinh\frac{g\tau_1}{c} \\ \frac{gL}{c^2}\sinh\frac{g\tau_1}{c} &= \sinh\frac{g}{c}(\tau_2 - \tau_1). && (32) \end{aligned}
$$

So $C_1 = \frac{gL}{c^2}$.

3. From the length contraction, we have

$$
\begin{aligned} L' &= \frac{x_2 - x_1}{\gamma_1} && (33) \\ \frac{dL'}{d\tau_1} &= \left(\frac{dx_2}{d\tau_2}\frac{d\tau_2}{d\tau_1} - \frac{dx_1}{d\tau_1}\right)\frac{1}{\gamma_1} - \frac{x_2 - x_1}{\gamma_1^2}\frac{d\gamma_1}{d\tau_1}. && (34) \end{aligned}
$$

Take derivative of eq.(30), eq.(31) and eq.(32), we get

$$
\frac{dx_1}{d\tau_1} = c\sinh\frac{g\tau_1}{c}, \qquad (35)
$$

$$
\frac{dx_2}{d\tau_2} = c\sinh\frac{g\tau_2}{c}, \qquad (36)
$$

$$
\frac{gL}{c^2}\cosh\frac{g\tau_1}{c} = \cosh\frac{g}{c}(\tau_2 - \tau_1)\left(\frac{d\tau_2}{d\tau_1} - 1\right). \qquad (37)
$$

The last equation can be rearrange to get

$$
\frac{d\tau_2}{d\tau_1} = \frac{\frac{gL}{c^2}\cosh\frac{g\tau_1}{c}}{\cosh\frac{g}{c}(\tau_2 - \tau_1)} + 1. \qquad (38)
$$

From eq.(29), we have

$$
x_2 - x_1 = \frac{c(t_2 - t_1)}{\beta_1} = \frac{c}{\tanh\frac{g\tau_1}{c}}\left(\frac{c}{g}\sinh\frac{g\tau_2}{c} - \frac{c}{g}\sinh\frac{g\tau_1}{c}\right). \qquad (39)
$$

Combining all these equations, we get

$$
\begin{aligned} \frac{dL_1}{d\tau_1} &= \left(c\sinh\frac{g\tau_2}{c}\frac{\frac{gL}{c^2}\cosh\frac{g\tau_1}{c}}{\cosh\frac{g}{c}(\tau_2 - \tau_1)} + c\sinh\frac{g\tau_2}{c} - c\sinh\frac{g\tau_1}{c}\right)\frac{1}{\cosh\frac{g\tau_1}{c}} \\ &\quad - \frac{c^2}{g}\left(\sinh\frac{g\tau_2}{c} - \sinh\frac{g\tau_1}{c}\right)\frac{1}{\tanh\frac{g\tau_1}{c}}\frac{1}{\cosh^2\frac{g\tau_1}{c}}\frac{g}{c}\sinh\frac{g\tau_1}{c} \\ \frac{dL_1}{d\tau_1} &= \frac{gL}{c}\frac{\sinh\frac{g\tau_2}{c}}{\cosh\frac{g}{c}(\tau_2 - \tau_1)}. && (40) \end{aligned}
$$

So $C_2 = \frac{gL}{c}$.

**Part E. Uniformly Accelerated Frame**

1. Distance from a certain point $x_p$ according to the particle’s frame is

$$
\begin{aligned} L' &= \frac{x - x_p}{\gamma} && (41) \\ L' &= \frac{\frac{c^2}{g_1}\left(\cosh\frac{g_1\tau}{c} - 1\right) - x_p}{\cosh\frac{g_1\tau}{c}} \\ L' &= \frac{c^2}{g_1} - \frac{\frac{c^2}{g_1} + x_p}{\cosh\frac{g_1\tau}{c}}. && (42) \end{aligned}
$$

For $L'$ equal constant, we need $x_p = -\frac{c^2}{g_1}$.

2. **First method:** If the distance in the S’ frame is constant $= L$, then in the S frame the length is

$$
L_s = L\sqrt{\frac{1+\beta^2}{1-\beta^2}}. \qquad (43)
$$

So the position of the second particle is

$$
\begin{aligned} x_2 &= x_1 + L_s\cos\theta && (44) \\ &= \frac{c^2}{g_1}\left(\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{g_1 t_1}{c}\right)^2} - 1\right) + L\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{g_1 t_1}{c}\right)} \\ x_2 &= \left(\frac{c^2}{g_1} + L\right)\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{g_1 t_1}{c}\right)^2} - \frac{c^2}{g_1}. && (45) \end{aligned}
$$

The time of the second particle is

$$
\begin{aligned} ct_2 &= ct_1 + L_s\sin\theta && (46) \\ &= ct_1 + L\sqrt{\frac{1+\beta^2}{1-\beta^2}}\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{1+\beta^2}} \\ ct_2 &= t_1\left(c + \frac{g_1 L}{c}\right). && (47) \end{aligned}
$$

Substitute eq.(47) to eq.(45) to get

$$
\begin{aligned} x_2 &= \left(\frac{c^2}{g_1} + L\right)\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{g_1}{c}\frac{t_2}{1+\frac{g_1 L}{c^2}}\right)^2} - \frac{c^2}{g_1} \\ x_2 &= \left(\frac{c^2}{g_1} + L\right)\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{g_1}{1+\frac{g_1 L}{c^2}}\frac{t_2}{c}\right)^2} - \frac{c^2}{g_1}. && (48) \end{aligned}
$$

From the last equation, we can identify

$$
g_2 \equiv \frac{g_1}{1 + \frac{g_1 L}{c^2}}. \qquad (49)
$$

As for confirmation, we can substitute this relation to the second particle position to get

$$
x_2 = \frac{c^2}{g_2}\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{g_2 t_2}{c}\right)^2} - \frac{c^2}{g_1}. \qquad (50)
$$

**Second method:** In this method, we will choose $g_2$ such that the special point like the one describe in the question 1 is exactly the same as the similar point for the proper acceleration $g_1$.

For first particle, we have $x_{p1}g_1 = c^2$

For second particle, we have $(L + x_{p1})g_2 = c^2$

Combining this two equations, we get

$$
\begin{aligned} g_2 &= \frac{c^2}{L + \frac{c^2}{g_1}} \\ g_2 &= \frac{g_1}{1 + \frac{g_1 L}{c^2}}. && (51) \end{aligned}
$$

3. The relation between the time in the two particles is given by eq.(47)

$$
\begin{aligned} t_2 &= t_1\left(1 + \frac{g_1 L}{c^2}\right) \\ \frac{c^2}{g_2}\sinh\frac{g_2\tau_2}{c} &= \frac{c^2}{g_1}\sinh\frac{g_1\tau_1}{c}\left(1 + \frac{g_1 L}{c^2}\right) \\ \sinh\frac{g_2\tau_2}{c} &= \sinh\frac{g_1\tau_1}{c} \\ g_2\tau_2 &= g_1\tau_1 && (52) \\ \frac{d\tau_2}{d\tau_1} &= \frac{g_1}{g_2} = 1 + \frac{g_1 L}{c^2}. && (53) \end{aligned}
$$

**Part F. Correction for GPS**

1. From Newtons Law

$$
\begin{aligned} \frac{GMm}{r^2} &= m\omega^2 r && (54) \\ r &= \left(\frac{gR^2T^2}{4\pi^2}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}} && (55) \\ r &= 2.66 \times 10^7\mathrm{m}. \end{aligned}
$$

The velocity is given by

$$
\begin{aligned} v = \omega r &= \left(\frac{2\pi g R^2}{T}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}} && (56) \\ &= 3.87 \times 10^3\mathrm{m/s}. \end{aligned}
$$

2. The general relativity effect is

$$
\begin{aligned} \frac{d\tau_g}{dt} &= 1 + \frac{\Delta U}{mc^2} && (57) \\ \frac{d\tau_g}{dt} &= 1 + \frac{gR^2}{c^2}\frac{R - r}{Rr}. && (58) \end{aligned}
$$

After one day, the difference is

$$
\begin{aligned} \Delta\tau_g &= \frac{gR^2}{c^2}\frac{R - r}{Rr}\Delta T && (59) \\ &= 4.55 \times 10^{-5}\mathrm{s}. \end{aligned}
$$

The special relativity effect is

$$
\begin{aligned} \frac{d\tau_s}{dt} &= \sqrt{1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}} && (60) \\ &= \sqrt{1 - \left(\left(\frac{2\pi g R^2}{T}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}\right)\frac{1}{c^2}} \\ &\approx 1 - \frac{1}{2}\left(\left(\frac{2\pi g R^2}{T}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}\right)\frac{1}{c^2}. && (61) \end{aligned}
$$

After one day, the difference is

$$
\begin{aligned} \Delta\tau_s &= -\frac{1}{2}\left(\left(\frac{2\pi g R^2}{T}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}\right)\frac{1}{c^2}\Delta T && (62) \\ &= -7.18 \times 10^{-6}\mathrm{s}. \end{aligned}
$$

The satellite’s clock is faster with total $\Delta\tau = \Delta\tau_g + \Delta\tau_s = 3.83 \times 10^{-5}\mathrm{s}$.

3. $\Delta L = c\Delta\tau = 1.15 \times 10^4\mathrm{m} = 11.5\mathrm{km}$.
