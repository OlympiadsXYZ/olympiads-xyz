## Условие

A thin-walled cylinder of mass $M$ and rough inner surface of radius $R$ can rotate about its fixed central horizontal axis OZ. The Z-axis is perpendicular to and out of the page. Another smaller uniform solid cylinder of mass $m$ and radius $r$ rolls without slipping (except for question 1.8) on the inner surface of $M$ about its own central axis which is parallel to OZ.

<figure className="problem-figure problem-figure--sized" style={{'--fig-w': '63.9%', '--fig-max': '409px'}}>
<img src="https://pub-43290baaaff14857b5dd59610ea438c7.r2.dev/problems/apho-2009-theory-th/p1-fig1.png" alt="Cross-section of the large cylinder M of radius R centred at O with X and Y axes; a small shaded cylinder m rolls on the inner surface at angle θ from the negative Y-axis; curved arrows show rotation of m and of M; a downward arrow labelled g = acceleration due to gravity." width="1278" height="818" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
</figure>

**1.1)** The rotation of $M$ is to be started from rest at the instant $t = 0$ when $m$ is resting at the lowest point. At a later time $t$ the angular position of the centre of mass of $m$ is $\theta$ and by then $M$ has turned through an angle $\phi$ radians. How many radians (designated $\psi$) would have mass $m$ turned through about its central axis relative to a fixed line (for example, the negative Y-axis)? Give your answer in terms of $\theta, \phi, R$ and $r$. **[0,8 т.]**

**1.2)** What is the angular acceleration of $m$, $\dfrac{d^2}{dt^2}\psi$, about its own axis through its centre of mass? Give your answer in terms of $R, r,$ and derivatives of $\theta$ and $\phi$. **[0,2 т.]**

**1.3)** Derive an equation for the angular acceleration of the centre of mass of $m$, $\dfrac{d^2}{dt^2}\theta$, in terms of $m, g, R, r, \theta, \dfrac{d^2}{dt^2}\phi$, and the moment of inertia $I_{\mathrm{CM}}$ of $m$ about its central axis. **[1,8 т.]**

**1.4)** What is the period of small amplitude oscillation of $m$ when $M$ is constrained to rotate at a constant angular velocity? Give your answer only in terms of $R, r,$ and $g$. **[1,3 т.]**

**1.5)** What is the value of $\theta$ for the equilibrium position of $m$ in question 1.4? **[0,2 т.]**

**1.6)** What is the equilibrium position of $m$ when $M$ is rotating with a constant angular acceleration $\alpha$? Give your answer in terms of $R, g,$ and $\alpha$. **[0,7 т.]**

**1.7)** Now $M$ is allowed to rotate (oscillate) freely, without constraint, about its central axis OZ while $m$ is executing a small-amplitude oscillation by pure rolling on the inner surface of $M$. Find the period of this oscillation. **[2,5 т.]**

**1.8)** Consider the situation in which $M$ is rotating steadily at an angular velocity $\Omega$ and $m$ is rotating (rolling) about its stationary centre of mass, at the equilibrium position found in question 1.5. $M$ is then brought abruptly to a halt. What must be the lowest value of $\Omega$ such that $m$ will roll up and reach the highest point of the cylindrical surface of $M$? The coefficient of friction between $m$ and $M$ is assumed to be sufficiently high that $m$ begins to roll without slipping soon after a short skidding right after $M$ is stopped. **[2,5 т.]**

### SOLUTION: Rolling Cylinders — archive original

<figure className="problem-figure problem-figure--sized" style={{'--fig-w': '48.1%', '--fig-max': '308px'}}>
<img src="https://pub-43290baaaff14857b5dd59610ea438c7.r2.dev/problems/apho-2009-theory-th/p1-sol-fig1.png" alt="Initial Rolling Cylinders key diagram: Y/X axes, M/R/O, m/C/P, theta/phi/psi and signed rotation arrows, downward g." width="964" height="814" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
</figure>


**1.1)** The point P which is fixed on the surface of $M$ was at the position directly below O at time $t=0$. Hence $m$ must have rolled through an angle $\frac{\phi R-\theta R}{r}$ radians relative to surface of $M$ in time $t$ during which the line OC has also turned anti-clockwise through an angle $\theta$. Therefore the total angular displacement of $m$ about its centre of mass relative to any fixed reference line in time $t$ is

$$
\psi=\frac{\phi R-\theta R}{r}+\theta=\frac{R}{r}\phi-\left(\frac{R-r}{r}\right)\theta.\qquad\text{(i)}
$$

**1.2)** By differentiating the equation (i) twice with respect to time, we get

$$
\frac{d^2\psi}{dt^2}=\frac{R}{r}\frac{d^2\phi}{dt^2}-\left(\frac{R-r}{r}\right)\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2}.\qquad\text{(ii)}
$$

**1.3)**

<figure className="problem-figure problem-figure--sized" style={{'--fig-w': '47.1%', '--fig-max': '302px'}}>
<img src="https://pub-43290baaaff14857b5dd59610ea438c7.r2.dev/problems/apho-2009-theory-th/p1-sol-fig2.png" alt="Rolling Cylinders forces for1.3: complete axes, M/R/O, m/C, theta, f/N/mg arrows and rotation directions." width="943" height="759" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
</figure>


The equations of motion of centre of mass of $m$ are:

$$
m(R-r)\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2}=f-mg\sin\theta,\qquad\text{(iii)}
$$

$$
m\left(\frac{d\theta}{dt}\right)^2(R-r)=N-mg\cos\theta.\qquad\text{(iv)}
$$

The equation for the rotation of $m$ about its centre of mass is:

$$
I_{\mathrm{CM}}\frac{d^2\psi}{dt^2}=I_{\mathrm{CM}}\left[\frac{R}{r}\frac{d^2\phi}{dt^2}-\left(\frac{R-r}{r}\right)\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2}\right]=fr,\qquad\text{(v)}
$$

where $I_{\mathrm{CM}}=\frac12mr^2$. Equations (iii) and (v) yield:

$$
\left(m+\frac{I_{\mathrm{CM}}}{r^2}\right)(R-r)\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2}=-mg\sin\theta+\frac{I_{\mathrm{CM}}R}{r^2}\frac{d^2\phi}{dt^2}.\qquad\text{(vi)}
$$

**1.4)** Here, $\frac{d^2\phi}{dt^2}=0$, $\sin\theta\approx\theta$ and also $I_{\mathrm{CM}}=\frac12mr^2$, the equation (vi) is reduced to:

$$
\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2}=-\frac{2g}{3(R-r)}\theta.\qquad\text{(vii)}
$$

This gives a period

$$
T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{3(R-r)}{2g}}.\qquad\text{(viii)}
$$

**1.5)** The equilibrium position of $m$ in question 1.4) is $\theta=0$.

**1.6)** But the equilibrium position for the case where $M$ is rotating with a constant angular acceleration $\alpha$ is by considering the equation (vi), namely,

$$
\frac32(R-r)\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2}=-g\sin\theta+\frac{R}{2}\alpha.\qquad\text{(ix)}
$$

Let $\theta_{\mathrm{eq}}$ be the equilibrium position; this implies that $m$ remains stationary at this position if it does not oscillate. Hence $\frac{d^2\theta_{\mathrm{eq}}}{dt^2}=0$, and

$$
\theta_{\mathrm{eq}}=\arcsin\left(\frac{R\alpha}{2g}\right).\qquad\text{(x)}
$$

**1.7)**

<figure className="problem-figure problem-figure--sized" style={{'--fig-w': '67.7%', '--fig-max': '434px'}}>
<img src="https://pub-43290baaaff14857b5dd59610ea438c7.r2.dev/problems/apho-2009-theory-th/p1-sol-fig3.png" alt="Original1.7 showing forces acting on m diagram, with reversed phi/psi directions and complete gravity/friction/normal labels." width="1355" height="759" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
</figure>


From the equation (i) we get, after changing the directions of $\psi$ and $\phi$,

$$
\frac{d\psi}{dt}=\frac{R}{r}\frac{d\phi}{dt}+\left(\frac{R-r}{r}\right)\frac{d\theta}{dt}.\qquad\text{(xi)}
$$

The equations of motion of $m$ and $M$ are:

$$
\frac12mr^2\frac{d^2\psi}{dt^2}=-fr,\qquad\text{(xii)}
$$

$$
MR^2\frac{d^2\phi}{dt^2}=+fR.\qquad\text{(xiii)}
$$

#### Method 1: (Angular Momentum)

The effect of gravity on the system as a whole is to change its angular momentum:

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\left[MR^2\frac{d\phi}{dt}+\frac12mr^2\frac{d\psi}{dt}-m(R-r)^2\frac{d\theta}{dt}\right]=+mg(R-r)\sin\theta.\qquad\text{(xiv.1)}
$$

Hence

$$
\frac{d^2\phi}{dt^2}=-\frac{m(R-r)}{(2M+m)R}\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2},\qquad\text{(xv.1)}
$$

and

$$
\left(MR+\frac12mr\right)R\frac{d^2\phi}{dt^2}-m(R-r)\left(R-\frac32r\right)\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2}=mg(R-r)\sin\theta.\qquad\text{(xvi.1)}
$$

Combining the last two equations:

$$
\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2}=-\frac{g}{R-r}\frac{2M+m}{3M+m}\sin\theta.\qquad\text{(xvii.1)}
$$

For a small-amplitude oscillation we put $\sin\theta\approx\theta$ and equation (xvii) is reduced to:

$$
\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2}=-\frac{g}{R-r}\frac{2M+m}{3M+m}\theta.\qquad\text{(xviii.1)}
$$

The period of this oscillation is, therefore,

$$
T=2\pi\sqrt{\left(\frac{R-r}{g}\right)\left(\frac{3M+m}{2M+m}\right)}.
$$

#### Method 2: (Newton’s law)

From Newton’s law: $mg\sin\theta-f=ma$,

$$
mg\sin\theta-f=-m(R-r)\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2}.\qquad\text{(xiv.2)}
$$

From equation (xiii): $f=MR\frac{d^2\phi}{dt^2}$. By substituting this into equation (xiv.2) we have

$$
mg\sin\theta=MR\frac{d^2\phi}{dt^2}-m(R-r)\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2}.\qquad\text{(xv.2)}
$$

From equations (xi) (xii) and (xiii), we then have

$$
\frac{d^2\phi}{dt^2}=-\frac{m}{2M+m}\left(\frac{R-r}{R}\right)\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2}.\qquad\text{(xvi.2)}
$$

Then (xv.2) becomes

$$
mg\sin\theta=-\frac{Mm}{2M+m}(R-r)\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2}-m(R-r)\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2},
$$

$$
\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2}=-\frac{g}{R-r}\frac{2M+m}{3M+m}\sin\theta.\qquad\text{(xvii.2)}
$$

For a small-amplitude oscillation we put $\sin\theta\approx\theta$ and equation (xvii) is reduced to:

$$
\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2}=-\frac{g}{R-r}\frac{2M+m}{3M+m}\theta.\qquad\text{(xviii.2)}
$$

The period of this oscillation is, therefore,

$$
T=2\pi\sqrt{\left(\frac{R-r}{g}\right)\left(\frac{3M+m}{2M+m}\right)}.
$$

#### Method 3: (Conservation of Energy)

The total mechanical energy of the system is given by

$$
E=\frac12MR^2\left(\frac{d\phi}{dt}\right)^2+\frac12\left(\frac12mr^2\right)\left(\frac{d\psi}{dt}\right)^2+\frac12m\left(\frac{d\theta}{dt}\right)^2(R-r)^2+mg(R-r)(1-\cos\theta).\qquad\text{(xiv.3)}
$$

We now use conservation of mechanical energy,

$$
\frac{dE}{dt}=MR^2\left(\frac{d\phi}{dt}\right)\left(\frac{d^2\phi}{dt^2}\right)+\left(\frac12mr^2\right)\left(\frac{d\psi}{dt}\right)\left(\frac{d^2\psi}{dt^2}\right)+m\left(\frac{d\theta}{dt}\right)\left(\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2}\right)(R-r)^2+mg(R-r)\left(\frac{d\theta}{dt}\right)\sin\theta=0.\qquad\text{(xv.3)}
$$

By applying the equations (xi), (xii), and (xiii), we have

$$
\frac{d^2\phi}{dt^2}=-\frac{R-r}{R}\frac{m}{2M+m}\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{d^2\psi}{dt^2}=\frac{R-r}{r}\frac{2M}{2M+m}\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2}.\qquad\text{(xvi.3)}
$$

Without loss of generality, we can integrate both equations and obtain

$$
\frac{d\phi}{dt}=-\frac{R-r}{R}\frac{m}{2M+m}\frac{d\theta}{dt}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{d\psi}{dt}=\frac{R-r}{r}\frac{2M}{2M+m}\frac{d\theta}{dt},\qquad\text{(xvii.3)}
$$

by imposing the condition that all bodies have zero linear and angular velocities at the same particular instant. And by substituting these relations into the equation above from conservation of energy, we have

$$
\left[\frac{Mm}{(2M+m)^2}+\frac{2M^2}{(2M+m)^2}+1\right]\left(\frac{d\theta}{dt}\right)\left(\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2}\right)(R-r)^2=-g(R-r)\left(\frac{d\theta}{dt}\right)\sin\theta.\qquad\text{(xviii.3)}
$$

This equation must hold at all time, so we can divide $\frac{d\theta}{dt}$ on both sides. After some simplifications, we have

$$
\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2}=-\frac{g}{R-r}\frac{2M+m}{3M+m}\sin\theta.\qquad\text{(xix.3)}
$$

For a small-amplitude oscillation we put $\sin\theta\approx\theta$, and the above expression is reduced to:

$$
\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2}=-\frac{g}{R-r}\frac{2M+m}{3M+m}\theta.\qquad\text{(xx.3)}
$$

The period of this oscillation is, therefore,

$$
T=2\pi\sqrt{\left(\frac{R-r}{g}\right)\left(\frac{3M+m}{2M+m}\right)}.
$$

Note that, although it seems like we have more than one degree of freedom, there exists only one mode of oscillations because the coupling to the potential energy is only through the angle $\theta$.

However, we have a freedom to impose any constant angular velocity $\frac{d\phi}{dt}$ and $\frac{d\psi}{dt}$ (and the condition for rolling without slipping), and this will not alter the period of the oscillations. This corresponds to a freedom in choosing different initial conditions of the motion of the system.

**1.8)** When $M$ is made to rotate steadily at an angular velocity $\Omega$ the equation (vi) becomes

$$
\frac32(R-r)\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2}=-g\sin\theta,\qquad\text{(xix)}
$$

which implies that $m$ remains at $\theta=0$ if $m$ does not oscillate. Hence the equation (i) is reduced to $\psi=\frac{R}{r}\phi$ and

$$
\frac{d\psi}{dt}=\frac{R}{r}\frac{d\phi}{dt}=\frac{R}{r}\Omega.\qquad\text{(xx)}
$$

This means that $m$ is rotating at a constant angular velocity $\frac{R}{r}\Omega$ prior to the instant when $M$ is stopped.

After that instant $m$ will accelerate itself by way of frictional impulse. This acceleration process lasts for only a short time due to the high value of frictional coefficient $(\mu)$. To simplify the calculation we will take to lower surface of $M$ to be flat.

<figure className="problem-figure problem-figure--sized" style={{'--fig-w': '59.2%', '--fig-max': '379px'}}>
<img src="https://pub-43290baaaff14857b5dd59610ea438c7.r2.dev/problems/apho-2009-theory-th/p1-sol-fig4-v2.png" alt="Original1.8 skidding forwards diagram: small purple cylinder m, v arrow, omega rotation, downward g and fm=mu mg arrow." width="1184" height="267" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
</figure>


$$
m\frac{dv}{dt}=+f_m,\qquad\text{(xxi)}
$$

$$
I_{\mathrm{CM}}\frac{d\omega}{dt}=-f_mr,\quad I_{\mathrm{CM}}=\frac12mr^2.\qquad\text{(xxii)}
$$

By solving these last two equations for $v(t)$ and $\omega(t)$ with initial conditions $v(0)=0$ and $\omega(0)=\frac{R}{r}\Omega$, and imposing the condition $v'(t)=\omega'(t)r$ for the onset of pure rolling we get

$$
v'=\frac13R\Omega,\quad\omega'=\frac13\frac{R}{r}\Omega.\qquad\text{(xxiii)}
$$

From now on, the cylinder $m$ will roll up the side of the cylindrical wall. And since frictional force does not do work in pure rolling we can use the principle of conservation of energy.

$$
\frac12mv^2+\frac12I_{\mathrm{CM}}\omega^2+2mg(R-r)=\frac12mv'^2+\frac12I_{\mathrm{CM}}\omega'^2.\qquad\text{(xxiv)}
$$

We have also

$$
N=m\frac{v^2}{R-r}-mg,\qquad\text{(xxv)}
$$

$$
N=\left(\frac{m}{R-r}\right)\left(\frac{R\Omega}{3}\right)^2-\frac{11}{3}mg.\qquad\text{(xxvi)}
$$

$m$ will reach the top if $N\ge0$. Hence

$$
\Omega\ge\sqrt{33g\left(\frac{R-r}{R^2}\right)}.\qquad\text{(xxvii)}
$$
