In this problem we consider a small ball bouncing back and forth between two points. In all parts below, the acceleration of gravity is $g$, collisions are perfectly elastic, air resistance is negligible, and the impact points are at the same height. The diagrams are not drawn to scale.

<ProblemSection id="a1-a">

### a.

Consider a ball bouncing between two inclined planes, which each make an angle $\theta < 90^{\circ}$ to the horizontal. The ball has speed $v_0$ at the impact points, which are separated by a distance $D$.

**i.** The ball can bounce back and forth along the same path, as shown. <img className="problem-inline-figure problem-inline-figure--sized" src="https://pub-43290baaaff14857b5dd59610ea438c7.r2.dev/problems/usapho-2023-exam/p1-fig1-v2.png" alt="Original same-path bounce, both theta labels and distance D." width="791" height="370" loading="lazy" decoding="async" style={{'--fig-w': '39.5%', '--fig-max': '253px'}} />

For what values of $\theta$ is this motion possible? For these values, what is $v_0$?

**ii.** The ball can also take one trajectory while traveling to the right, and a separate trajectory when traveling back. Let $\phi \neq 0$ be the angle between the paths at the impact points. <img className="problem-inline-figure problem-inline-figure--sized" src="https://pub-43290baaaff14857b5dd59610ea438c7.r2.dev/problems/usapho-2023-exam/p1-fig2-v2.png" alt="Original two-path bounce, both theta labels and distance D." width="791" height="469" loading="lazy" decoding="async" style={{'--fig-w': '39.5%', '--fig-max': '253px'}} />

For what values of $\theta$ and $\phi$ is this motion possible? For these values, what is $v_0$?

</ProblemSection>

<ProblemSection id="a1-b">

### b.

Now suppose the ball bounces within an hemispherical well of radius of curvature $R$. As in part a.ii, it alternates between two distinct paths, with flight times $t_1$ and $t_2 \neq t_1$. <img className="problem-inline-figure problem-inline-figure--sized" src="https://pub-43290baaaff14857b5dd59610ea438c7.r2.dev/problems/usapho-2023-exam/p1-fig3-v2.png" alt="Original hemispherical well, complete two paths and radius R." width="720" height="430" loading="lazy" decoding="async" style={{'--fig-w': '36%', '--fig-max': '230px'}} />

Find all of the possible values of $R$, in terms of $t_1$ and $t_2$.

</ProblemSection>

<ProblemSection id="a1-c">

### c.

Finally, suppose the well has a sinusoidal shape, described by $y(x) = -L\sin(2x/L)$. The ball takes two distinct paths with flight times $t_1$ and $t_2 \neq t_1$, and the horizontal distance between the impact points is less than $\pi L$. Find all of the possible values of $L$, in terms of $t_1$ and $t_2$.

</ProblemSection>

## Решение

<Spoiler title="Покажи официалното решение">

**a.i.**

<figure className="problem-figure problem-figure--sized" style={{'--fig-w': '39.5%', '--fig-max': '253px'}}>
<img src="https://pub-43290baaaff14857b5dd59610ea438c7.r2.dev/problems/usapho-2023-exam/p1-sol-fig1.png" alt="Official key same-path bounce in blue." width="791" height="367" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
</figure>


 To go back and forth on the same path, the ball must impact the plane normally, which means that $\theta$ is the angle of its velocity to the vertical. The range of the ball is
$$
D = \frac{v_0^2\sin\big(2(90^{\circ}-\theta)\big)}{g} = \frac{v_0^2\sin 2\theta}{g}
$$
which implies
$$
v_0 = \sqrt{\frac{gD}{\sin 2\theta}}.
$$
Evidently, the motion is possible for any $\theta$.

**a.ii.**

<figure className="problem-figure problem-figure--sized" style={{'--fig-w': '39.5%', '--fig-max': '253px'}}>
<img src="https://pub-43290baaaff14857b5dd59610ea438c7.r2.dev/problems/usapho-2023-exam/p1-sol-fig2.png" alt="Official key two distinct paths in red and blue." width="791" height="443" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
</figure>


 The two paths have the same initial speed and the same range, which means the initial angles of the velocity to the horizontal must be $\pi/4 \pm \phi/2$. Since the initial and final angles to the normal are equal in the collision, the angle of the planes to the horizontal must be $\theta = 45^{\circ}$. Given this, $\phi$ can take any value in the range $0<\phi<90^{\circ}$.

Considering the range yields
$$
D = \frac{v_0^2\sin\big(2(45^{\circ}+\phi/2)\big)}{g} = \frac{v_0^2\cos\phi}{g}
$$
from which we find
$$
v_0 = \sqrt{\frac{gD}{\cos\phi}}.
$$
As expected, this reduces to the answer above as $\phi \to 0$, and becomes infinite as $\phi \to 90^{\circ}$.

**b.**

<figure className="problem-figure problem-figure--sized" style={{'--fig-w': '36%', '--fig-max': '230px'}}>
<img src="https://pub-43290baaaff14857b5dd59610ea438c7.r2.dev/problems/usapho-2023-exam/p1-sol-fig3-v2.png" alt="Official key hemispherical well with red and blue paths and R." width="720" height="420" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
</figure>


 From the previous question we know that the slope of the wall $\theta = \frac{\pi}{4}$ at the collision points. So $D = R\sqrt{2}$.

$x$ displacement with $t_1$ is
$$
v_0\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{4}-\phi/2\right)t_1 = R\sqrt{2} \qquad (A1-1)
$$

$y$-component of velocity in the upper point is 0
$$
v_0\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{4}+\phi/2\right) - g\frac{t_2}{2} = 0
$$
transforms into
$$
v_0\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{4}-\phi/2\right) = g\frac{t_2}{2} \qquad (A1-2)
$$
Dividing (A1-1) by (A1-2) we get
$$
R = \frac{gt_1t_2}{2\sqrt{2}}.
$$
Only one $R$ satisfies this, so the answer is unique.

**c.** The slope of the wall $\theta=\frac{\pi}{4}$ at the collision points. So $z_x' = -1$ for one end and there is a symmetric point on the other point of collision. $-2\cos\frac{2x}{L}=-1$ has solutions $x=\pm\frac{\pi L}{6}$. So we got two possible values of $L$.

i. Case of $x=\frac{\pi L}{6}$ corresponds to $D=\frac{\pi L}{2}-\frac{\pi L}{3}=\frac{\pi L}{6}$
$$
v_0\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{4}-\phi/2\right)t_1 = \frac{\pi L}{6}
$$
and
$$
v_0\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{4}-\phi/2\right) = g\frac{t_2}{2}.
$$
Combining these two we get
$$
L_a = \frac{3gt_1t_2}{\pi}
$$

ii. Case of $x=-\frac{\pi L}{6}$ corresponds to $D=\frac{\pi L}{2}+\frac{\pi L}{3}=\frac{5\pi L}{6}$
$$
v_0\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{4}-\phi/2\right)t_1 = \frac{5\pi L}{6}
$$
and
$$
v_0\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{4}-\phi/2\right) = g\frac{t_2}{2}.
$$
Combining these two we get
$$
L_b = \frac{3gt_1t_2}{5\pi}
$$


</Spoiler>
