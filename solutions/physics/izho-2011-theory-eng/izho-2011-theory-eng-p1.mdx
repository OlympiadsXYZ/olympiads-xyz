## Условие

This problem consists of three independent parts.

**1A** The body is a cube in the center of which a spherical cavity of radius $R$ is carved out. Inside the spherical cavity at its bottom there is a motionless puck whose geometric sizes are negligible. Find the minimal horizontal velocity (at all possible cube-to-puck mass ratios) which has to be imposed on the puck so as the cubic body should jump up from the table surface in the motion process followed. Friction in the system is completely absent. At which value of the cube-to-puck mass ratio $M/m$ that minimal velocity is achieved? **[3,5 т.]**

**1B** The resistance $R = 2.0\ \Omega$ is connected to an infinite number of current sources obtained by repetition as shown in the figure on the right. Find the electric current flowing through the resistance $R$. The emfs’ and internal resistances of the sources are known to be $\varepsilon_1 = 2.0\ \mathrm{V}$, $r_1 = 1.0\ \Omega$, and $\varepsilon_2 = 1.0\ \mathrm{V}$, $r_2 = 2.0\ \Omega$. **[4 т.]**

**1C** In Figure on the right b, you can see the object AB and its image A’B’ in a thin lens. By drawing method, please find:
a) the optical center of the lens (0.5 points);
б) the lens plane (1 point);
в) the main focuses of the lens (0.5 points).
Is the lens concave (diverging) or convex (collecting)? Please write down the answer. (0.5 points). **[2,5 т.]**

<Spoiler title="Покажи отговорите">

- **1A** $\mathrm{v}_{\min} = 3\sqrt{gR}, \quad M/m = 2$

## Решение

<Spoiler title="Покажи официалното решение">

**1A (3.5 points)**

It is elementary to show that the jump of the cube appears at the puck position depicted in the picture on the left hand side. Let $u$ be the cube velocity of mass $M$ at this time moment and let $w$ be the horizontal relative velocity of the puck of mass $m$ with respect to the cube. Since the friction in the system is totally absent, the horizontal projection of the total momentum of the system is conserved,

<figure className="problem-figure problem-figure--sized" style={{'--fig-w': '36.5%', '--fig-max': '234px'}}>
<img src="https://pub-43290baaaff14857b5dd59610ea438c7.r2.dev/problems/izho-2011-theory-eng/p1-sol-fig1.png" alt="The cube on the hatched table with the spherical cavity; the puck at the top of the cavity with velocity w to the right and forces mg and N pointing down; the cube velocity u points to the left from the centre." width="730" height="569" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
</figure>

$$m\mathrm{v} = Mu + m(u - w), \qquad (1)$$

as well as with the total mechanical energy,

$$\frac{m\mathrm{v}^2}{2} = \frac{Mu^2}{2} + \frac{m}{2}\left[w^2 + (u - w)^2\right] + 2mgR. \qquad (2)$$

In the instant frame of reference associated with the cube, the puck moves with the velocity $w$ along the circle of radius $R$ and its equation of motion projected on the radial direction is given by

$$N + mg = \frac{mw^2}{R}. \qquad (3)$$

It is rather obvious that the condition of the cube’s jump from the plane of the table is found, according to Newton's third law, as

$$N = Mg. \qquad (4)$$

Solving the set of equations (1)-(4), the puck velocity is obtained as

$$\mathrm{v} = \sqrt{gR}\sqrt{5 + \frac{M}{m} + 4\frac{m}{M}}. \qquad (5)$$

The minimal velocity of the puck is derived from relation (5) by differentiating over $M/m$,

$$\mathrm{v}_{\min} = 3\sqrt{gR} \qquad (6)$$

and it is achieved at the mass ratio

$$M/m = 2. \qquad (7)$$

**1B (4 points)**

We can replace the infinite circuit of current sources by an effective current source with an emf $\varepsilon$ and an internal resistance $r$. Thus, we obtain the circuit shown in the figure on the left hand side. Then, we disconnect the resistance $R$, add another two current sources and connect back the resistance $R$. Hence, we obtain the circuit shown in the figure on the right hand side. Since the number of cells with the sources is infinite, then both circuits should be equivalent at any value of $R$.

<figure className="problem-figure problem-figure--sized" style={{'--fig-w': '57.2%', '--fig-max': '366px'}}>
<img src="https://pub-43290baaaff14857b5dd59610ea438c7.r2.dev/problems/izho-2011-theory-eng/p1-sol-fig2.png" alt="Two equivalent circuits joined by a double arrow: on the left an effective source ε, r connected to the resistor R; on the right the effective source ε, r, a source ε1, r1 in the upper wire, a source ε2, r2 in parallel, and the resistor R." width="1144" height="422" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
</figure>

It can be shown from the direct current laws that the following two statements are valid:

1. Let us take two current sources with $\varepsilon_1, r_1$ and $\varepsilon_2, r_2$, connected in series. Then, they can be replaced by a single source with $\varepsilon = \varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2$ and $r = r_1 + r_2$.
2. Let us take two current sources with $\varepsilon_1, r_1$ and $\varepsilon_2, r_2$, connected in parallel. Then, they can be replaced by a single source with $\varepsilon = (\varepsilon_1 r_2 + \varepsilon_2 r_1)/(r_1 + r_2)$ and $r = r_1 r_2/(r_1 + r_2)$.

Now, applying 1 and 2 to the circuit shown on the right hand side, we should obtain the circuit shown on the left hand side, thus the following relations must be satisfied:

$$\varepsilon = \frac{(\varepsilon + \varepsilon_1)r_2 + \varepsilon_2(r + r_1)}{r + r_1 + r_2}, \qquad (1)$$

$$r = \frac{r_2(r + r_1)}{r + r_1 + r_2}. \qquad (2)$$

Solution is given by

$$\varepsilon = \varepsilon_2 + \frac{\varepsilon_1}{2}\left(\sqrt{1 + \frac{4r_2}{r_1}} - 1\right) = 3.0\ \mathrm{V}, \qquad (3)$$

$$r = \frac{r_1}{2}\left(\sqrt{1 + \frac{4r_2}{r_1}} - 1\right) = 1.0\ \Omega. \qquad (4)$$

Therefore, the current flowing through the resistance $R$ is found as

$$I = \frac{\varepsilon}{R + r} = 1.0\ \mathrm{A}. \qquad (5)$$

**1C (2.5 points)**

<figure className="problem-figure problem-figure--sized" style={{'--fig-w': '85.7%', '--fig-max': '549px'}}>
<img src="https://pub-43290baaaff14857b5dd59610ea438c7.r2.dev/problems/izho-2011-theory-eng/p1-sol-fig3.png" alt="Ray construction: object AB (vertical arrow) and image B′A′ (horizontal arrow); lines AA′ and BB′ intersect at the optical centre O; the lens plane is the thick line through D, C, E and O; the dash-dotted main optical axis passes through O with the foci F1 (upper right) and F2 (lower) marked; auxiliary rays through D and E." width="1715" height="1762" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
</figure>

All the rays eradiated from the point A have to pass through the point A' after refraction in the lens; all the rays eradiated from the point B have to pass through the point B' after refraction in the lens. Rays passing through the optical center of the lens do not change direction. Therefore, the point of intersection of lines AA' and BB' is the optical center O of the lens. If a ray passes through both the points A and B, then it should necessarily pass through the points A' and B'. Consequently, the point of the intersection of lines AB and A'B' lies in the plane of the lens. Thus, the plane of the lens passes through the points O and C. The main optical axis of the lens passes through its optical center and is perpendicular to the plane of the lens, Further constructions are traditional: we draw ray BD through the point B which is parallel to the main optical axis, and after refraction in the lens the ray (or its extension) should pass through B'. From its continuation to the intersection with the main optical axis, we find one of the main focuses $F_1$. Similarly, we find the second main focus $F_2$. The drawing above shows that the lens is concave (diverging).
