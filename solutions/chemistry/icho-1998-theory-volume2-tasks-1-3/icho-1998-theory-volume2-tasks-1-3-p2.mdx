Preface  
This publication contains the competition problems (Volume 2) from the 21st – 40th  
International Chemistry Olympiads (IChO) organized in the years 1989 – 2008  and is a
continuation of the publication that appeared last year as Volume 1 and contained
competition problems from the first twenty IChOs.  The whole review of the competition
tasks set in the IChO in its fourty-year history is a contribution of the IChO International  
Information Centre in Bratislava (Slovakia) to the development of this world known
international competition. This Volume 2 contains 154 theoretical and 46 practical
competition problems from the mentioned years. The review as a whole presents
altogether 279 theoretical and 96 practical problems.  
In the elaboration of this collection the editor had to face certain difficulties because
the aim was not only to make use of past recordings but also to give them such a form
that they may be used in practice and further chemical education. Consequently, it was
necessary to make some corrections in order to unify the form of the problems. However,
they did not concern the contents and language of the problems.  
Unfortunately, the authors of the particular competition problems are not known and
due to the procedure of the creation of the IChO competition problems, it is impossible to
assign any author's name to a particular problem. As the editor I would appreciate many  
times some discussion with the authors about any critical places that occurred in the text.  
On the other hand, any additional amendments to the text would be not correct from the  
historical point of view. Therefore, responsibility for the scientific content and language of
the problems lies exclusively with the organizers of the particular International Chemistry  
Olympiads.    

Some parts of texts, especially those gained as scanned materials, could not be
used directly and thus, several texts, schemes and pictures had to be re-written or created
again. Some solutions were often available in a brief form and necessary extent only, just
for the needs of members of the International Jury.  

Recalculations of the solutions were made in some special cases only when the
numeric results in the original solutions showed to be obviously not correct. Although the
numbers of significant figures in the results of several solutions do not obey the criteria
generally accepted, they were left without change.      



In this publication SI quantities and units are used and a more modern method of
chemical calculations is introduced. Only some exceptions have been made when, in an
effort to preserve the original text, the quantities and units have been used that are not SI.  
There were some problems with the presentation of the solutions of practical tasks,
because most of the relatively simple calculations were based on the experimental results
of contestants. Moreover, the practical problems are accompanied with answer sheets in
the last years and several additional questions and tasks have appeared in them that were
not a part of the text of the original experimental problems. Naturally, answer sheets could
not be included in this publication and can only be preserved as archive materials.
  
When reading the texts of the IChO problems one must admire and appreciate the
work of those many known and unknown people – teachers, authors, pupils, and
organizers – who contributed so much to development and success of this important
international competition.

I am sure about the usefulness of the this review of the IChO problems. It may serve
not only as archive material but, in particular, this review should serve to both competitors
and their teachers as a source of further inspiration in their preparation for this challenging
competition.




Bratislava, July 2009

### Part A: Dating Historical Events Using Pb-210


Nathan Thompson, one of the first inhabitants of Lord Howe Island, planted some European deciduous trees in his garden. The exact time of planting is unknown. Over the years, pollen from the European oak and elm accumulated at the bottom of the lake near his house. Very small quantities of radioactive Pb-210, with half-life 22.0 years, were deposited at the same time. European oak and elm pollinate in their first year of growth.

In 1995, researchers sampled a sediment core from the lake bottom, cut it into 1 cm slices and examined the slices for pollen and radioactive Pb-210. They found:

- European oak and elm pollen first occurs at a depth of 50 cm.
- Pb-210 activity is 356 Bq/kg at the top and 1.40 Bq/kg at 50 cm.



**2.1**  
In what year did Nathan Thompson plant the seeds?

Pb-210 is a daughter of U-238. U-238 occurs in the earth's crust; some Pb-210 rains out of the atmosphere and attaches to sediment particles accumulating at lake bottoms. The printed abbreviated decay chain is

$$
\mathrm{U\!-\!238\longrightarrow U\!-\!234\longrightarrow Th\!-\!230
\longrightarrow Ra\!-\!226\longrightarrow Rn\!-\!222
\longrightarrow(Po\!-\!218\longrightarrow Bi\!-\!214)^*
\longrightarrow Pb\!-\!210\longrightarrow Pb\!-\!206\ (stable)}.
$$

The starred nuclides have very short half-lives: minutes and days.



**2.2**  
Which step explains how Pb-210 reaches rainwater while its parent U-238 occurs only in the earth's crust?



</ProblemSection>

### Part B: Separation of Radionuclides for Nuclear Medicine Applications


Ga-67 is used to image lymphoma. It is preferentially produced by bombarding a Zn-68-enriched target, greater than 98%, with high-energy protons for 11 hours. Natural Zn-68 abundance is 18.8%. Target design allows production of other radionuclides. Twelve hours after bombardment, Ga-67 is bound on a cation exchange. Other radionuclides and Zn-68 are eluted in the wash solution, leaving Ga-67 on the column.

**Table 1**

| Radionuclide | Half-life |
| --- | --- |
| Co-55 | 18.2 hr |
| Ni-57 | 36.0 hr |
| Co-57 (daughter of Ni-57) | 270 days |
| Cu-64 | 12.7 hr |
| Cu-67 | 61.7 hr |
| Zn-65 | 244 days |
| Ga-67 | 78.35 hr |
| Ga-66 | 9.4 hr |

Cu-64 and Co-55 have ideal half-lives for nuclear medicine, so isolating them from the wash solution would be useful.

The distribution coefficient $D$ measures partition of a metal ion between ion-exchange resin and eluant:

$$
D=\frac{\text{radioactivity per mg of resin}}
{\text{radioactivity per cm}^3\text{ of eluant}}.
$$

Reasonable separation requires the two ions' $D$ values to differ by at least 10 units.

**2.3**  
Evaporate the wash solution to dryness, resuspend in a small amount of 0.2 M HCl in 96% methanol, and load onto an anion-exchange column. Use figures 1 and 2 to rank the best solvent systems, from the given alternatives, for eluting Cu-64 and Co-55.



**2.4**  
Long-lived radionuclides may interfere with medical use of Cu-64, Co-55 or Ga-67. Mark each statement true or false; one or more may be true.

a) Ni-57 may be present as a contaminant of Co-55.

b) Co-57 will interfere with medical use of Co-55.

c) Cu-67 will interfere with medical use of Cu-64.

d) Ga-66 will interfere with use of Ga-67.

e) Ga-67 will interfere with medical use of Cu-64.



**2.5**  
Which methods reduce radionuclide contaminants of Cu-64, Co-55 or Ga-67? Mark each statement true or false; one or more may be true.

a) Remove Ni-57 before isolating Co-55.

b) Separate Ni-57 from the bombarded target material before isolating Ga-67.

c) Separate radionuclides closer to the end of bombardment.

d) Allow Ni-57 to decay before isolating Co-55.



**2.6**  
If naturally abundant zinc replaces enriched Zn-68 during proton bombardment, mark each statement true or false; one or more may be true.

a) Ga-67 would be produced at fivefold higher yields.

b) Ga-67 would be produced at fivefold lower yields.

c) Ga-67 would have lower yields and contamination by Cu-64, Co-55, Co-57 and Ni-57 would increase.

d) Ga-67 would have lower yields and contamination by Cu-64, Co-55, Co-57 and Ni-57 would remain the same.

### 2.1


The printed apparent-decay sequence over 50 cm is

$$
356-178-89-44.5-22.5-11.25-5.63-2.81-1.39.
$$

This represents eight half-lives, or $8\times22=176$ years. The planting year is $1995-176=1819\ (\pm2)$.



</ProblemSection>

<ProblemSection id="solution-native-key-2">

### 2.2


Ra-226 → Rn-222.



</ProblemSection>

<ProblemSection id="solution-native-key-3">

### 2.3


The four alternatives are printed in the solution, with boxes for ranks 1–4 (1 best):

| Alternative | Elution sequence |
| --- | --- |
| A | 0.2 M HCl / 96% methanol to remove Ni-57; then 2.0 M HCl / 55% isopropyl alcohol to remove Cu-64; then 1.0 M HCl / 55% isopropyl alcohol to remove Co-55 |
| B | 0.2 M HCl / 96% methanol to remove Ni-57; then 2.0 M HCl / 55% isopropyl alcohol to remove Co-55; then 1.0 M HCl / 55% isopropyl alcohol to remove Cu-64 |
| C | 2.0 M HCl / 55% isopropyl alcohol to remove Co-55; then 1.0 M HCl / 55% isopropyl alcohol to remove Cu-64 |
| D | 0.2 M HCl / 96% methanol to remove Ni-57; then 3.0 M HCl / 55% isopropyl alcohol to remove Co-55; then 4.0 M HCl / 55% isopropyl alcohol to remove Cu-64 |

Best sequence: **B, C, D, A**.

The sequences **B, C, A, D**, **C, B, D, A** and **C, B, A, D** were also accepted, with fewer points.



</ProblemSection>
