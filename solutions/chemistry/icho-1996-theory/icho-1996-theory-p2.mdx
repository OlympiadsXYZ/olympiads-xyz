This publication contains the competition problems (Volume 2) from the 21st – 40th  
International Chemistry Olympiads (IChO) organized in the years 1989 – 2008  and is a
continuation of the publication that appeared last year as Volume 1 and contained
competition problems from the first twenty IChOs.  The whole review of the competition
tasks set in the IChO in its fourty-year history is a contribution of the IChO International  
Information Centre in Bratislava (Slovakia) to the development of this world known
international competition. This Volume 2 contains 154 theoretical and 46 practical
competition problems from the mentioned years. The review as a whole presents
altogether 279 theoretical and 96 practical problems.  
In the elaboration of this collection the editor had to face certain difficulties because
the aim was not only to make use of past recordings but also to give them such a form
that they may be used in practice and further chemical education. Consequently, it was
necessary to make some corrections in order to unify the form of the problems. However,
they did not concern the contents and language of the problems.  
Unfortunately, the authors of the particular competition problems are not known and
due to the procedure of the creation of the IChO competition problems, it is impossible to
assign any author's name to a particular problem. As the editor I would appreciate many  
times some discussion with the authors about any critical places that occurred in the text.  
On the other hand, any additional amendments to the text would be not correct from the  
historical point of view. Therefore, responsibility for the scientific content and language of
the problems lies exclusively with the organizers of the particular International Chemistry  
Olympiads.    

Some parts of texts, especially those gained as scanned materials, could not be
used directly and thus, several texts, schemes and pictures had to be re-written or created
again. Some solutions were often available in a brief form and necessary extent only, just
for the needs of members of the International Jury.  

Recalculations of the solutions were made in some special cases only when the
numeric results in the original solutions showed to be obviously not correct. Although the
numbers of significant figures in the results of several solutions do not obey the criteria
generally accepted, they were left without change.      



In this publication SI quantities and units are used and a more modern method of
chemical calculations is introduced. Only some exceptions have been made when, in an
effort to preserve the original text, the quantities and units have been used that are not SI.  
There were some problems with the presentation of the solutions of practical tasks,
because most of the relatively simple calculations were based on the experimental results
of contestants. Moreover, the practical problems are accompanied with answer sheets in
the last years and several additional questions and tasks have appeared in them that were
not a part of the text of the original experimental problems. Naturally, answer sheets could
not be included in this publication and can only be preserved as archive materials.
  
When reading the texts of the IChO problems one must admire and appreciate the
work of those many known and unknown people – teachers, authors, pupils, and
organizers – who contributed so much to development and success of this important
international competition.

I am sure about the usefulness of the this review of the IChO problems. It may serve
not only as archive material but, in particular, this review should serve to both competitors
and their teachers as a source of further inspiration in their preparation for this challenging
competition.

The detection limit is one of the basic parameters in quantitative analysis of trace amounts of elements: the least mass of an element determined by a method with given accuracy. Consider the determination of microscopic amounts of bismuth. In1927 German chemist Berg suggested precipitating bismuth as practically insoluble8-hydroxyquinolinium tetraiodobismuthate, $\mathrm{[C_9H_6(OH)NH][BiI_4]}$, $M_r=862.7$.

<ProblemSection id="native-section-1">

### Tasks




**2.1**  
a) Draw structural formulae of the cation and anion of this salt. b) What is the oxidation state of Bi?



**2.2**  
Evaluate the smallest reliably determined mass of bismuth in mg by Berg's method, if the smallest reliably measured precipitate mass is50.0mg.

For trace bismuth, R.Belcher and co-workers from Birmingham developed a multiplicative method: a chain of reactions followed by titration of the final product. A detailed description follows.

Step1: To approximately $2\ \mathrm{cm^3}$ cold acidified solution containing trace $\mathrm{Bi^{3+}}$, add50mg potassium hexathiocyanatochromate(III), $\mathrm{K_3[Cr(SCN)_6]}$, in the cold. Bismuth precipitation is practically quantitative.



**2.3**  
Write a balanced net ionic equation of this reaction.

Step2: Filter off the precipitate, wash with cold water, and treat with $5\ \mathrm{cm^3}$10% sodium hydrogen carbonate. The precipitate transforms to oxobismuth(III) carbonate, $\mathrm{(BiO)_2CO_3}$, releasing hexathiocyanatochromate(III) ions into solution.



**2.4**  
Write a balanced net ionic equation of this reaction.

Step3: Transfer the slightly acidified filtrate to a separatory funnel, add $0.5\ \mathrm{cm^3}$ saturated iodine solution in chloroform, and shake vigorously. Iodine oxidizes the complex ligand to ICN and sulphate.



**2.5**  
Write a balanced net ionic equation of this reaction.

Step4: After5min add $4\ \mathrm{cm^3}$2M $\mathrm{H_2SO_4}$. Acidification causes coproportionation with evolution of molecular iodine.



**2.6**  
Write a balanced net ionic equation of the reaction on acidification.

Step5: Quantitatively extract iodine with4portions of chloroform. Transfer the aqueous layer to a flask, add $1\ \mathrm{cm^3}$ bromine water and mix5min.



**2.7**  
Write balanced net ionic equations upon addition of bromine water. Excess bromine can react with hydrogen cyanide to give BrCN, and iodide is oxidized to $\mathrm{IO_3^-}$.

Step6: Eliminate excess molecular bromine by adding $3\ \mathrm{cm^3}$90% methanoic(formic) acid.



**2.8**  
Write a balanced net ionic equation of this reaction.

Step7: Add excess(1.5g) potassium iodide to the slightly acidic solution.



**2.9**  
Write balanced net ionic equations upon addition of KI, considering that iodide reacts with BrCN similarly to ICN, forming molecular iodine.

Step8: Titrate the resulting solution with standard0.00200M $\mathrm{Na_2S_2O_3}$ and use the results to calculate sample bismuth content.



**2.10**  
a) How many moles of thiosulphate are equivalent to1mol initial bismuth? b) What is the least determined bismuth mass, assuming reliable determination requires at least $1\ \mathrm{cm^3}$ standard0.00200M $\mathrm{Na_2S_2O_3}$?



**2.11**  
How many times more sensitive is this multiplicative method than Berg's gravimetric method?



</ProblemSection>

### 2.10


a)228mol thiosulphate corresponds to1mol bismuth. Iodine titration follows $\mathrm{I_2+2S_2O_3^{2-}\longrightarrow2I^-+S_4O_6^{2-}}$.

Assume1mol initial Bi. In reaction5 each Bi produces42mol iodide (for convenience divide all coefficients of reaction4 by2);6mol is consumed in2.6. Thus36mol is consumed in2.7(a), giving36mol iodate. Reaction2.9(a) gives $36\times3=108$mol iodine, taking $108\times2=216$mol thiosulphate. Additionally,6mol HCN is generated per Bi by2.5 and2.6. Reaction2.7(b) gives6mol BrCN, which2.9(b) converts to6mol iodine, consuming12more mol thiosulphate. Total:$216+12=228$.

b) $1.00\ \mathrm{cm^3}$0.00200M thiosulphate contains $2.00\times10^{-6}$mol $\mathrm{Na_2S_2O_3}$, corresponding to

$$
\frac{209.0\times2.00\times10^{-6}}{228}=1.83\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm g=1.83\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{mg}=1.83\ \mu\mathrm g.
$$



</ProblemSection>

### 2.11


$$
\frac{\text{Detection limit of gravimetric method}}{\text{Detection limit of multiplicated method}}=6600.
$$

The multiplicative method is more sensitive by $12.1\ \mathrm{mg}/(1.83\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{mg})=6600$times.
