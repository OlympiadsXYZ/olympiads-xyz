This publication contains the competition problems (Volume 2) from the 21st – 40th  
International Chemistry Olympiads (IChO) organized in the years 1989 – 2008  and is a
continuation of the publication that appeared last year as Volume 1 and contained
competition problems from the first twenty IChOs.  The whole review of the competition
tasks set in the IChO in its fourty-year history is a contribution of the IChO International  
Information Centre in Bratislava (Slovakia) to the development of this world known
international competition. This Volume 2 contains 154 theoretical and 46 practical
competition problems from the mentioned years. The review as a whole presents
altogether 279 theoretical and 96 practical problems.  
In the elaboration of this collection the editor had to face certain difficulties because
the aim was not only to make use of past recordings but also to give them such a form
that they may be used in practice and further chemical education. Consequently, it was
necessary to make some corrections in order to unify the form of the problems. However,
they did not concern the contents and language of the problems.  
Unfortunately, the authors of the particular competition problems are not known and
due to the procedure of the creation of the IChO competition problems, it is impossible to
assign any author's name to a particular problem. As the editor I would appreciate many  
times some discussion with the authors about any critical places that occurred in the text.  
On the other hand, any additional amendments to the text would be not correct from the  
historical point of view. Therefore, responsibility for the scientific content and language of
the problems lies exclusively with the organizers of the particular International Chemistry  
Olympiads.    

Some parts of texts, especially those gained as scanned materials, could not be
used directly and thus, several texts, schemes and pictures had to be re-written or created
again. Some solutions were often available in a brief form and necessary extent only, just
for the needs of members of the International Jury.  

Recalculations of the solutions were made in some special cases only when the
numeric results in the original solutions showed to be obviously not correct. Although the
numbers of significant figures in the results of several solutions do not obey the criteria
generally accepted, they were left without change.      



In this publication SI quantities and units are used and a more modern method of
chemical calculations is introduced. Only some exceptions have been made when, in an
effort to preserve the original text, the quantities and units have been used that are not SI.  
There were some problems with the presentation of the solutions of practical tasks,
because most of the relatively simple calculations were based on the experimental results
of contestants. Moreover, the practical problems are accompanied with answer sheets in
the last years and several additional questions and tasks have appeared in them that were
not a part of the text of the original experimental problems. Naturally, answer sheets could
not be included in this publication and can only be preserved as archive materials.
  
When reading the texts of the IChO problems one must admire and appreciate the
work of those many known and unknown people – teachers, authors, pupils, and
organizers – who contributed so much to development and success of this important
international competition.

I am sure about the usefulness of the this review of the IChO problems. It may serve
not only as archive material but, in particular, this review should serve to both competitors
and their teachers as a source of further inspiration in their preparation for this challenging
competition.




Bratislava, July 2009

Denmark's subsoil is mainly limestone. Groundwater containing carbon dioxide dissolves some calcium carbonate as calcium hydrogen carbonate. This makes the water hard; as tap water it precipitates calcium carbonate in kitchens and bathrooms.

Carbon dioxide is a diprotic acid in water. At $0\,{}^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{CO_2(aq)}+\mathrm{H_2O(l)}&\rightleftharpoons\mathrm{HCO_3^-(aq)}+\mathrm{H^+(aq)},&pK_{a1}&=6.630,\\
\mathrm{HCO_3^-(aq)}&\rightleftharpoons\mathrm{CO_3^{2-}(aq)}+\mathrm{H^+(aq)},&pK_{a2}&=10.640.
\end{aligned}
$$

Neglect liquid-volume changes from dissolving $\mathrm{CO_2}$ throughout. All temperatures are $0\,{}^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$.

<ProblemSection id="native-section-1">

### Carbon dioxide and carbonate solutions




**6.1**  
At $p(\mathrm{CO_2})=1.00\,\mathrm{bar}$ the total carbon-dioxide concentration in saturated water is $0.0752\,\mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}}$. Calculate the gas volume dissolved in one litre of water. $R=8.314\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}=0.08314\,\mathrm{L\,bar\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$.



**6.2**  
Calculate the equilibrium concentrations of hydrogen ions and $\mathrm{CO_2}$ in water saturated at $p(\mathrm{CO_2})=1.00\,\mathrm{bar}$.



**6.3**  
Calculate the equilibrium hydrogen-ion concentration in $0.0100\,\mathrm{M}$ aqueous sodium hydrogen carbonate saturated at $p(\mathrm{CO_2})=1.00\,\mathrm{bar}$.



**6.4**  
Calculate the equilibrium hydrogen-ion concentration in $0.0100\,\mathrm{M}$ aqueous sodium carbonate saturated at $p(\mathrm{CO_2})=1.00\,\mathrm{bar}$. Ignore water dissociation.



**6.5**  
Calcium carbonate's solubility is $0.0012\,\mathrm{g}$ per $100\,\mathrm{cm^3}$ of water. Calculate the calcium-ion concentration in its saturated aqueous solution.



</ProblemSection>

<ProblemSection id="native-section-2">

### Hard groundwater


Groundwater reacts with limestone and dissolved carbon dioxide:

$$
\mathrm{CaCO_3(s)}+\mathrm{CO_2(aq)}+\mathrm{H_2O(l)}\rightleftharpoons\mathrm{Ca^{2+}(aq)}+2\mathrm{HCO_3^-(aq)}.
$$

The equilibrium constant is $K=10^{-4.25}$ at $0\,{}^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$.



**6.6**  
Calculate calcium-ion concentration in water equilibrated with calcium carbonate in an atmosphere with carbon-dioxide partial pressure $1.00\,\mathrm{bar}$.



**6.7**  
A $0.0150\,\mathrm{M}$ calcium-hydroxide solution is saturated with carbon dioxide at $1.00\,\mathrm{bar}$. Calculate calcium-ion concentration using the equilibrium in part 6.6.



**6.8**  
Dilute the part 6.7 calcium-hydroxide solution to twice its volume before saturating with carbon dioxide at $1.00\,\mathrm{bar}$. Calculate calcium-ion concentration in the resulting saturated solution.



**6.9**  
Calculate calcium carbonate's solubility-product constant from the data above.



</ProblemSection>

## Решение

<Spoiler title="Покажи решението от архива">




<ProblemSection id="solution-native-key-1">

### 6.1


$c(\mathrm{CO_2})=0.0752\,\mathrm{M}$; $n(\mathrm{CO_2})=0.0752\,\mathrm{mol}$. From $pV=nRT$:

$$
1.00\,\mathrm{bar}\times V=0.0752\,\mathrm{mol}\times0.08314\,\mathrm{dm^3\,bar\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\times273.15\,\mathrm{K},
$$

so $V=1.71\,\mathrm{dm^3}$.



</ProblemSection>

<ProblemSection id="solution-native-key-2">

### 6.2


$\mathrm{CO_2+H_2O\to HCO_3^-+H^+}$; $[\mathrm{H^+}]=[\mathrm{HCO_3^-}]=x$, and $[\mathrm{CO_2}]+[\mathrm{HCO_3^-}]=0.0752$.

$$
K_a=10^{-6.63}=\frac{[\mathrm{H^+}][\mathrm{HCO_3^-}]}{[\mathrm{CO_2}]}=\frac{x^2}{0.0752-x}.
$$

$[\mathrm{H^+}]=0.000133$ and $[\mathrm{CO_2}]=0.0751$.



</ProblemSection>

<ProblemSection id="solution-native-key-3">

### 6.3


$[\mathrm{CO_2}]=0.0751$ and $[\mathrm{HCO_3^-}]=0.0100$ in $\mathrm{CO_2+H_2O\to HCO_3^-+H^+}$:

$$
K_a=10^{-6.63}=\frac{[\mathrm{H^+}][\mathrm{HCO_3^-}]}{[\mathrm{CO_2}]}=\frac{x(0.0100)}{0.0751},\qquad x=[\mathrm{H^+}]=1.76\times10^{-6}.
$$



</ProblemSection>

<ProblemSection id="solution-native-key-4">

### 6.4


$\mathrm{CO_2+CO_3^{2-}+H_2O\to2HCO_3^-}$ gives $[\mathrm{HCO_3^-}]=0.0200$. For $\mathrm{CO_2+H_2O\to HCO_3^-+H^+}$:

$$
K_a=10^{-6.63}=\frac{[\mathrm{H^+}][\mathrm{HCO_3^-}]}{[\mathrm{CO_2}]}=\frac{x(0.0200)}{0.0751},\qquad x=[\mathrm{H^+}]=8.8\times10^{-7}.
$$



</ProblemSection>

<ProblemSection id="solution-native-key-5">

### 6.5


The key prints $0.0012\,\mathrm{g}$ of $\mathrm{CaCO_3}$ in $100\,\mathrm{cm^3}$, followed by

$$
0.0012\,\mathrm{g}\,/100\times0872\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}=0.000012\,\mathrm{mol\ CaCO_3}\text{ in }100\,\mathrm{cm^3}\text{ of water}.
$$

$[\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}]=1.2\times10^{-4}$; $c(\mathrm{Ca^{2+}})=1.2\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}}$.



</ProblemSection>

<ProblemSection id="solution-native-key-6">

### 6.6


The printed expressions are

$$
K=\frac{[\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}][\mathrm{HCO_3^-}]}{[\mathrm{CO_2}]}=10^{-4.25},\qquad 2[\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}]=[\mathrm{HCO_3^-}],
$$

$$
\frac{4[\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}]}{0.0751}=10^{-4.25},\qquad [\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}]=1.02\times10^{-2},\qquad c(\mathrm{Ca^{2+}})=1.02\times10^{-2}\,\mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}}.
$$



</ProblemSection>

<ProblemSection id="solution-native-key-7">

### 6.7


$c(\mathrm{Ca(OH)_2})=0.015\,\mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}}$ and $\mathrm{OH^-+CO_2\to HCO_3^-}$. All hydroxide has been consumed ($K=10^{7.37}$). Part 6.6 gives a smaller maximum calcium concentration, so $\mathrm{CaCO_3}$ precipitates. $[\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}]=1.02\times10^{-2}$; $c=1.02\times10^{-2}\,\mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}}$.



</ProblemSection>

<ProblemSection id="solution-native-key-8">

### 6.8


$c(\mathrm{Ca(OH)_2})=0.0075\,\mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}}$, below the maximum $1.02\times10^{-2}$ from 6.6. No calcium carbonate precipitates; $[\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}]=0.75\times10^{-2}$ and $c=0.75\times10^{-2}\,\mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}}$.



</ProblemSection>

<ProblemSection id="solution-native-key-9">

### 6.9


The key prints

$$
K=\frac{[\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}][\mathrm{HCO_3^-}]}{[\mathrm{CO_2}]}=\frac{[\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}][\mathrm{HCO_3^-}]}{[\mathrm{CO_2}]}\times\frac{[\mathrm{CO_3^{2-}}][\mathrm{H^+}]}{[\mathrm{CO_3^{2-}}][\mathrm{H^+}]}=\frac{K_{sp}K_{a1}}{K_{a2}},\qquad K_{sp}=10^{-8.26}.
$$



</ProblemSection>
