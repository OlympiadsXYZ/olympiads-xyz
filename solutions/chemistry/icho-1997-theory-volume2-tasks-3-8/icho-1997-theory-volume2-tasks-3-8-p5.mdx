This publication contains the competition problems (Volume 2) from the 21st – 40th  
International Chemistry Olympiads (IChO) organized in the years 1989 – 2008  and is a
continuation of the publication that appeared last year as Volume 1 and contained
competition problems from the first twenty IChOs.  The whole review of the competition
tasks set in the IChO in its fourty-year history is a contribution of the IChO International  
Information Centre in Bratislava (Slovakia) to the development of this world known
international competition. This Volume 2 contains 154 theoretical and 46 practical
competition problems from the mentioned years. The review as a whole presents
altogether 279 theoretical and 96 practical problems.  
In the elaboration of this collection the editor had to face certain difficulties because
the aim was not only to make use of past recordings but also to give them such a form
that they may be used in practice and further chemical education. Consequently, it was
necessary to make some corrections in order to unify the form of the problems. However,
they did not concern the contents and language of the problems.  
Unfortunately, the authors of the particular competition problems are not known and
due to the procedure of the creation of the IChO competition problems, it is impossible to
assign any author's name to a particular problem. As the editor I would appreciate many  
times some discussion with the authors about any critical places that occurred in the text.  
On the other hand, any additional amendments to the text would be not correct from the  
historical point of view. Therefore, responsibility for the scientific content and language of
the problems lies exclusively with the organizers of the particular International Chemistry  
Olympiads.    

Some parts of texts, especially those gained as scanned materials, could not be
used directly and thus, several texts, schemes and pictures had to be re-written or created
again. Some solutions were often available in a brief form and necessary extent only, just
for the needs of members of the International Jury.  

Recalculations of the solutions were made in some special cases only when the
numeric results in the original solutions showed to be obviously not correct. Although the
numbers of significant figures in the results of several solutions do not obey the criteria
generally accepted, they were left without change.      



In this publication SI quantities and units are used and a more modern method of
chemical calculations is introduced. Only some exceptions have been made when, in an
effort to preserve the original text, the quantities and units have been used that are not SI.  
There were some problems with the presentation of the solutions of practical tasks,
because most of the relatively simple calculations were based on the experimental results
of contestants. Moreover, the practical problems are accompanied with answer sheets in
the last years and several additional questions and tasks have appeared in them that were
not a part of the text of the original experimental problems. Naturally, answer sheets could
not be included in this publication and can only be preserved as archive materials.
  
When reading the texts of the IChO problems one must admire and appreciate the
work of those many known and unknown people – teachers, authors, pupils, and
organizers – who contributed so much to development and success of this important
international competition.

I am sure about the usefulness of the this review of the IChO problems. It may serve
not only as archive material but, in particular, this review should serve to both competitors
and their teachers as a source of further inspiration in their preparation for this challenging
competition.

**В тази задача**

- [Problem 5](#native-section-1)

</nav>


Iron metal melts at 1811 K. Between room temperature and its melting point it can exist in different allotropic or crystalline forms. From room temperature to 1185 K, its body-centred cubic (bcc) lattice is known as $\alpha$-iron. From 1185 K to 1667 K, the structure is face-centred cubic (fcc), called $\gamma$-iron. Above 1667 K to melting it reverts to a bcc structure similar to $\alpha$-iron; this phase is $\delta$-iron.

The density of pure iron is $7.874\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ at 293 K.

<ProblemSection id="native-section-1">

### Problem 5

**5.1**  
Calculate the atomic radius of iron in cm.



**5.2**  
Calculate its density in $\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ at 1250 K. Ignore small thermal-expansion effects. Clearly define any symbols used, e.g. $r=$ atomic radius of Fe.

Steel is an iron–carbon alloy in which small carbon atoms occupy some interstitial spaces (“holes”) of the iron crystal lattice. Its carbon content typically ranges from 0.1% to 4.0%. In a blast furnace, melting is facilitated by 4.3% carbon by mass. If this mixture is cooled too rapidly, carbon remains dispersed within the $\alpha$-iron phase. The new solid, martensite, is extremely hard and brittle. Although slightly distorted, its unit-cell size is the same as $\alpha$-iron (bcc).

Assume carbon is evenly distributed in the iron structure.



**5.3**  
Calculate the average number of carbon atoms per unit cell of $\alpha$-iron in martensite containing 4.3% C by mass.



**5.4**  
Calculate the density of this material in $\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$.

Molar masses and constants:

$$
M(\mathrm{Fe})=55.847\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}},\quad
M(\mathrm{C})=12.011\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}},\quad
N_A=6.02214\times10^{23}\ \mathrm{mol^{-1}}.
$$

### 5.1–5.2


**Expected steps of the calculation**

1. Define lengths $a,b,c,d_1,d_2,r$ and volumes $V_1,V_2$ for bcc and fcc iron (details below).
2. Calculate $V_1$ of the $\alpha$-iron unit cell from density $\rho_{\mathrm{bcc}}$ at 293 K, molar weight $M(\mathrm{Fe})$, and $N_A$.
3. Calculate bcc edge $d_1$ from $V_1$.
4. Calculate atomic radius $r$ from $d_1$.
5. Calculate fcc edge $d_2$ at 1250 K from $r$.
6. Calculate fcc volume $V_2$ of $\gamma$-iron from $d_2$.
7. Calculate mass $m$ of the iron atoms in a $\gamma$-iron unit cell using $M(\mathrm{Fe})$ and $N_A$.
8. Calculate $\rho_{\mathrm{fcc}}$ from $m$ and $V_2$.

An alternative route uses the percent space-filling ratios of both cells, replacing steps 5–8:

5′. Calculate the bcc space-filling ratio.

6′. Calculate the fcc space-filling ratio.

7′. Calculate $\rho_{\mathrm{fcc}}/\rho_{\mathrm{bcc}}$ from fcc/bcc space-filling ratios.

8′. Calculate $\rho_{\mathrm{fcc}}$ from step 7′.

**Calculations in detail**

At 293 K, $\alpha$-iron is bcc: each unit cell has two atoms, one at its centre. At 1250 K, $\gamma$-iron is fcc: each unit cell has four atoms, each side having one-half an atom at its centre.

| Symbol | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| $r$ | atomic radius of iron |
| $a$ | diagonal on one side of the bcc unit cell |
| $b$ | diagonal passing through the centre of the bcc unit cell |
| $c$ | diagonal on one side of the fcc unit cell |
| $d_1$ | edge of the bcc $\alpha$-iron cell |
| $d_2$ | edge of the fcc $\gamma$-iron cell |
| $V_1$ | bcc $\alpha$-iron cell volume |
| $V_2$ | fcc $\gamma$-iron cell volume |
| $V_a$ | volume of one atom |
| $V_{a1}$ | volume occupied by two atoms in a bcc cell |
| $V_{a2}$ | volume occupied by four atoms in an fcc cell |
| $R_1$ | bcc percent space-filling ratio |
| $R_2$ | fcc percent space-filling ratio |

$$
V_a=\frac43\pi r^3,\quad V_{a1}=2V_a,\quad V_{a2}=4V_a.
$$

$$
b=4r,\quad a^2=2d_1^2,\quad b^2=d_1^2+a^2=3d_1^2,
$$

$$
d_1=(b^2/3)^{1/2}=(16r^2/3)^{1/2},\quad
V_1=d_1^3=[(16r^2/3)^{1/2}]^3,
$$

$$
c=4r,\quad c^2=2d_2^2,\quad
d_2=(c^2/2)^{1/2}=(16r^2/2)^{1/2},\quad
V_2=d_2^3=[(16r^2/2)^{1/2}]^3.
$$

2. At 293 K, $1.000\ \mathrm{cm^3}$ of iron weighs 7.874 g; a mole weighs 55.847 g. Thus $7.874/55.847=0.1410$ mol occupies $1.000\ \mathrm{cm^3}$, or a mole occupies $7.093\ \mathrm{cm^3}$. A mole corresponds to $6.02214\times10^{23}$ atoms:

$$
V_1=\frac{(7.093\ \mathrm{cm^3\,mol^{-1}})(2\ \text{atoms/unit cell})}
{6.02214\times10^{23}\ \text{atoms/mol}}
=2.356\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm{cm^3/unit\ cell}.
$$

3. $d_1=V_1^{1/3}=(2.356\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm{cm^3})^{1/3}=2.867\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{cm}$.

4. For bcc, $d_1=(16r^2/3)^{1/2}$, hence

$$
r=(3d_1^2/16)^{1/2}
=[3(2.867\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{cm})^2/16]^{1/2}
=1.241\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{cm}.
$$

5. For fcc at 1250 K:

$$
d_2=(16r^2/2)^{1/2}
=[16(1.241\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{cm})^2/2]^{1/2}
=3.511\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{cm}.
$$

6. $V_2=d_2^3=(3.511\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{cm})^3=4.327\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm{cm^3}$.

7. The mass of four iron atoms is

$$
m=\frac{(55.847\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}})(4\ \text{atoms/unit cell})}
{6.02214\times10^{23}\ \text{atoms/mol}}
=3.709\times10^{-22}\ \mathrm{g/unit\ cell}.
$$

8. $\rho_{\mathrm{fcc}}=m/V_2=(3.709\times10^{-22})/(4.327\times10^{-23})=8.572\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$.

**Alternative route**

$$
\begin{aligned}
R_1&=\frac{V_{a1}}{V_1}\times100\%
=\frac{2V_a}{V_1}\times100\%\\
&=\frac{2(4/3)\pi r^3}{[(16r^2/3)^{1/2}]^3}\times100\%
=\frac{(8/3)\pi r^3}{(16/3)^{3/2}r^3}\times100\%\\
&=\frac{(8/3)\pi}{(16/3)^{3/2}}\times100\%
=\frac{8.378}{12.32}\times100\%=68.02\%.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
R_2&=\frac{V_{a2}}{V_2}\times100\%
=\frac{4V_a}{V_2}\times100\%\\
&=\frac{4(4/3)\pi r^3}{[(16r^2/2)^{1/2}]^3}\times100\%
=\frac{(16/3)\pi r^3}{8^{3/2}r^3}\times100\%\\
&=\frac{(16/3)\pi}{8^{3/2}}\times100\%
=\frac{16.76}{22.63}\times100\%=74.05\%.
\end{aligned}
$$

7′. $\rho_{\mathrm{fcc}}/\rho_{\mathrm{bcc}}=74.05\%/68.02\%=1.089$.

8′. $\rho_{\mathrm{fcc}}=1.089\rho_{\mathrm{bcc}}=1.089\times7.874=8.572\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$.

### 5.3–5.4


**Expected steps**

1. From martensite's mass-percent composition calculate relative moles of C and Fe.
2. Bring the C/Fe molar ratio to one unit cell (two Fe atoms).
3. Find the smallest whole number of C atoms for the smallest whole number of unit cells (facultative).
4. Calculate iron mass per cell.
5. Calculate carbon mass per cell.
6. Calculate total C and Fe mass in one cell.
7. Calculate martensite density from total C and Fe mass and bcc volume $V_1$.

**Details**

1. In 100.0 g of 4.3% C martensite:

$$
\frac{4.3\ \mathrm{g\ C}}{12.011\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}}=0.36\ \mathrm{mol\ C},\quad
\frac{95.7\ \mathrm{g\ Fe}}{55.847\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}}=1.71\ \mathrm{mol\ Fe}.
$$

There is one carbon atom for 4.8 iron atoms, or 0.21 carbon atoms per iron atom.

2. Martensite is bcc with two iron atoms per cell:

$$
\frac{1\ \mathrm{C\ atom}}{4.8\ \mathrm{Fe\ atoms}}\,
\frac{2\ \mathrm{Fe\ atoms}}{\mathrm{unit\ cell}}
=0.42\ \mathrm{C\ atoms/unit\ cell}.
$$

3. Five carbon atoms $[(0.42\ \mathrm{C\ atom}/0.42)\times5]$ in 12 cells $[(1\ \mathrm{unit\ cell}/0.42)\times5]$: five carbon atoms dispersed in 12 unit cells.

4. Iron mass per $\alpha$-iron cell:

$$
\frac{55.847\ \mathrm{g/mol}}{6.02214\times10^{23}\ \mathrm{atoms/mol}}\,
\frac{2\ \mathrm{atoms}}{\mathrm{unit\ cell}}
=1.8547\times10^{-22}\ \mathrm{g\ Fe/unit\ cell}.
$$

5. Carbon mass per atom is $12.011/(6.02214\times10^{23})=1.9945\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm{g\ C/atom}$.

6. Total cell mass:

$$
1.8547\times10^{-22}\ \mathrm{g\ Fe}
+(0.42\ \mathrm{C\ atoms})(1.9945\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm{g/C\ atom})
=1.938\times10^{-22}\ \mathrm{g\ C\ and\ Fe}.
$$

7. Each cell occupies $V_1=2.356\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm{cm^3}$ (“cf. Question i”):

$$
\rho(\text{martensite at }4.3\%\mathrm{\ C})
=\frac{1.938\times10^{-22}\ \mathrm{g\ C\ and\ Fe}}
{2.356\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm{cm^3}}
=8.228\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}.
$$
