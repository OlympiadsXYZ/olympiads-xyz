This publication contains the competition problems (Volume 2) from the 21st – 40th  
International Chemistry Olympiads (IChO) organized in the years 1989 – 2008  and is a
continuation of the publication that appeared last year as Volume 1 and contained
competition problems from the first twenty IChOs.  The whole review of the competition
tasks set in the IChO in its fourty-year history is a contribution of the IChO International  
Information Centre in Bratislava (Slovakia) to the development of this world known
international competition. This Volume 2 contains 154 theoretical and 46 practical
competition problems from the mentioned years. The review as a whole presents
altogether 279 theoretical and 96 practical problems.  
In the elaboration of this collection the editor had to face certain difficulties because
the aim was not only to make use of past recordings but also to give them such a form
that they may be used in practice and further chemical education. Consequently, it was
necessary to make some corrections in order to unify the form of the problems. However,
they did not concern the contents and language of the problems.  
Unfortunately, the authors of the particular competition problems are not known and
due to the procedure of the creation of the IChO competition problems, it is impossible to
assign any author's name to a particular problem. As the editor I would appreciate many  
times some discussion with the authors about any critical places that occurred in the text.  
On the other hand, any additional amendments to the text would be not correct from the  
historical point of view. Therefore, responsibility for the scientific content and language of
the problems lies exclusively with the organizers of the particular International Chemistry  
Olympiads.    

Some parts of texts, especially those gained as scanned materials, could not be
used directly and thus, several texts, schemes and pictures had to be re-written or created
again. Some solutions were often available in a brief form and necessary extent only, just
for the needs of members of the International Jury.  

Recalculations of the solutions were made in some special cases only when the
numeric results in the original solutions showed to be obviously not correct. Although the
numbers of significant figures in the results of several solutions do not obey the criteria
generally accepted, they were left without change.      



In this publication SI quantities and units are used and a more modern method of
chemical calculations is introduced. Only some exceptions have been made when, in an
effort to preserve the original text, the quantities and units have been used that are not SI.  
There were some problems with the presentation of the solutions of practical tasks,
because most of the relatively simple calculations were based on the experimental results
of contestants. Moreover, the practical problems are accompanied with answer sheets in
the last years and several additional questions and tasks have appeared in them that were
not a part of the text of the original experimental problems. Naturally, answer sheets could
not be included in this publication and can only be preserved as archive materials.
  
When reading the texts of the IChO problems one must admire and appreciate the
work of those many known and unknown people – teachers, authors, pupils, and
organizers – who contributed so much to development and success of this important
international competition.

I am sure about the usefulness of the this review of the IChO problems. It may serve
not only as archive material but, in particular, this review should serve to both competitors
and their teachers as a source of further inspiration in their preparation for this challenging
competition.




Bratislava, July 2009

Much of the world's platinum-group metal supply is derived from residues recovered from electrolytic refining of copper and nickel. A recovery flow chart for platinum and palladium follows.

### 6.2


Reaction of a main-group chloride (24.71 g) with ammonia (10.90 g) gave $\mathrm{NH_4Cl}$ (25.68 g), a solid element **A** (2.57 g), and a yellow crystalline nitride of **A** (7.37 g), according to

$$
n\mathrm{A}_w\mathrm{Cl}_x+m\mathrm{NH_3}
\longrightarrow p\mathrm{NH_4Cl}+q\mathrm{A}+r\mathrm{A}_y\mathrm{N}_z.
$$

The coefficients $n,m,p,q,r,w,x,y,z$ are to be determined. A nitride sample exploded violently when struck with a hammer but underwent controlled polymerization on heating to a bronze-coloured fibrous solid exhibiting metallic conductivity. **A** also polymerizes to a high-molecular-weight linear polymer on heating.

Molar masses: $M(\mathrm{Cl})=35.453$, $M(\mathrm{N})=14.007$, $M(\mathrm{H})=1.008\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$.



**i**  
Identify element **A**.



**ii**  
Write a complete balanced equation for the reaction of the chloride with ammonia.



**iii**  
Assuming conventional oxidation states, write the balanced redox half-reaction equations involved.



</ProblemSection>

### 6.1


### i

$\mathrm{PtCl_6^{2-}}$ consists of Pt(IV) centred in a regular octahedron of Cl⁻ ions. The printed prose describes $\mathrm{PdCl_4^{2-}}$ as “Pt(II)” centred in a square of Cl⁻ ions; the native diagram labels its centre Pd.

</figure>


### ii

Like $\mathrm{PdCl_4^{2-}}$, $\mathrm{Pd(NH_3)_2Cl_2}$ is square planar. There are two arrangements of the two ligand types. Placing both Cl⁻ ions on adjacent corners (and both NH₃ on the other adjacent corners) gives the *cis* isomer. Placing like ligand pairs on diagonally opposite corners gives the *trans* isomer. Only these two stereoisomers are possible for the monomeric complex.

<figure className="problem-figure problem-figure--sized" style={{'--fig-w': '44.2%', '--fig-max': '284px'}}>
<img src="https://pub-43290baaaff14857b5dd59610ea438c7.r2.dev/problems/icho-1997-theory-volume2-tasks-3-8/p6-sol-fig2-sol61-v2-read233-287-e7297ee64f0b5b1c3a07c9d1f25e7595ad3df43778af6109c7543188ee41ff3a.png" alt="Native cis and trans structures" width="886" height="355" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
</figure>


### iii

$\mathrm{FeSO_4}$, i.e. Fe(II), is a reducing agent. Under the process conditions it reduces Au(III) to Au(0), but is not strong enough to reduce Pd(II) or Pt(IV).

$$
\mathrm{HAuCl_4+3FeSO_4\longrightarrow Au^0+HCl+FeCl_3+Fe_2(SO_4)_3}.
$$

### iv

The key prints three alternative ignition equations and their half-reactions:

$$
\mathrm{Pd(NH_3)_2Cl_2+O_2\longrightarrow Pd^0+N_2+2H_2O+2HCl}.
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{Pd^{2+}+2e^-}&\longrightarrow\mathrm{Pd^0}&&\text{reduction},\\
\mathrm{2O^0+4e^-}&\longrightarrow\mathrm{2O^{2-}}&&\text{reduction},\\
\mathrm{2N^{3-}-6e^-}&\longrightarrow\mathrm{2N^0}&&\text{oxidation}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Or:

$$
\mathrm{Pd(NH_3)_2Cl_2+2O_2\longrightarrow Pd^0+2NO+2H_2O+2HCl},
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{Pd^{2+}+2e^-}&\longrightarrow\mathrm{Pd^0}&&\text{reduction},\\
\mathrm{4O^0+8e^-}&\longrightarrow\mathrm{4O^{2-}}&&\text{reduction},\\
\mathrm{2N^{3-}-10e^-}&\longrightarrow\mathrm{2N^{2+}}&&\text{oxidation}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Or:

$$
\mathrm{Pd(NH_3)_2Cl_2+3O_2\longrightarrow Pd^0+2NO_2+2H_2O+2HCl},
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{Pd^{2+}+2e^-}&\longrightarrow\mathrm{Pd^0}&&\text{reduction},\\
\mathrm{6O^0+12e^-}&\longrightarrow\mathrm{6O^{2-}}&&\text{reduction},\\
\mathrm{2N^{3-}-14e^-}&\longrightarrow\mathrm{2N^{4+}}&&\text{oxidation}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Ammonia is oxidized to water and dinitrogen (or nitrogen oxides); Pd(II) and dioxygen are reduced. Part of the oxidation supplies two electrons to convert Pd(II) to Pd(0), and part reduces dioxygen to water. Noble-metal catalysts can oxidize NH₃ to $(\mathrm{NO})_x$, so other nitrogen species are possible in principle.

### 6.2


### i

**A = sulphur.**

All chlorine is in NH₄Cl. Since 53.492 g NH₄Cl contains 35.453 g Cl, 25.68 g NH₄Cl contains

$$
25.68\times35.453/53.492=17.02\ \mathrm{g\ Cl}.
$$

The printed total amount of A is “24.71 g Cl − 17.02 g Cl = 7.69 g A”. There are 2.57 g free A and $7.69-2.57=5.12$ g A in the nitride. Nitride nitrogen is $7.37-5.12=2.25$ g. The key also prints “nitrogen bound in NH₄Cl” as $25.68-17.02=8.66$ g.

Apply the rule of multiple proportions.

**Proportions 1:1**

Chloride: 7.69 g A binds 17.02 g Cl. The amount binding 35.453 g Cl is

$$
35.453\times7.69/17.02=16.02\ \mathrm{g}.
$$

A could be oxygen, but it is a gas and is excluded.

Nitride: 5.12 g A binds 2.25 g N. The amount binding 14.007 g N is

$$
14.007\times5.12/2.25=31.87\ \mathrm{g}.
$$

Sulphur fits the physical description and $M_r(\mathrm{S})=32.064$. The key's footnote states that phosphorus, $M_r(\mathrm{P})=30.97$, is also possible but must be excluded because the highest known degree of “polymerization” is four, in P₄.

**Proportions 1:2**

Chloride: 7.69 g A binds 17.02 g Cl. The amount binding $2\times35.453$ g Cl is

$$
2\times35.453\times7.69/17.02=32.03\ \mathrm{g}.
$$

Again sulphur is possible.

Nitride: 5.12 g A binds 2.25 g N. The amount binding $2\times14.007$ g N is

$$
2\times14.007\times5.12/2.25=63.75\ \mathrm{g}.
$$

Cu is possible numerically but is a Group B element and is excluded. Therefore A is sulphur.

### ii

$$
\mathrm{3SCl_2+8NH_3\longrightarrow6NH_4Cl+S+S_2N_2},
$$

or

$$
\mathrm{6SCl_2+16NH_3\longrightarrow12NH_4Cl+2S+S_4N_4}.
$$

The native key says elemental S is S₈ and therefore the equation should be multiplied by eight. Although S₂N₂ roughly fits the colour and explosive instability, S₄N₄ is actually produced. Carefully controlled heating polymerizes both rings to the high-molecular-weight linear polymer $(\mathrm{SN})_n$, one of the rare examples of a metal containing only lighter p-group elements.

Polymerization is favoured by relatively weak S–N bonds and large ring strain. The weak S–S bond is also broken by heating, so S₈ polymerizes at high temperature. S₈ has hardly any ring-strain energy, so its polymerization is not highly exothermic or explosive. Other sulphur chlorides, S₂Cl₂ and SCl₄, do not fit the reaction stoichiometry.

### iii

Sulphur disproportionation:

$$
\mathrm{2S^{2+}-2e^-\longrightarrow2S^{3+}},\qquad
\mathrm{S^{2+}+2e^-\longrightarrow S^0}.
$$
