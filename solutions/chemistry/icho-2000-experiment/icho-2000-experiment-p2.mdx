This publication contains the competition problems (Volume 2) from the 21st – 40th  
International Chemistry Olympiads (IChO) organized in the years 1989 – 2008  and is a
continuation of the publication that appeared last year as Volume 1 and contained
competition problems from the first twenty IChOs.  The whole review of the competition
tasks set in the IChO in its fourty-year history is a contribution of the IChO International  
Information Centre in Bratislava (Slovakia) to the development of this world known
international competition. This Volume 2 contains 154 theoretical and 46 practical
competition problems from the mentioned years. The review as a whole presents
altogether 279 theoretical and 96 practical problems.  
In the elaboration of this collection the editor had to face certain difficulties because
the aim was not only to make use of past recordings but also to give them such a form
that they may be used in practice and further chemical education. Consequently, it was
necessary to make some corrections in order to unify the form of the problems. However,
they did not concern the contents and language of the problems.  
Unfortunately, the authors of the particular competition problems are not known and
due to the procedure of the creation of the IChO competition problems, it is impossible to
assign any author's name to a particular problem. As the editor I would appreciate many  
times some discussion with the authors about any critical places that occurred in the text.  
On the other hand, any additional amendments to the text would be not correct from the  
historical point of view. Therefore, responsibility for the scientific content and language of
the problems lies exclusively with the organizers of the particular International Chemistry  
Olympiads.    

Some parts of texts, especially those gained as scanned materials, could not be
used directly and thus, several texts, schemes and pictures had to be re-written or created
again. Some solutions were often available in a brief form and necessary extent only, just
for the needs of members of the International Jury.  

Recalculations of the solutions were made in some special cases only when the
numeric results in the original solutions showed to be obviously not correct. Although the
numbers of significant figures in the results of several solutions do not obey the criteria
generally accepted, they were left without change.      



In this publication SI quantities and units are used and a more modern method of
chemical calculations is introduced. Only some exceptions have been made when, in an
effort to preserve the original text, the quantities and units have been used that are not SI.  
There were some problems with the presentation of the solutions of practical tasks,
because most of the relatively simple calculations were based on the experimental results
of contestants. Moreover, the practical problems are accompanied with answer sheets in
the last years and several additional questions and tasks have appeared in them that were
not a part of the text of the original experimental problems. Naturally, answer sheets could
not be included in this publication and can only be preserved as archive materials.
  
When reading the texts of the IChO problems one must admire and appreciate the
work of those many known and unknown people – teachers, authors, pupils, and
organizers – who contributed so much to development and success of this important
international competition.

I am sure about the usefulness of the this review of the IChO problems. It may serve
not only as archive material but, in particular, this review should serve to both competitors
and their teachers as a source of further inspiration in their preparation for this challenging
competition.

Peptide synthesis reacts one amino acid with another to form an amide bond. To prevent self-reaction and ensure a single product, mask the first amino acid's amino group and the second's carboxyl group. The following procedure masks amino-acid carboxylic groups before peptide formation.

The native safety instructions specify a ventilated hood because thionyl chloride is an irritant and irritating gases evolve. Thionyl chloride is a corrosive acid chloride: avoid skin and eye contact, and immediately flush splashes with water. Larger amounts react violently with water.

### Procedure


Quickly transfer $2.0\,\mathrm{cm^3}$ absolute methanol to a dry test tube and close with aluminium foil. Use the foil lid throughout subsequent manipulations to protect from atmospheric moisture. Cool in an ice bath for 1–2 min. Draw $0.52\,\mathrm{cm^3}$ thionyl chloride into a $1\,\mathrm{cm^3}$ graduated syringe with polyethylene tube tip as in Procedure B. Cautiously add it to the methanol over approximately 5 min, following the precautions above.

Keep at $0\,{}^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ for approximately 2 min. Add the supplied weighed sample of (S)-serine (0.210 g). Keep at room temperature for approximately 2 min, then gently heat to boiling in a sand bath for 10 min. All material should dissolve.

Cool in an ice bath for approximately 2 min. Add $10\,\mathrm{cm^3}$ dry tert.-butyl methyl ether. Scratch the tube wall at the solution's surface with a glass spatula for about 1 min. Leave in the ice bath for another 5–15 min to crystallize. Isolate crystals by Procedure A, collecting filtrate in a $100\,\mathrm{cm^3}$ beaker.

Wash the crystals twice on the filter, each time with $1\,\mathrm{cm^3}$ tert.-butyl methyl ether. Finally press the cake with the piston and pre-dry by pumping air through it with the piston. Collect the solid on filter paper to absorb residual solvent. Once dry, place the residue in the tared plastic lidded Eppendorf sample tube supplied in the box, close and weigh.



</ProblemSection>

### PROCEDURE A: Filtration procedures


Modified syringes are used for filtration in the laboratory tasks. Task 1 uses a $60\,\mathrm{cm^3}$ syringe with a disc of porous polypropylene; task 2 uses a $10\,\mathrm{cm^3}$ syringe with a disc of filtration paper. Figure 1 sketches the procedure.

</figure>


Procedure:

1. Fill the syringe from above with the suspension to be filtered, up to the hole's level. Replace the piston.
2. Close the hole and press the piston for filtration.
3. Stop before passing the hole.
4. Open the hole and draw the piston back.
5. Repeat steps 2–4 a couple of times.
6. Remove the piston and place filter paper on top of the filter cake.
7. Press the piston against the filter cake.
8. Push the filter cake out with a straightened-out paper clip.

**Filtration procedure for practical problem 1.** The provided filter syringe is made from a standard $60\,\mathrm{cm^3}$ medical polypropylene syringe, with its piston temporarily removed and a 3 mm hole drilled at the $35\,\mathrm{cm^3}$ mark. Use a plastic spatula to press a tightly fitting porous-polypropylene disc down to the base. Apply the mixture without inserting the piston. Move drops downwards by tapping the syringe against a solid surface.

Place the piston in the syringe and press gently while closing the hole with a finger, to promote passage of solvent through the filter. Just above the hole, remove the finger and draw the piston back to the top. Repeat a couple of times until the cake looks dry. Close the drilled hole when moving the piston down; open it when moving up. Wash the cake and press out washing solution using similar cycles. Wick remaining outlet solvent with a small piece of tissue paper. Remove the solid and collect it on weighing paper for drying.

**Filtration procedure for practical problem 2.** The provided filter syringe is made from a standard $10\,\mathrm{cm^3}$ medical polypropylene syringe, with piston temporarily removed and a 3 mm hole drilled at the $5.5\,\mathrm{cm^3}$ mark. Press snugly fitting filter paper to the bottom using the piston. Filter and wash as for task 1. Before removing the cake, withdraw the piston, then press a second fitting piece of filter paper onto the cake using the piston. Press the cake with the piston. Slowly draw the piston back until reaching the hole, and then out of the syringe, leaving the cake between two papers. Wick remaining outlet solvent with tissue paper.

Cautiously push the cake out with a straightened-out metal paper clip through the outlet. Remove the solid, preferably as a coherent plug. Using a small metal spatula, collect the residue on filter paper for drying. Fix filtration paper with the paper-clip tip while removing adhering solid with the spatula.



</ProblemSection>

### PROCEDURE B: Measuring volumes of liquids using a syringe


<figure className="problem-figure problem-figure--sized" style={{'--fig-w': '65.7%', '--fig-max': '421px'}}>
<img src="https://pub-43290baaaff14857b5dd59610ea438c7.r2.dev/problems/icho-2000-experiment/p2-fig3-sol61-v2-read343-397-167d4d5704994c3693f94664fc80d546ddd17428f274da1b4fd5c4cdfd34632f.png" alt="Native Figure 2: measuring liquid volumes with a syringe" width="1315" height="713" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />

1. Suck a slight excess of liquid into the syringe.
2. Turn the syringe upside down; keep the tube tip in the storage “nottle” (native spelling). Air accumulates at the top of the syringe.
3. Press the piston to remove air. Press further until the desired liquid volume remains, keeping the tip in the storage bottle.
4. Turn the syringe, put the tube tip in the receiver flask, and press until the desired liquid volume has left the syringe.



</ProblemSection>
